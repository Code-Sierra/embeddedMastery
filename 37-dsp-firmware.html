01

Discrete-time signals: what firmware actually sees

BEGINNER▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

A real-world signal, such as a battery voltage or a microphone pressure, changes continuously. A microcontroller cannot store a continuous curve. It can only store numbers. So the ADC measures the signal at regular moments, and firmware sees a list of numbers, one per measurement. That list is called a discrete-time signal, and DSP (Digital Signal Processing) is the maths of processing such lists.

ADC = Analog-to-Digital Converter: the peripheral that turns a voltage into an integer code.
Sample = one measurement, one number in the list.
x[n] = the n-th sample. Square brackets mean "sample index", so x[0] is the first sample and x[n−1] is the one just before x[n].
fs = sampling frequency in Hz (samples per second). Ts = 1/fs = sampling period, the time between two samples.
Normalised frequency = a frequency expressed relative to fs (cycles per sample) instead of in Hz.

2. STEP BY STEP: FROM WIRE TO ARRAY

  1. A timer (for example TIM2 on the STM32F407) overflows every Ts. At fs = 1 kHz that is every 1 ms.
  2. The timer's trigger output starts an ADC conversion. The ADC holds the input voltage steady (sample-and-hold) and converts it to a 12-bit code from 0 to 4095.
  3. DMA (Direct Memory Access, a hardware copier) moves the code into a RAM buffer without the CPU.
  4. Firmware reads the buffer. To the code, the signal is now just uint16_t x[256]. The time between elements is implied by fs. It is not stored anywhere.
  5. Every filter, FFT or detector on this page works on that array. Its coefficients only know "samples", never "seconds".
The dashed curve exists only in the analogue world. Firmware only ever has the cyan dots.

3. WHY FREQUENCY BECOMES "CYCLES PER SAMPLE"

Because the array carries no time stamps, a filter only "sees" how many samples one cycle of a sine takes. A 10 Hz sine sampled at 1 kHz takes 100 samples per cycle. A 100 Hz sine sampled at 10 kHz also takes 100 samples per cycle. To the filter, those two arrays are identical. So frequency is measured as f / fs.

…you mix up MATLAB's normalisation. butter(2, 0.1) means 0.1 × Nyquist, not 0.1 × fs. Your cutoff comes out a factor of 2 wrong.

4. WHY DO THIS IN SOFTWARE AT ALL?

Digital filters do not drift with temperature or component tolerance. They can be changed by a firmware update, and they can have perfectly linear phase. They also make things possible that are impractical in analogue circuits, such as a notch that tracks the mains frequency, or 100 dB of stopband rejection. The one thing they cannot do is remove aliasing. Once an out-of-band component has folded into the band during sampling, it looks exactly like real signal (card 02). That is why the anti-alias filter in front of the ADC always stays analogue.

A discrete-time signal is a sequence of samples x[n] taken every Ts = 1/fs. A digital filter only knows samples, so its cutoff is fixed in cycles per sample, f/fs. If I change the sample rate without recomputing the coefficients, every cutoff moves by the same ratio. And digital filtering can't undo aliasing, so the anti-alias filter has to stay in analogue before the ADC.
02

Sampling and aliasing: the recap you need for DSP

BEGINNER▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

Aliasing is when a frequency that is too high for the sample rate pretends to be a lower frequency. Picture filming a spinning wheel: at the wrong camera frame rate it appears to spin slowly, or even backwards. The camera is sampling too slowly to see the real motion. An ADC does the same thing to fast signals. Full treatment, with an interactive aliasing lab: page 09.

Nyquist frequency = fs/2, the highest frequency a sample rate can represent without ambiguity.
Nyquist criterion = fs must be greater than 2 × the highest frequency present at the ADC input, including noise.
Alias = the false lower frequency that an out-of-band component turns into.
Anti-alias filter (AAF) = an analogue low-pass filter before the ADC that removes content above Nyquist.
Decimation = reducing the sample rate in software by keeping only every R-th sample (card 24).

2. HOW TO CALCULATE THE ALIAS, STEP BY STEP

  1. Take the input frequency f and the sample rate fs.
  2. Find the integer k that makes f − k·fs closest to zero (k = round(f / fs)).
  3. The alias is falias = | f − k·fs |. It always lands between 0 and fs/2.
  4. If falias lands in your band of interest, no digital filter can remove it. The fix must happen before sampling.
falias = | f − round(f/fs)·fs |
Accelerometer sampled at f_s = 1000 Hz. Motor bearing vibration at 980 Hz. k = round(980 / 1000) = 1 f_alias = |980 − 1 × 1000| = 20 Hz The FFT shows a clean 20 Hz tone. Nothing in the data says it is fake. Same sensor, 2730 Hz gear mesh: k = round(2.73) = 3 → |2730 − 3000| = 270 Hz
The spectrum folds like a paper strip at every multiple of fs/2. Everything lands somewhere in 0 … fs/2.

3. THE RULES THAT MATTER FOR FIRMWARE DSP

  1. Nyquist is about what is present, not what you care about. If you only care about 0–50 Hz but the input contains 980 Hz vibration, the 980 Hz still aliases.
  2. Decimating in software is sampling again. Keeping every 2nd sample halves fs. You must low-pass filter to the new Nyquist before you throw samples away.
  3. Oversample, filter, decimate is the modern pattern. Sample fast with a cheap 1st-order RC filter in front, then let a sharp digital FIR remove everything above the final band before dropping the rate. A sigma-delta ADC does exactly this internally.
✗ WRONG: decimate by just skipping samples
✓ RIGHT: low-pass to the new Nyquist first

…the anti-alias filter is missing or too weak. Symptom: a stable, real-looking low-frequency tone in your data that does not exist physically. It moves when you change fs (a real tone would not). The quickest diagnosis is to change fs by 10 % and see whether the tone moves.

"Sample at 2× the signal frequency." At exactly 2× a sine can be sampled at its zero crossings and disappear completely. In practice you need 2.5–10× plus an anti-alias filter whose attenuation at fs/2 exceeds your dynamic range.
Any content above fs/2 at the ADC input folds back to |f − k·fs|, so 980 Hz at 1 kHz sampling looks like 20 Hz. Once it's sampled, it is indistinguishable from real signal, so the fix is an analogue anti-alias filter or a higher sample rate. The same rule applies when I decimate in software: low-pass first, then drop samples.
03

LTI systems: why "linear" and "time-invariant" matter

BEGINNER▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

An LTI system (Linear Time-Invariant) is a processing block that behaves the same way no matter how loud the input is or when it arrives. Almost every filter on this page is LTI, and that is what lets us describe it with a single curve, the frequency response. If something isn't LTI, those tools no longer apply.

Linear = superposition holds. Double the input and the output doubles. Feed in two signals added together, and the output is the sum of the two separate outputs: T{a·x1 + b·x2} = a·T{x1} + b·T{x2}.
Time-invariant = delaying the input by k samples just delays the output by k samples. The filter behaves the same today and tomorrow.
Causal = the output at time n uses only the current and past inputs, never future ones. Every real-time firmware filter must be causal.
Eigenfunction = an input that comes out with the same shape, only scaled. For LTI systems, sinusoids are eigenfunctions.

2. WHAT LTI BUYS YOU, STEP BY STEP

  1. Because the system is linear, any input can be split into simple pieces, either impulses or sines, and each piece processed on its own.
  2. Because it is time-invariant, each delayed piece produces the same response, just delayed.
  3. So one measurement describes the system completely: the impulse response h[n] (card 04). In the frequency domain, the equivalent is the frequency response H(f).
  4. A sine of frequency f goes in, and a sine of the same f comes out, scaled by |H(f)| and shifted by the phase ∠H(f). An LTI filter can never create a new frequency.
Test for linearity: a "filter" y[n] = x[n]² (squaring, used in RMS calculation) input 1 → output 1; input 2 → output 4 (should be 2 if linear) ✗ NOT linear input sin(2π·50t) → sin² = ½ − ½·cos(2π·100t) → a NEW frequency (100 Hz) plus DC appeared. Only non-linear blocks do that. Test for time-invariance: decimator y[n] = x[2n] x = [1,2,3,4,5,6] → y = [1,3,5] delay x by 1: [0,1,2,3,4,5] → y = [0,2,4] (not just y delayed) ✗ NOT time-invariant

A linear filter can't do that, so something non-linear happened: the ADC or DAC clipped, an int16 overflowed and wrapped, or an intermediate saturated. That observation alone narrows the search enormously, and it is a great debugging point to make in an interview.

A system is LTI if superposition holds and a delayed input gives an equally delayed output. That means the impulse response, or equivalently the frequency response, describes it completely, and a sine in gives a sine of the same frequency out. So if I see a new frequency at the output, I know something non-linear happened, like clipping or overflow. Medians, saturation and decimators are the common non-LTI blocks.
04

Impulse response and convolution

BEGINNER▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

The impulse response is what a filter outputs when you give it a single "kick": one sample of value 1 and then nothing but zeros. Convolution is the recipe for building the output to any input from that kick response. Because the filter is LTI, every input sample just launches its own scaled copy of the kick response, and the output is all those copies added together.

δ[n] (delta, unit impulse) = 1 at n = 0 and 0 everywhere else.
h[n] = the impulse response, the output when the input is δ[n]. For an FIR filter these numbers are exactly the filter coefficients (also called taps).
MAC = Multiply-ACcumulate, acc += a × b. One MAC per tap per output sample is the basic cost of FIR filtering.
FIR / IIR = Finite / Infinite Impulse Response. An FIR's kick response ends after N samples. An IIR uses feedback, so its kick response decays forever.
Transient = the start-up period while the filter's memory is still filling with real data.
y[n] = Σk=0..N−1 h[k] · x[n − k]    (convolution, written y = h * x)

2. STEP BY STEP: COMPUTING ONE OUTPUT SAMPLE

  1. Take the newest input x[n] and multiply it by h[0].
  2. Take the previous input x[n−1] and multiply it by h[1]. Add the result to the accumulator.
  3. Keep going back in time: x[n−k] × h[k], until k = N−1.
  4. The accumulator is y[n]. Shift everything by one sample and repeat for y[n+1].
h = [0.25, 0.5, 0.25] (a 3-tap smoother, Σh = 1) x = [0, 0, 4, 4, 4, 0, 0] y[2] = 0.25·x[2] + 0.5·x[1] + 0.25·x[0] = 0.25·4 + 0 + 0 = 1 y[3] = 0.25·4 + 0.5·4 + 0.25·0 = 3 y[4] = 0.25·4 + 0.5·4 + 0.25·4 = 4 ← fully settled y[5] = 0.25·0 + 0.5·4 + 0.25·4 = 3 y[6] = 0.25·0 + 0.5·0 + 0.25·4 = 1 Output length = len(x) + len(h) − 1. The step 0→4 became a soft ramp 1, 3, 4.

3. CODE

✗ WRONG: index runs forward in time
✓ RIGHT: h[k] pairs with x[n−k]
  • Σh = 1 means the DC gain is 1: a constant input comes out unchanged once the filter has filled. Always check Σh when you design a smoothing filter. If Σh = 0.98, your battery reading is 2 % low.
  • Convolution in time = multiplication in frequency. This one fact explains why windowing smears an FFT (card 20) and why fast convolution with FFTs works (card 23).

…you forget the transient. The first N−1 outputs are computed with zeros (or garbage) in the history, so they are wrong. Symptom: the battery reading starts at 0 V and ramps up at power-on, and a "low battery" check fires at boot. Fix: pre-fill the history with the first sample, or ignore the first N outputs.

The impulse response h[n] is the filter's output for a single unit kick, and because the filter is LTI, any output is the sum of shifted, scaled copies of it: y[n] = Σ h[k]·x[n−k]. For an FIR, h is just the coefficient list, so each output costs N multiply-accumulates. Convolution in time equals multiplication in frequency, which is why windows smear spectra and why long filters can be run with FFTs.
05

Moving average: the most used (and misused) filter

BEGINNER▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

A moving average (MA) outputs the average of the last N samples. Each new sample pushes out the oldest one, like a window sliding along the data. It is the simplest smoothing filter there is. It is excellent at reducing random noise and very poor at separating one frequency from another.

N = window length (number of samples averaged). It is an FIR filter with N equal taps of 1/N.
Running sum = a variable holding the sum of the window, updated by adding the newest and subtracting the oldest sample, so the cost does not grow with N.
Null = a frequency the filter blocks completely (gain exactly 0).
Group delay = how many samples late the output is compared to the input. For an MA it is (N−1)/2.
σ (sigma) = standard deviation of the noise, its typical size.
y[n] = (1/N) · Σk=0..N−1 x[n−k]     |H(f)| = | sin(πfN/fs) / (N·sin(πf/fs)) |

2. STEP BY STEP: THE O(1) RUNNING-SUM VERSION

  1. Keep a circular buffer of the last N samples and a running sum of those samples.
  2. A new sample arrives. Add it to sum.
  3. Subtract the oldest sample (the one in the buffer slot about to be overwritten) from sum.
  4. Store the new sample in that slot and advance the index, wrapping at N.
  5. Output = sum / N. If N is a power of 2, the divide becomes a right shift. Add N/2 first to round instead of truncate.
N = 4, buffer holds [10, 12, 11, 13], sum = 46, output = 46/4 = 11.5 New sample 20 arrives; oldest is 10: sum = 46 + 20 − 10 = 56 buffer = [20, 12, 11, 13] output = 56 / 4 = 14 Cost: 1 add + 1 subtract + 1 shift, whether N is 4 or 4096. Properties at f_s = 1 kHz, N = 20 (window = 20 ms): nulls at k·f_s/N = 50, 100, 150 Hz … ← exactly mains and its harmonics −3 dB ≈ 0.443·f_s/N = 22 Hz delay = (N−1)/2 = 9.5 samples = 9.5 ms white-noise σ reduced by √N = √20 ≈ 4.5× first sidelobe only −13.3 dB (at ≈1.43·f_s/N = 71 Hz)
|H(f)| of a moving average (drawn live from the formula). Excellent nulls, weak stopband in between.

A moving average is an N-tap FIR with equal weights. I implement it with a running sum, adding the newest sample and subtracting the oldest, so it's O(1) per sample, with integers so it never drifts and power-of-2 N so the divide is a shift. It cuts white noise by √N and has exact nulls at multiples of fs/N, which is why a 20 ms window rejects 50 Hz mains. But its sidelobes are only −13 dB, so it's a poor frequency-selective filter, and it delays the signal by (N−1)/2 samples.

EMA, the one-line IIR: y += α(x − y)

BEGINNER▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

The exponential moving average (EMA) moves the output a fixed fraction α of the way toward each new input. If the output is 10 and a new sample says 20, with α = 0.1 the output moves 10 % of the gap, to 11. It needs one variable and one multiply, with no buffer at all. That is why it is the most common filter in embedded code: battery voltage, temperature, current offsets and ADC smoothing.

α (alpha) = the smoothing factor, between 0 and 1. Small α = heavy smoothing and slow response. α = 1 = no filtering.
IIR = Infinite Impulse Response. The output feeds back into the next calculation, so one kick decays forever (geometrically, by (1−α) each sample).
Pole = the feedback factor. Here it is 1−α. A pole close to 1 means long memory (card 09).
Cutoff fc = the frequency where the output power has dropped to half (−3 dB, amplitude × 0.707).
Time constant τ = the time for the output to cover 63 % of a step.
Dead band = a range of input changes the filter ignores because integer rounding makes the update zero.
y[n] = y[n−1] + α·(x[n] − y[n−1]) = α·x[n] + (1−α)·y[n−1]    H(z) = α / (1 − (1−α)z−1)

2. STEP BY STEP: WHAT ONE UPDATE DOES

  1. Compute the error: how far the new sample is from the current estimate, e = x − y.
  2. Scale it: α·e. This is the correction.
  3. Apply it: y = y + α·e. The output closes a fraction α of the gap.
  4. After k samples of a step input, the remaining gap is (1−α)k. That is why the decay is exponential.

3. α ↔ CUTOFF FREQUENCY (THE FORMULA INTERVIEWERS ASK FOR)

exact: α = cos ωc − 1 + √(cos²ωc − 4·cos ωc + 3),   ωc = 2π·fc/fs
approx (fc ≪ fs): α ≈ 1 − e−2π·fc/fs ≈ 2π·fc/fs
τ = −Ts / ln(1−α) ≈ Ts/α   99 % settling ≈ 4.6 τ

Where the exact formula comes from: the gain squared is |H|² = α² / (1 − 2p·cos ω + p²) with p = 1−α. Set it equal to ½ (half power), which gives p² − (4 − 2cos ω)·p + 1 = 0. Take the root below 1 and α = 1 − p.

Filter specifications: passband, stopband, ripple, attenuation

BEGINNER▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

Before designing a filter you write down, with numbers, what it must let through, what it must block, and how sharp the edge between them must be. That list is the specification. "A low-pass at 100 Hz" is not a spec. "Pass 0–100 Hz within 0.1 dB, attenuate ≥ 60 dB above 150 Hz, delay under 10 ms" is a spec, and it decides the filter type and order for you.

dB (decibel) for amplitude = 20·log10(gain). −20 dB = ×0.1, −40 dB = ×0.01, −6 dB = ×0.5, −3 dB = ×0.708 (half power).
Passband = frequencies that must pass. Its edge is fp.
Passband ripple Ap = how much the gain may wobble inside the passband (e.g. ±0.1 dB ≈ ±1.2 %).
Stopband = frequencies that must be blocked. Its edge is fst.
Stopband attenuation As = the minimum rejection in the stopband, in dB.
Transition band = the gap between fp and fst, width Δf. The narrower it is, the more expensive the filter.
Order = the number of poles of an IIR filter, or the number of taps − 1 of an FIR.
The response (cyan) must stay inside the green box and below the red box. The amber gap is where it is free to roll off.

2. STEP BY STEP: TURNING A REQUIREMENT INTO A SPEC

  1. Passband edge = the highest frequency your signal really contains. For a battery voltage that might be 1 Hz. For a current loop it could be 2 kHz.
  2. Passband ripple = your accuracy budget. A 0.5 % measurement error budget allows ±0.04 dB.
  3. Stopband edge = the lowest frequency of the nearest thing you must reject: the nearest interferer, the PWM frequency, or the new fs/2 if you are about to decimate.
  4. Attenuation = how small the interferer must become. Rule: to bury it below an N-bit LSB you need about 6.02·N dB. A 12-bit system needs ≈ 74 dB.
  5. Delay budget = for control loops, the maximum group delay before phase margin suffers.
  6. Pick the family and order to satisfy all five (cards 11 and 17).
Requirement: f_s = 1 kHz. Pass up to 100 Hz. An interferer sits at 150 Hz with amplitude 0.5 V; the signal resolution needed is 1 mV. needed attenuation = 20·log10(0.5 V / 1 mV) = 20·log10(500) = 54 dB → spec A_s = 60 dB transition Δf = 150 − 100 = 50 Hz → Δf/f_s = 0.05 FIR (fred harris rule): taps ≈ A_s / (22 × Δf/f_s) = 60 / (22 × 0.05) = 55 taps IIR Butterworth: 60 dB over 100→150 Hz (0.18 decade) needs 20·N·0.18 ≥ 60 → N ≈ 17 (!) → Elliptic does it in ≈ 6th order; or relax the spec. This is why specs come first.

An FIR needs roughly As/(22·Δf/fs) taps, and an IIR gives 20 dB per decade per order. Then I pick the family that meets all of it, including the delay.

z-transform intuition: z−1 is a one-sample delay

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

The z-transform is bookkeeping that turns a filter's C code (a "difference equation") into a simple fraction of polynomials. That fraction then tells you the filter's gain at every frequency and whether it is stable. You don't need contour integrals for firmware work. You need to know that z−1 means "one sample ago".

Difference equation = the filter written as a formula over samples, e.g. y[n] = 0.5·x[n] + 0.5·x[n−1]. This is exactly what your C code computes.
z−1 = the delay operator. X(z)·z−1 is "x delayed by one sample", i.e. x[n−1].
H(z) = transfer function = Y(z)/X(z), output divided by input in the z domain.
B(z), A(z) = numerator (feed-forward, "b" coefficients) and denominator (feedback, "a" coefficients) polynomials.

Poles, zeros and stability

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

Factor the top and bottom polynomials of H(z) and you get a handful of special points in the complex plane. Zeros are where the filter's output is zero. Poles are where it would be infinite. Where these points sit relative to the unit circle tells you, at a glance, the shape of the frequency response and whether the filter is stable. The Filter Lab draws them live.

Zero = a root of B(z), the numerator. Near the unit circle it causes a dip; on the circle it causes an exact null.
Pole = a root of A(z), the denominator. Near the circle it causes a peak.
Stable = a bounded input always gives a bounded output (BIBO). For a causal filter: every pole strictly inside the unit circle, |p| < 1.
Pole radius r = distance of a pole from the origin. 1 − r is roughly how wide the resulting peak is.
Minimum phase = all zeros inside the circle. Gives the least delay for a given magnitude response.

2. STEP BY STEP: READING A POLE-ZERO PLOT

  1. Pick a frequency f. Find its point on the unit circle at angle ω = 2πf/fs.
  2. Measure the distance from that point to every zero and multiply them together.
  3. Measure the distance to every pole and multiply those.
  4. Gain = g × (zero distances) / (pole distances). Close to a zero → small gain. Close to a pole → large gain.
  5. Stability check: are all poles inside the circle? A pole on the circle gives a sustained oscillation. Outside it, the output grows exponentially.

FIR filters: structure, linear phase, group delay

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

An FIR filter is a weighted average of the last N input samples, with no feedback. Because nothing feeds back, it can never blow up. If the weights are symmetric, the filter delays every frequency by exactly the same amount, so waveforms keep their shape. No feedback filter (IIR) can do that in real time.

Tap = one coefficient h[k] and its delayed sample. "64 taps" = N = 64.
Delay line = the memory holding the last N samples.
Phase = how far (in angle) a sine is shifted when it passes through the filter.
Linear phase = the phase shift grows in a straight line with frequency. That is equivalent to a constant time delay.
Group delay τg = −dφ/dω, the delay of the signal's envelope, in samples. For a symmetric FIR it is (N−1)/2 at every frequency.

2. STEP BY STEP: WHY SYMMETRY GIVES LINEAR PHASE

  1. Take a symmetric 5-tap filter h = [a, b, c, b, a]. The centre tap c sits at k = 2 = (N−1)/2.
  2. Pair taps around the centre: a at k=0 and k=4, b at k=1 and k=3.
  3. Each pair contributes a·(e0 + e−j4ω) = a·e−j2ω·2cos(2ω). Every pair has the same factor e−j2ω.
  4. So H(ω) = e−j2ω × (a real number). Its phase is −2ω plus 0 or π: a straight line with slope −2.
  5. Slope −2 rad per rad/sample means every frequency is delayed by exactly 2 samples = (N−1)/2.
63-tap low-pass at f_s = 1 kHz: group delay = (63 − 1)/2 = 31 samples = 31 ms, at EVERY frequency a 10 Hz sine is delayed 31 ms (= 112° of phase); a 40 Hz sine is also delayed 31 ms (= 446°) → the shape of a pulse made of both is preserved, just 31 ms late. Same magnitude spec with a 4th-order Butterworth IIR (2 biquads): delay at DC ≈ 2–4 ms, but it varies with frequency → pulse shape distorts (overshoot/ringing). Cost on Cortex-M4 (q15, SMLAD = 2 MACs/cycle): 63 taps ≈ 32 MAC cycles + overhead per sample.

Windowed-sinc FIR design and taps vs transition width

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

The "perfect" low-pass filter, which passes everything below fc and nothing above, has an impulse response shaped like a sinc function (sin x / x) that goes on forever in both directions. We can't use infinitely many taps, so we cut it to N taps. A plain cut causes ripples, so we fade the ends smoothly with a window. That is the whole method, and it is the FIR design you can derive on a whiteboard.

sinc(x) = sin(πx)/(πx), with sinc(0) = 1.
fc here = cutoff in cycles per sample (0 … 0.5). The ideal impulse response is h[n] = 2fc·sinc(2fc·n).
Window w[n] = a bell-shaped weighting that is 1 in the middle and small at the edges (Hann, Hamming, Blackman, Kaiser).
Gibbs phenomenon = the ≈9 % overshoot ripple near the edge caused by abrupt truncation, which no number of taps removes.

2. STEP BY STEP: DESIGNING ONE

  5. Normalise so Σh = 1 (exact unity DC gain).
  6. Check the response (MATLAB freqz, or the Filter Lab). Quantise to q15 and check again.

Implementing FIR: circular buffer and MAC

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

An FIR needs the last N samples. The obvious way is to shift the whole array by one position every time a sample arrives. That costs N memory moves per sample. A circular buffer instead leaves the data where it is and moves an index that points to the newest sample. Old samples are overwritten in a ring. Every embedded interview asks you to code one.

Circular (ring) buffer = an array used as a loop. When the index reaches the end it wraps back to 0.
head = index of the newest sample.
Power-of-2 mask = if N = 2k, then (i & (N−1)) wraps i into 0 … N−1 with one AND instruction.
VFMA = the Cortex-M4F floating-point fused multiply-add instruction: one MAC.
SMLAD = Signed Multiply Accumulate Dual: two 16×16 MACs in one instruction, one cycle on Cortex-M4.
Block processing = filtering a batch of B samples per function call instead of one at a time.

2. STEP BY STEP: ONE OUTPUT WITH A RING BUFFER

  1. Advance head: head = (head + 1) & (N − 1).
  2. Write the new sample at hist[head]. That slot held the oldest sample, which we no longer need.
  3. Start the accumulator at 0. Set i = head.
  4. For k = 0 … N−1: acc += h[k] × hist[i], then step backwards in time with i = (i − 1) & (N − 1).
  5. Output acc. No data moved.

Direct Form I vs II vs Transposed DF II

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

The same five biquad coefficients can be wired into code in several orders. In perfect maths all of them give exactly the same output. In real 16- or 32-bit arithmetic they differ a lot in how much internal headroom they need and how much rounding noise they add. Interviewers love this because it separates people who have only used filter() in MATLAB from people who have shipped fixed-point filters.

State = a value remembered between samples (x1, x2, y1, y2 or w1, w2).
DF I (Direct Form I) = zeros first, then poles, with separate input and output delay lines. 4 states.
DF II = poles first into an intermediate signal w, then zeros, sharing one delay line. 2 states ("canonical").
TDF II (Transposed DF II) = the DF II diagram with all arrows reversed. 2 states that hold partial sums.
Accumulator = the wide register a MAC adds into (32 or 64 bits on Cortex-M).
Internal gain = how much bigger an internal signal can get than the input.
DF I states x1 x2 y1 y2 acc = b0·x + b1·x1 + b2·x2 − a1·y1 − a2·y2 ← all 5 products in ONE wide accumulator y = round/saturate(acc) ← the only rounding point x2 = x1; x1 = x; y2 = y1; y1 = y; DF II states w1 w2 w = x − a1·w1 − a2·w2 ← w = x / A(z): poles first, can be HUGE y = b0·w + b1·w1 + b2·w2 ← then zeros bring it back down w2 = w1; w1 = w; ← w must be STORED: rounded + saturated TDF II states s1 s2 y = b0·x + s1 s1 = b1·x − a1·y + s2 s2 = b2·x − a2·y

2. STEP BY STEP: WHY DF II OVERFLOWS

  1. DF II first computes w = x/A(z), i.e. it applies only the poles.
  2. For a low-pass with low cutoff, 1/A(z) has a huge gain at DC: 1/(1 + a1 + a2).
  3. So w becomes many times bigger than x, even though the final y is fine.
  4. In fixed point, w is stored in a 16-bit (or 32-bit) state. It saturates or wraps.
  5. The zeros, applied afterwards, can't undo the clipping. The output is distorted, or the filter oscillates.
  6. DF I never stores an intermediate like w. Its states are just past inputs and outputs, which are by definition in range. All products go into a 64-bit accumulator with one rounding at the end.

Cascades of second-order sections (SOS)

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

When you need a filter steeper than one biquad, for example a 6th-order Butterworth, you do not write it as one big equation with 13 coefficients. You split it into three biquads and run them one after another, with the output of each feeding the next. This is called a cascade of second-order sections (SOS). It is the only safe way to run high-order IIR filters.

SOS = Second-Order Section, i.e. one biquad.
Root sensitivity = how far the poles move when a coefficient changes slightly. For high-order polynomials with clustered roots it is extreme.
Pairing = deciding which zeros go in the same section as which poles.
Ordering = which section runs first.
Scaling = gain inserted between sections so intermediate signals don't overflow.

2. STEP BY STEP

  1. Design the filter in a tool and ask for SOS output directly: sos = butter(6, Wn, 'sos') in SciPy, or [sos, g] = tf2sos(b, a) in MATLAB.
  2. Each row of sos is [b0 b1 b2 a0 a1 a2] for one biquad. An odd order gives one first-order section (b2 = a2 = 0).
  3. Pair each pole pair with the zeros nearest to it, so each section has modest gain.
  4. Order the sections (fixed point): put the most resonant (highest-Q) section last, so its gain peak isn't amplified further downstream before the output.
  5. Scale: distribute the overall gain g so that no intermediate signal can exceed full scale.
  6. Run them in series. CMSIS does all of this in one call: arm_biquad_cascade_df1_f32(&S, in, out, blockSize) with numStages sections.

Butterworth vs Chebyshev vs Bessel (vs Elliptic)

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

These are "recipes" for where to place a filter's poles. Each one optimises a different thing: flattest passband (Butterworth), steepest edge for a given order (Chebyshev, Elliptic), or best preservation of waveform shape (Bessel). You can't have all of them at once. Picking the family is choosing which compromise suits your application.

Roll-off = how fast attenuation increases past the cutoff, e.g. 40 dB/decade for 2nd order.
Ripple = wobble in the gain within the passband or stopband.
Overshoot = how far a step response goes past its final value before settling.
Group-delay flatness = whether all frequencies are delayed by the same time. Flat = shape preserved.

2. STEP BY STEP: CHOOSING

  1. Is the waveform shape important (pulses, steps, control loops, ECG)? → Bessel, or a linear-phase FIR.
  2. Is it a general measurement where flat gain matters and moderate overshoot is OK? → Butterworth (the default).
  3. Do you need a sharp edge with a low order and can tolerate ripple? → Chebyshev I (passband ripple) or II (stopband ripple).
  4. Do you need the absolute minimum order for a brick-wall spec and don't care about phase? → Elliptic.

The DFT: bins and resolution fs/N

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) answers one question about a block of N samples: how much of each frequency is in it? It tests the block against N/2 + 1 evenly spaced frequencies, and for each one it reports an amplitude and a phase. The FFT (card 19) is just a fast way to compute the same thing.

N = number of samples in the block (the "FFT size").
X[k] = the k-th output: a complex number whose magnitude is "how much" and whose angle is "phase" of frequency k.
Bin = one of those test frequencies. Bin k is at fk = k·fs/N.
Bin width / resolution Δf = fs/N = 1/T, where T = N/fs is the block duration. You can only separate frequencies that are about this far apart.
Single-sided spectrum = for real input, bins N/2+1 … N−1 are mirror images (complex conjugates) of 1 … N/2−1, so only 0 … N/2 are shown.
X[k] = Σn=0..N−1 x[n] · e−j2πkn/N    fk = k·fs/N    amplitude ≈ 2|X[k]|/N

2. STEP BY STEP: WHAT ONE BIN COMPUTES

  1. Generate a test cosine and sine at exactly k cycles per block: cos(2πkn/N) and sin(2πkn/N).
  2. Multiply the signal by each, sample by sample, and sum. This is a correlation: "how much does x look like this frequency?"
  3. Re X[k] = Σ x·cos and Im X[k] = −Σ x·sin.
  4. Magnitude |X[k]| = √(Re² + Im²). For a sine of amplitude A that fits exactly k cycles, |X[k]| = A·N/2.
  5. Scale: amplitude = 2|X[k]|/N for 0 < k < N/2, and |X[0]|/N for DC.
  6. Doing this for all k directly costs N × N complex multiplies. That is why we need the FFT.
x = [1, 0, −1, 0] (a cosine at 1 cycle per 4 samples), N = 4 X[0] = 1 + 0 − 1 + 0 = 0 (no DC) X[1] = 1·1 + 0 + (−1)·e^(−jπ) + 0 = 1 + 1 = 2 → amplitude 2·2/4 = 1.0 ✓ X[2] = 1 + (−1)·e^(−j2π) = 0 X[3] = 2 (mirror of X[1]) Real numbers: f_s = 10 kHz, N = 1024 Δf = 10000/1024 = 9.77 Hz per bin; block length T = 102.4 ms bin 5 = 48.8 Hz, bin 6 = 58.6 Hz → 50 Hz falls BETWEEN bins (card 20) To separate 50 Hz from 52 Hz you need Δf ≲ 1 Hz → T ≥ 1 s → N ≥ 10 000 samples.

3. CODE: A DIRECT DFT (FOR UNDERSTANDING, NOT FOR SPEED)

…you expect more resolution from a faster sample rate. Δf = fs/N = 1/T. Doubling fs with the same N halves the observation time and makes resolution worse. Only a longer block improves resolution. …you forget the 2/N scaling. A 1 V sine shows up as "512" in a 1024-point FFT, and people report the wrong amplitude.

"Zero-padding from 1024 to 4096 gives 4× better resolution." It gives a smoother interpolated curve of the same 1024-sample information. The ability to separate two close tones is still set by T = 1024/fs (card 21).
The DFT correlates N samples with N complex sinusoids at k·fs/N, so the bin spacing is fs/N, which is 1 over the observation time. A sine of amplitude A that lands exactly on bin k gives |X[k]| = A·N/2, so I scale by 2/N for single-sided amplitude. Resolution only improves by observing longer; sampling faster with the same N makes it worse. Computed directly it's O(N²), which is why we use the FFT.

The FFT: radix-2 butterfly and O(N log N)

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

The Fast Fourier Transform computes exactly the same numbers as the DFT, but reuses work. The trick is that an N-point DFT can be split into two N/2-point DFTs (even samples and odd samples), combined with one cheap step. Repeat the split until you reach 2-point DFTs. N² operations become N/2·log2N small "butterflies". The FFT Explorer tab animates every butterfly of an 8-point FFT.

Radix-2 = splitting by 2 each time, so N must be a power of 2.
Decimation in time (DIT) = split the input into even and odd samples.
Twiddle factor WNk = e−j2πk/N, a unit-length complex rotation. Precomputed in a table.
Butterfly = the basic 2-in/2-out operation: A' = A + W·B, B' = A − W·B. One complex multiply, two complex adds.
Bit reversal = the input order DIT needs: index 1 (001) goes to position 4 (100), 3 (011) to 6 (110), and so on.
Stage = one pass over all N values. There are log2N stages, each with N/2 butterflies.

2. STEP BY STEP: AN 8-POINT DIT FFT

  1. Reorder the input by bit-reversed index: x[0], x[4], x[2], x[6], x[1], x[5], x[3], x[7].
  2. Stage 1: butterflies on adjacent pairs (span 1), all with W = W80 = 1. This produces four 2-point DFTs.
  3. Stage 2: butterflies with span 2, twiddles W80 and W82 = −j. This produces two 4-point DFTs.
  4. Stage 3: span 4, twiddles W80..3. This produces the 8-point DFT, in natural order X[0] … X[7].
  5. Total: 3 stages × 4 butterflies = 12 butterflies = 12 complex multiplies (several of them trivial, ×1 or ×−j). A direct DFT would need 64.

Windowing and spectral leakage

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

The DFT quietly assumes your N-sample block repeats forever. If the tone doesn't complete a whole number of cycles in the block, the repeated version has a jump at the seam. That jump contains energy at all frequencies, so a single pure tone "leaks" into many bins. A window fades the block's ends to zero so there is no jump. You trade a slightly wider peak for far less leakage.

Spectral leakage = energy from one frequency appearing in other bins.
Rectangular window = no window (all ones). Equivalent to just cutting the block.
Main lobe = the peak around the true frequency. Its width limits how close two tones can be and still be resolved.
Sidelobes = the ripples of leakage either side. "Highest sidelobe −31 dB" means leakage is at most 31 dB below the peak.
Coherent gain (CG) = mean of the window. It shrinks amplitudes, so divide by Σw instead of N to correct.
ENBW = Equivalent Noise BandWidth in bins. A wider window collects more noise per bin.
Scalloping loss = how much a tone exactly halfway between bins reads low.

2. STEP BY STEP: WHY LEAKAGE HAPPENS

  1. Take N = 1024 samples of a tone at 100.5 cycles per block, i.e. between bins 100 and 101.
  2. The DFT treats the block as periodic. At the seam between copies, the waveform jumps.
  3. Equivalently: you multiplied an infinite sine by a rectangle, and in frequency that convolves the line with the rectangle's transform, a sinc with sidelobes falling only 6 dB per octave.
  4. Result: the peak reads 3.9 dB low (scalloping) and bins far away still show −30 to −45 dB of "tone".
  5. Multiply by a Hann window first. The ends taper to 0, so the seam is smooth. Sidelobes fall by 18 dB per octave, and the same far bins are at −70 to −110 dB.

Zero-padding myths, real FFT, magnitude and dB

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

Three practical details decide whether your FFT numbers are right. Zero-padding appends zeros to make the block longer. It makes the plot smoother, but it does not reveal new detail. A real FFT exploits the fact that ADC samples have no imaginary part, which halves the work. Magnitude in dB needs the right scale factors, or every number you report is off.

Zero-padding = appending zeros to N real samples to make an M-point FFT (M > N).
Interpolation = computing values between existing points. Zero-padding interpolates the spectrum.
Real FFT (RFFT) = an FFT specialised for real input. It computes N/2+1 unique bins in about half the time.
dBFS = decibels relative to full scale. A full-scale sine reads 0 dBFS.
Packed format = how CMSIS stores the RFFT output to fit N/2+1 complex bins into N floats.

2. ZERO-PADDING, STEP BY STEP

  1. You have N = 256 real samples at fs = 1 kHz: T = 256 ms and Δf = 3.9 Hz.
  2. Append 768 zeros and run a 1024-point FFT. The points are now spaced 0.98 Hz apart.
  3. But the main lobe width is still set by T = 256 ms. Two tones 2 Hz apart still merge into one peak, now just drawn with more points.
  4. Where zero-padding does help: locating the peak of a single tone more precisely (less scalloping), and making the FFT size a power of 2.

Goertzel: detecting one tone cheaply

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

If you only care about one or a few frequencies (is the 1 kHz test tone present? which DTMF key is pressed? is there 50 Hz on this line?), computing a whole FFT is wasteful. The Goertzel algorithm computes a single DFT bin with a tiny 2nd-order recursion: one multiply and two adds per sample, no buffer, no bit-reversal. The Tools tab has a working DTMF decoder built on it.

k = the bin index you want, k = round(N·ftarget/fs).
coeff = 2·cos(2πk/N), the only constant.
s, s1, s2 = the current and two previous values of the internal recursion.
DTMF = Dual-Tone Multi-Frequency, the telephone keypad tones. Each key is one "row" tone (697, 770, 852, 941 Hz) plus one "column" tone (1209, 1336, 1477, 1633 Hz).

2. STEP BY STEP

  1. Choose N and compute k = round(N·f/fs) and coeff = 2cos(2πk/N). Done once.
  2. Set s1 = s2 = 0.
  3. For each sample: s = x + coeff·s1 − s2, then s2 = s1, s1 = s. (This is a resonator: an IIR with poles exactly on the unit circle at bin k.)
  4. After N samples: power = s1² + s2² − coeff·s1·s2. This equals |X[k]|².
  5. Compare the power to a threshold (or to the other bins' power), then reset and start the next block.

Overlap-add: fast convolution with FFTs

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

Long FIR filters (hundreds to thousands of taps, e.g. a room-correction or matched filter) cost too much as direct convolution. Because convolution in time equals multiplication in frequency, you can instead FFT a block, multiply by the filter's FFT, and inverse FFT. Overlap-add is the bookkeeping that makes block-by-block FFT filtering give exactly the same output as a continuous FIR.

L = input block length. M = filter length (taps). NFFT ≥ L + M − 1.
Circular convolution = what multiplying FFTs really computes. The result wraps around the block edges unless you pad enough.
Overlap-add (OLA) = each block's output is L+M−1 long. Add the overlapping M−1 tail into the next block's output.
Overlap-save (OLS) = the alternative: overlap the input blocks and discard the first M−1 outputs of each.

2. STEP BY STEP (OVERLAP-ADD)

Decimation and interpolation (multirate)

ADVANCED▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

Decimation lowers the sample rate (e.g. 48 kHz → 8 kHz) and interpolation raises it (8 kHz → 48 kHz). Firmware does this constantly: oversample an ADC and decimate to gain resolution, feed a 16 kHz voice codec from a 48 kHz I2S bus, or run a slow control loop on a fast current measurement. The rule is simple and often broken: filter before you throw samples away, and filter after you insert samples.

↓R (downsample by R) = keep every R-th sample. ↑L (upsample by L) = insert L−1 zeros between samples.
Anti-alias filter (digital, before ↓R) = low-pass at the new Nyquist, fs/(2R).
Imaging = the copies of the spectrum that appear after zero-insertion. The anti-imaging filter (after ↑L) removes them.
Polyphase = an implementation trick: compute only the outputs you keep (decimator) or skip multiplying by the inserted zeros (interpolator). It saves a factor of R or L.

2. STEP BY STEP: DECIMATE 8 kHz → 1 kHz (R = 8)

  1. New Nyquist = 1000/2 = 500 Hz. Everything between 500 Hz and 4 kHz must be attenuated, or it will fold into 0–500 Hz.
  2. Design an FIR: pass 0–400 Hz, stop ≥ 500 Hz with 60 dB. Δf/fs = 100/8000 = 0.0125 → about 60/(22·0.0125) ≈ 218 taps.
  3. Naive: run all 218 taps at 8 kHz and keep 1 output in 8. That's 1.74 M MAC/s, and 7/8 of the work is thrown away.
  4. Polyphase: compute the FIR only at the kept instants. That's 218 MACs × 1 kHz = 218 k MAC/s, 8× less. This is what arm_fir_decimate_f32 does.
  5. Better still, use stages: ↓2, ↓2, ↓2 with short half-band filters (card 13), or a CIC followed by a compensation FIR (card 25).

…you decimate by plain sample-skipping. To decimate by R I low-pass to below the new Nyquist, fs/2R, then keep every R-th sample, and I only compute the outputs I keep, which is the polyphase trick in arm_fir_decimate. To interpolate by L I insert L − 1 zeros and low-pass to remove the spectral images, with a gain of L. For large ratios I split into stages, for example half-bands, or a CIC plus a compensation FIR.

CIC filters: decimation with no multipliers

ADVANCED▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

A CIC (Cascaded Integrator-Comb) filter is a moving average rearranged so it needs only adders and delays, no multipliers, regardless of the decimation ratio. It is the standard first stage after a sigma-delta modulator, for example a PDM MEMS microphone, or the STM32 DFSDM peripheral, whose "sincN filter" is a CIC.

Integrator = y[n] = y[n−1] + x[n] (a running sum), running at the high input rate.
Comb = y[n] = x[n] − x[n−M] (a difference), running at the low output rate.
R = decimation ratio. M = differential delay (usually 1). N = number of stages (order).
PDM = Pulse-Density Modulation, a 1-bit stream where the density of 1s encodes the amplitude.
Bit growth = the extra bits the integrators need: N·log2(R·M).
Droop = the CIC's passband isn't flat. It sags like a sincN, so a short compensation FIR (CFIR) follows it.
H(z) = [ (1 − z−RM) / (1 − z−1) ]N   gain = (RM)N   Bout = Bin + N·log2(RM)

2. STEP BY STEP

  1. N integrators in series at the input rate: each sample just adds into a register.
  2. Downsample by R: keep every R-th integrator output.
  3. N combs in series at the output rate: each subtracts the value from M samples ago.
  4. Result = an N-th order moving average of length R·M, with DC gain (RM)N. Shift right by N·log2(RM) to normalise.
  5. The integrators will overflow and wrap. That's fine: two's-complement wrap-around cancels in the combs, provided the register width ≥ Bout and you use wrapping (not saturating) arithmetic.

Zero multiplies.

A CIC is an N-th order moving average built from N integrators at the high rate and N combs at the low rate, so it decimates with no multipliers at all. Median filters for spikes

ADVANCED▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

A median filter outputs the middle value of the last N samples after sorting them. A single huge spike, such as a contactor bounce, an ESD hit or an ADC glitch, ends up at one end of the sorted list and is simply ignored. An averaging filter would instead smear the spike into a smaller but longer error. Medians are non-linear (card 03), which is exactly why they work.

Median = the middle value of a sorted list. Median of {3, 100, 4} = 4.
Impulsive noise = rare, large, short outliers (as opposed to Gaussian noise, which is small and constant).
Breakdown point = how many outliers the filter tolerates. A median of N rejects up to (N−1)/2 consecutive spike samples.
Sorting network = a fixed sequence of compare-and-swap operations, branch-free with constant time. Ideal for N = 3, 5, 7.

2. STEP BY STEP

  1. Keep the last N samples (N odd, usually 3 or 5).
  2. Copy them and sort the copy. Never sort the history buffer itself, because you'd lose the time order.
  3. Output the middle element, index (N−1)/2.
  4. Follow with an EMA or MA if you also need to smooth Gaussian noise: median first, then average.
Battery voltage (mV), one contactor spike: x = 3700 3702 3698 1200 3701 3699 EMA α=0.25: 3700 3700.5 3699.9 3074.9 3231.4 3348.3 ← dips 625 mV and recovers slowly MA-3: – – 3700 2867 2866 2867 ← 3 samples ruined median-3: – – 3700 3698 3698 3699 ← spike gone completely A "pack < 3300 mV → shutdown" rule trips with the EMA and MA, never with the median.

3. CODE

✗ WRONG: qsort on every sample, sorts history
✓ RIGHT: branch-free median of 3 / 5

…you use a median where the signal has fast real features. A median of N removes any genuine pulse shorter than (N+1)/2 samples. A real 1-sample current spike, which is exactly what an overcurrent detector needs to see, disappears. Use medians only on signals whose real content is slower than the window.

A median filter sorts the last N samples and outputs the middle one, so an isolated spike lands at the end of the sorted list and is ignored completely. A linear filter would only spread it out. It's non-linear, so it has no frequency response. I use median-of-3 or 5 with a small sorting network, then an EMA for Gaussian noise. It's ideal for contactor or ESD spikes on battery voltage, but it would also delete any real pulse shorter than half the window.

Kalman filter (1-D scalar) and complementary filters

ADVANCED▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

A Kalman filter blends a prediction ("where I think the value is now") with a noisy measurement, weighting each by how much it can be trusted. In 1-D, for a slowly varying quantity, it turns out to be an EMA whose α (called the Kalman gain K) is computed from the noise levels instead of hand-tuned. A complementary filter blends two sensors with opposite weaknesses, typically a gyro (accurate short-term, drifts long-term) and an accelerometer (noisy short-term, correct on average), using a high-pass on one and a low-pass on the other.

x̂ (x-hat) = the estimate. P = the variance of the estimate (how unsure we are).
Q = process noise variance: how much the true value can change per step.
R = measurement noise variance: σ² of the sensor.
K = Kalman gain, 0…1: how much to trust the new measurement.
Innovation = z − x̂, the measurement minus the prediction.

2. STEP BY STEP (SCALAR, RANDOM-WALK MODEL)

  1. Predict: x̂ stays the same (the model says "constant plus drift"). The uncertainty grows: P = P + Q.
  2. Gain: K = P / (P + R). A very unsure estimate (large P) means trust the measurement (K → 1). A very noisy sensor (large R) means K → 0.
  3. Update: x̂ = x̂ + K·(z − x̂). Look familiar? This is y += α(x − y) with α = K.
  4. Shrink uncertainty: P = (1 − K)·P.
  5. Repeat. K starts large (fast initial convergence) and settles to a constant K∞. From then on it is exactly an EMA.
steady state: P⁻ = (Q + √(Q² + 4QR)) / 2,   K∞ = P⁻ / (P⁻ + R)
Temperature sensor: σ = 1 °C → R = 1.0; true value drifts ~0.1 °C/step → Q = 0.01 start P = 10 (unsure): K1 = 10.01/11.01 = 0.909, then 0.48, 0.33, 0.25, … → K∞ P⁻ = (0.01 + √(0.0001 + 0.04)) / 2 = (0.01 + 0.20025)/2 = 0.10512 K∞ = 0.10512 / 1.10512 = 0.0951 → identical to an EMA with α = 0.095 noise reduction: output σ ≈ √(K/(2−K)) × 1 °C ≈ 0.22 °C Complementary filter (pitch angle), dt = 10 ms, crossover τ = 0.5 s: a = τ/(τ + dt) = 0.5/0.51 = 0.980 angle = 0.980·(angle + gyro_rate·dt) + 0.020·accel_angle → gyro trusted above ≈ 1/(2πτ) = 0.32 Hz, accelerometer below.
gyro rate ─► ∫dt ─► [ high-pass τ ] ──┐ (+)──► angle HP + LP = 1 (complementary) accel angle ─────► [ low-pass τ ] ────┘
✗ WRONG: K hard-coded, P never updated
✓ RIGHT: scalar Kalman

…Q is too small (you claim the value never changes): K → 0 and the filter stops following real changes. A battery estimate then lags a real load step by minutes. …Q is too large: K → 1 and nothing is filtered. Tune R from the sensor's measured σ², then pick Q from how fast the real quantity can move.

A scalar Kalman filter predicts with P += Q, computes the gain K = P/(P + R), updates x += K(z − x) and shrinks P by (1 − K). With a constant model, K converges to a steady value, so in steady state it's exactly an EMA whose α is derived from the process and measurement noise rather than guessed. A complementary filter is the sensor-fusion cousin: high-pass the integrated gyro, low-pass the accelerometer, with weights summing to 1, for example 0.98 and 0.02.

Fixed-point DSP: Q15, Q31, accumulators, saturation, scaling

ADVANCED▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

Many MCUs (Cortex-M0/M3, many M4 designs, DSP-heavy loops) do DSP with integers instead of floats, because integer maths is faster, deterministic and supports two 16-bit operations per instruction. To represent fractions with integers, we agree that an int16 value v means v / 32768. That convention is called Q15. The whole art of fixed-point DSP is keeping numbers in range (no overflow) while keeping enough bits (no precision loss).

Qm.n / Qn = a fixed-point format with n fractional bits. Value = integer / 2n.
Q15 = int16, range [−1, +1 − 2−15] = [−1, 0.99997], step 3.05 × 10−5.
Q31 = int32, range [−1, 1 − 2−31], step 4.66 × 10−10.
Saturation = clamp to the max/min instead of wrapping. 32767 + 1 → 32767, not −32768.
Guard bits = extra integer bits in the accumulator so a sum of many products can't overflow.
Scaling = shifting signals or coefficients to keep each stage's output in range.

2. STEP BY STEP: ONE Q15 MULTIPLY

  1. a = 0.5 → 16384 (0x4000). b = 0.25 → 8192 (0x2000).
  2. Multiply as integers into 32 bits: 16384 × 8192 = 134 217 728 = 0x0800_0000. This is a Q30 number (15 + 15 fractional bits).
  3. Shift right by 15 to get back to Q15: 134 217 728 >> 15 = 4096 → 4096/32768 = 0.125 ✓.
  4. Round instead of truncating: add 1 << 14 before the shift.
  5. Special case: −1 × −1 = +1, which doesn't exist in Q15. (−32768)² >> 15 = 32768 overflows int16 → you must saturate to 32767.
Accumulator width for a 64-tap Q15 FIR: each product: Q30, up to 2³⁰ in magnitude sum of 64 products: up to 64 × 2³⁰ = 2³⁶ → needs 1 + 36 = 37 bits → a 32-bit accumulator can overflow (unless Σ|h| ≤ 1 and inputs are scaled) → CMSIS arm_fir_q15 uses a 64-bit accumulator (34.30 format): no overflow possible → arm_fir_fast_q15 uses 32 bits (2.30): faster, but YOU must scale inputs by log2(numTaps) Guard bits needed = ceil(log2(N)): 64 taps → 6 bits Q15 coefficient precision: 0.001 → round(0.001 × 32768) = 33 → 0.001007 (0.7 % error) 0.00001 → 0.33 → 0 (coefficient vanishes!)

4. SCALING PER STAGE

  1. For each stage, find the worst-case gain from the input to that stage's output: L1 norm Σ|h| (absolute guarantee), L∞ = peak |H(f)| (for sinusoids) or L2 (for noise, statistical).

Coefficient quantisation, limit cycles and fixed-point stability

ADVANCED▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

When you round a filter's coefficients to 16 bits, you get a slightly different filter. For FIR filters that only raises the stopband a bit. For IIR filters with poles near the unit circle, the poles can move enough to change the response drastically, or even cross the circle and make the filter unstable. Separately, rounding inside the feedback loop can make an IIR filter hum forever at a small amplitude with zero input. That is a limit cycle.

Coefficient quantisation = rounding ideal (real) coefficients to the nearest representable fixed-point value.
Pole sensitivity = how much a pole moves per unit coefficient change. It is worst when poles are close to each other and to z = 1.
Zero-input limit cycle = a small sustained oscillation, or a stuck non-zero value, caused by rounding when the input is zero.
Overflow limit cycle = a large oscillation caused by two's-complement wrap. Saturation prevents it.
Dead band = the range of values the rounding cannot move the output out of.
Error feedback / noise shaping = keeping the rounding error and adding it back next sample, so rounding stops biasing the loop.

2. STEP BY STEP: WHY A LOW-CUTOFF BIQUAD BREAKS

  1. A low cutoff (fc ≪ fs) puts both poles very close to z = 1, so a1 ≈ −2 and a2 ≈ +1.
  2. The distance of the poles from z = 1 is what sets the cutoff, and it is encoded in the tiny difference 1 + a1 + a2.
  3. Rounding a1 and a2 to 2−14 (Q14, postShift 1) changes that small difference by a comparable amount, so the pole moves significantly.
  4. The b coefficients ((1−cos ω0)/2 ≈ ω0²/4) are tiny and round to 0. The filter's gain collapses.
  5. Fewer coefficient bits → a pole can land exactly on |z| = 1 (an oscillator) or outside it (unstable).

  1. Check the quantised filter, not the ideal one: recompute poles and response from the integer coefficients (the Filter Lab does this: toggle fixed point and reduce the bit count).
  2. Lower fs for the filter (decimate first), so fc/fs isn't tiny.
  3. Use DF I with a wide accumulator (CMSIS q31), or float with TDF II.

  5. Error feedback or dither eliminates small zero-input limit cycles.

Quantising IIR coefficients moves the poles, and low-cutoff filters are the worst case because their poles cluster near z = 1 where a1 ≈ −2 and a2 ≈ 1. Rounding in the feedback path also causes limit cycles, small oscillations or stuck values with zero input. Cortex-M4/M7 DSP instructions: SMLAD, SIMD, single-cycle MAC

ADVANCED▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

The Cortex-M4 and M7 add a "DSP extension" to the basic Cortex-M instruction set: instructions that do a multiply and an add in one go, work on two 16-bit values packed in one 32-bit register at once (SIMD), and saturate automatically. A Cortex-M3 or M0 has none of these. That is why the same FIR runs 2–4× faster on an M4 at the same clock. Interviewers at semiconductor companies like to ask what SMLAD does.

SIMD = Single Instruction, Multiple Data: one instruction processes two 16-bit (or four 8-bit) values packed in a register.

Performance: cycles per sample, block processing, DMA ping-pong

ADVANCED▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

Real-time DSP has a hard budget: every sample must be processed before the next one arrives. Instead of interrupting the CPU for every sample (expensive), the ADC or I2S data is streamed by DMA into a buffer split in two halves. While DMA fills one half, the CPU processes the other. This is the ping-pong or double-buffer pattern, and it is the backbone of every audio, metering and vibration product.

Cycles per sample = CPU clock / sample rate. The total budget for everything.
Block size B = samples processed per call. Larger B means less per-call overhead but more latency.
Circular DMA = DMA that wraps back to the buffer start automatically (STM32: CIRC bit in DMA_SxCR).
HT / TC interrupts = Half-Transfer and Transfer-Complete. They fire when the first or second half of the buffer has been filled.
Latency = the time from a sample entering to its result coming out. At least one block plus the filter's group delay.
DWT_CYCCNT = a cycle counter in the Cortex-M Data Watchpoint and Trace unit, used to measure exact cycles.

2. STEP BY STEP: PING-PONG

  1. Allocate buf[2·B]. Start the ADC with circular DMA over the whole buffer, with HT and TC interrupts enabled.
  2. DMA fills buf[0 … B−1]. The HT interrupt fires and sets a flag: "half A ready".
  3. The DSP task processes half A, while DMA is already filling buf[B … 2B−1].
  4. TC fires: half B is ready. DMA wraps around and starts overwriting half A.
  5. The DSP must finish each half in less than B/fs seconds, or DMA overwrites data still being processed. Check that with an overrun flag.
Audio: f_s = 48 kHz, STM32F407 @ 168 MHz → 168e6 / 48e3 = 3 500 cycles per sample Block B = 64 → deadline per half = 64 / 48 000 = 1.333 ms = 224 000 cycles Work per block (estimates, measure on target): 4 biquads f32 (≈ 15–25 cycles/stage/sample × 4 × 64 ≈ 4–6 k) + 128-tap FIR (≈ 1.3 cycles/tap × 128 × 64 ≈ 10.6 k) ≈ 15–17 k cycles → ≈ 7 % of the 224 k budget. Per-sample interrupts instead: 48 000 × ~30 cycles entry/exit = 1.4 M cycles/s wasted. Latency ≥ one block (1.33 ms) + output buffer (1.33 ms) + filter group delay. Measure, don't guess: CoreDebug->DEMCR |= CoreDebug_DEMCR_TRCENA_Msk; DWT->CYCCNT = 0; DWT->CTRL |= 1; uint32_t t0 = DWT->CYCCNT; process(); cycles = DWT->CYCCNT − t0;
✗ WRONG: heavy DSP inside the DMA ISR, no overrun check
✓ RIGHT: ISR flags, task processes, detect overrun

…processing takes longer than one half-period. DMA overwrites the half you're reading. Symptom: periodic glitches at the block rate, worse when the CPU is busy elsewhere. …the D-cache on an M7 is enabled and the DMA buffer is cacheable: the CPU reads stale data. Invalidate the cache for that half (SCB_InvalidateDCache_by_Addr) or place the buffer in non-cacheable memory or DTCM.

I start from the budget, clock divided by sample rate, for example 3500 cycles per sample at 48 kHz on a 168 MHz M4. Then I stream samples with circular DMA into a ping-pong buffer: on the half-transfer interrupt I process the first half while DMA fills the second, and vice versa, so I pay interrupt overhead per block, not per sample. Each half must be processed within B/fs. I measure that with DWT_CYCCNT and an overrun counter, and on M7 I handle cache coherency for the DMA buffer.

Applications I: power metering (RMS, power factor) and current sensing

ADVANCED▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

An energy meter, a smart plug, or a charger's input monitor samples mains voltage and current and computes how much real power flows. The maths is simple: averages of products. Getting it accurate needs DSP care: integer numbers of mains cycles, DC offset removal, and phase alignment between the V and I channels. The RMS/Power tool in the Tools tab lets you break each of these deliberately.

RMS (Root Mean Square) = √(mean of x²). The DC value that would deliver the same heating power. A sine of peak A has RMS A/√2.
Real (active) power P = mean of v·i over whole cycles, in watts.
Apparent power S = Vrms × Irms, in VA.
Power factor PF = P/S. It equals cos φ only for pure sinusoids. With harmonics, PF = cos φ1 × 1/√(1 + THDi²).
THD = Total Harmonic Distortion = √(Σ harmonic²) / fundamental.
Shunt = a small precise resistor whose voltage drop measures current.

2. STEP BY STEP

  1. Sample v and i simultaneously (dual ADC or simultaneous sample-and-hold), e.g. fs = 4 kHz = 80 samples per 50 Hz cycle.
  2. Remove the DC offset from each channel (the ADC mid-scale, plus drift) with a slow high-pass or by subtracting the running mean.
  3. Accumulate Σv², Σi², Σv·i over an integer number of mains cycles (detect zero crossings, or a window like 10 cycles = 200 ms).
  4. Vrms = √(Σv²/N), Irms = √(Σi²/N), P = Σv·i/N, S = Vrms·Irms, PF = P/S.
  5. Calibrate gain per channel and compensate the phase error between V and I paths (CT and filter delays) by a fractional-sample delay.
230 V rms, 5 A rms fundamental lagging 30°, plus 20 % 3rd harmonic current: I1 = 5 A, I3 = 1 A → I_rms = √(25 + 1) = 5.099 A P = 230 × 5 × cos30° = 995.9 W (harmonics carry no power: V is a pure sine) S = 230 × 5.099 = 1172.8 VA PF = 995.9 / 1172.8 = 0.849 (cos φ alone = 0.866; distortion factor = 1/√1.04 = 0.981) Window of 10.3 cycles instead of 10: the leftover 0.3 cycle adds a start-phase-dependent term → up to ≈ ±0.8 % RMS ripple per window Current sensing: 2 mΩ shunt, 20 A → 40 mV; gain-50 amplifier → 2.0 V at the ADC 12-bit LSB at 3.3 V = 0.806 mV → at the shunt 0.806 mV / 50 = 16.1 µV → / 2 mΩ = 8.06 mA per LSB
✗ WRONG: fixed N not matched to cycles, DC left in
✓ RIGHT: offset-removed, whole cycles, 64-bit sums

…the window isn't a whole number of cycles: RMS and P readings ripple from window to window. …the V and I channels are sampled at different times (sequential ADC conversions, e.g. 20 µs apart): a 20 µs skew at 50 Hz is 0.36° of phase, which gives a 0.6 % PF error at PF = 0.5. …DC offset is left in: Irms reads several amps with no load.

I sample voltage and current simultaneously, remove each channel's DC offset, and accumulate Σv², Σi² and Σv·i over a whole number of mains cycles, usually triggered by zero crossings. Then Vrms and Irms are square roots of the means, P is the mean of v·i, and PF = P/(Vrms·Irms), which only equals cos φ when there are no harmonics. Accuracy comes from cycle-synchronous windows, 64-bit accumulators, and calibrating gain and V-I phase skew.

Applications II: motor control, battery smoothing, audio, vibration

ADVANCED▶

1. WHAT IS IT?

The same handful of tools (EMA, biquad, FIR, median, FFT, Goertzel) show up in every product. What changes is the constraint that dominates: delay in motor control, spikes and accuracy in battery monitoring, throughput and latency in audio, frequency resolution in vibration analysis. Knowing which constraint matters is what interviewers are really testing.

FOC = Field-Oriented Control, the standard method for driving brushless and PMSM motors. It runs a current loop at the PWM rate (10–20 kHz).
Phase margin = how much extra phase lag a control loop tolerates before it oscillates.
OCV = Open-Circuit Voltage of a battery cell, which maps to state of charge.
Envelope analysis = band-pass, rectify, low-pass, then FFT. Used to find bearing-fault repetition rates hidden under a high-frequency resonance.

2. EACH APPLICATION, STEP BY STEP

  1. Motor control (FOC): sample phase currents at the PWM centre (centre-aligned PWM), where the switching-ripple current passes through its average value, so one sample equals the average current with no filter at all. Keep filters inside the current loop to almost nothing, because each sample of delay at 20 kHz costs 360° × fc/20 kHz of phase at crossover fc. Filter outside the loop (speed display, telemetry) as much as you like. Speed from an encoder: differentiate position, then a 1st-order low-pass or a PLL observer.
  2. Battery voltage smoothing (BMS): sample at a fixed fs, median-of-3/5 to kill contactor and ESD spikes, then an EMA (τ ≈ 1–5 s) for the display and SoC. Keep the unfiltered path for over- and under-voltage protection, which needs speed, but debounce it with a short median plus a count. Compensate load: VOCV ≈ Vmeas + I·Rint.
  3. Audio: I2S at 48 kHz with DMA ping-pong, block 32–128 samples. EQ as cascaded f32 biquads (TDF II) or q31 DF I. Latency budget is usually < 10 ms for live sound. FIR for linear-phase crossovers; FFT overlap-add for long reverbs.
  4. Vibration analysis: MEMS accelerometer at 1–26 kHz, using the sensor's own digital anti-alias filter and ODR (output data rate) setting. Compute overall RMS velocity over 10–1000 Hz (ISO 10816/20816 machine-health bands, in mm/s RMS). FFT with Hann window and averaging, for 1× / 2× running-speed peaks (imbalance, misalignment). Envelope analysis for bearing defect frequencies.

60-SECOND REVISION
  • Digital filters work in cycles/sample. Change fs and every cutoff moves.
  • Aliasing can only be prevented before sampling (or before decimation).
  • LTI ⇒ impulse response describes everything. A new frequency at the output means something non-linear happened.
  • MA = best noise reduction for a given step response, nulls at fs/N, −13 dB sidelobes, O(1) with a running sum.
  • EMA: y += α(x−y), α ≈ 2πfc/fs, keep extra state bits.
  • Poles inside the unit circle = stable. FIR is always stable.
  • Symmetric FIR = linear phase = (N−1)/2 delay.
  • Window sets FIR attenuation; taps set transition width.
  • Biquads from the RBJ cookbook; Q = 0.707 = Butterworth; cascade for higher order.
  • DF I for fixed point, TDF II for float. DF II overflows internally.
  • Δf = fs/N = 1/T. Window to reduce leakage; Σw for amplitude.
  • FFT = N/2·log2N butterflies. Goertzel for a few tones.
  • Decimate: filter then drop; CIC is multiplier-free with (RM)N gain.
  • Median for spikes; Kalman 1-D converges to an EMA.
  • Q15×Q15 = Q30; 64-bit accumulators; saturate; scale per stage.
  • Coefficient quantisation moves IIR poles; low fc/fs is the danger zone.
  • SMLAD = 2 q15 MACs per cycle. Loops are load-bound.
  • DMA ping-pong: process one half while the other fills, finish within B/fs.
  • Control loops care about delay; metering about cycle sync; BMS about spikes.
