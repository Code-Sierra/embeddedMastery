01What problem do clocks, timers and watchdogs solve?

A microcontroller (MCU) is a small computer on one chip: a CPU, memory and peripherals (blocks of hardware such as UARTs, timers and ADCs). Nothing inside it does anything unless a clock ticks. A clock is a signal that switches between 0 and 1 at a steady rate, and every flip-flop in the chip advances one step on each tick. So the first problem is simply: where does that steady signal come from, and how fast is it?

The second problem is time itself. Firmware constantly needs to answer questions like:

  • "Blink this LED every 500 ms." (a periodic event)
  • "Drive this motor with a 20 kHz square wave that is high 25 % of the time." (a waveform, called PWM)
  • "How long was this pulse from the sensor?" (a measurement)
  • "If the modem has not answered in 2 seconds, give up." (a timeout)

You could try to do all of this with software loops (for (i = 0; i < 100000; i++);), but a loop's duration changes with the compiler, the optimisation level, the flash speed and every interrupt that happens to arrive. A hardware timer solves this: it is a counter that is incremented by the clock, completely independently of the CPU, so it measures time exactly and keeps doing so while the CPU is busy elsewhere.

The third problem is failure. Firmware can hang: a deadlock, an infinite loop, a corrupted pointer, a peripheral that never answers. A product in a field cabinet has nobody to press reset. A watchdog is a hardware timer that resets the chip unless software proves, regularly, that it is still healthy. It turns "hung forever" into "rebooted after one second".

Clocks give the chip its heartbeat, timers turn that heartbeat into exact time, waveforms and measurements without using the CPU, and the watchdog resets the chip if the software stops proving it is alive.

02The physical picture: what is actually on the chip and the board

2.1 Where the clock comes from: oscillators

An oscillator is a circuit that produces a repeating signal on its own. An MCU uses two kinds:

  1. RC oscillator (R = resistor, C = capacitor). Built entirely inside the chip. A capacitor charges through a resistor until it reaches a threshold, then the circuit flips and starts again. It starts in microseconds and costs nothing, but its frequency depends on the exact resistor and capacitor values, which change with temperature and supply voltage. On the F407 the 16 MHz RC oscillator, called HSI (High-Speed Internal), is within ±1 % at room temperature but can be 8 % slow at −40 °C.
  2. Crystal oscillator. A tiny slice of quartz on the PCB, connected to two MCU pins, plus two small capacitors. Quartz is piezoelectric: it bends when a voltage is applied and produces a voltage when it bends. It mechanically vibrates at one very precise frequency set by its size and cut, like a tuning fork. It takes milliseconds to start, but it is accurate to about 20 parts per million. On the F407 the external crystal is called HSE (High-Speed External), typically 8 or 25 MHz.

There are also two slow ones: LSI (Low-Speed Internal, an RC around 32 kHz, used by the watchdog) and LSE (Low-Speed External, a 32.768 kHz watch crystal, used by the real-time clock).

2.2 Making it faster: the PLL

8 MHz is too slow for a CPU that can run at 168 MHz, and crystals much above 25 MHz are awkward. So the chip contains a PLL (Phase-Locked Loop): a circuit containing a voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) that is constantly corrected so that its output, divided down, stays exactly in step with the crystal. The result is a high frequency that has the crystal's long-term accuracy. On the F407 you choose a divider M, a multiplier N and an output divider P: 8 MHz ÷ 4 × 168 ÷ 2 = 168 MHz.

2.3 Distributing it: the clock tree and buses

The chosen clock is called SYSCLK (system clock). It is then divided for different parts of the chip, because not everything can run at full speed. The CPU and memory sit on the AHB (Advanced High-performance Bus) at HCLK. Peripherals sit on two slower APB buses (Advanced Peripheral Bus): APB1 at most 42 MHz and APB2 at most 84 MHz. This branching structure is called the clock tree. Every peripheral calculates its timing (baud rate, timer period) from its own bus clock, not from the CPU clock.

2.4 The timer: just a counter with comparators

A hardware timer is a register that adds 1 on every tick of its input clock, plus a few comparators that watch it:

timer clock ──► [ prescaler ÷(PSC+1) ] ──► [ counter CNT: 0,1,2,…,ARR,0,1,… ]
                                                  │                 │
                                     CNT == ARR ? ┘                 └ CNT < CCR ?
                                     → wrap to 0,                    → output pin
                                       "update event",                 high or low
                                       interrupt                       (PWM)

That is the whole idea. Periodic interrupts, PWM, pulse measurement and timeouts are all variations on "a counter, a limit, and a compare value".

2.5 The watchdog: a counter you must keep resetting

A watchdog is a down-counter driven by its own clock (on the F407, the independent watchdog uses LSI). Software must write a magic value to it ("kick" or "feed" it) before it reaches zero. If it reaches zero, it pulls the chip's reset line. Once started, software cannot turn it off.

03Key terms, one at a time

Read each definition once. Every later card uses these words without re-defining them.

Frequency (f) and period (T)
How many times per second something repeats, in hertz (Hz). 1 kHz = 1 000 per second, 1 MHz = 1 000 000. The period is the time for one repeat: T = 1/f. At 168 MHz, T = 1/168 000 000 = 5.95 ns (nanoseconds). At 1 kHz, T = 1 ms.
ppm (parts per million)
A way to express small frequency errors. 1 ppm = 0.0001 %. A 20 ppm clock that should run at 8 000 000 Hz may run at 8 000 160 Hz. Over a day (86 400 s), 20 ppm adds up to 86 400 × 20 / 1 000 000 = 1.73 s.
SYSCLK, HCLK, PCLK1, PCLK2
SYSCLK is the clock selected for the whole system. HCLK = SYSCLK ÷ AHB prescaler; it clocks the CPU, memory and DMA. PCLK1 and PCLK2 are the APB1 and APB2 bus clocks that peripherals use.
Prescaler
A divider. A prescaler of 4 turns 168 MHz into 42 MHz. In timers the prescaler register PSC divides by PSC + 1, so PSC = 0 means "divide by 1".
f_TIM (timer kernel clock)
The clock that actually feeds a timer. On STM32 it is the bus clock, doubled if that bus's prescaler is not 1. This surprises almost everyone (card 07).
CNT (counter)
The timer's current count. It goes up by one on every tick of the prescaled clock.
ARR (auto-reload register)
The top value. When CNT reaches ARR, the next tick makes it wrap back to 0. The counter therefore has ARR + 1 states (0 to ARR), so one full cycle takes ARR + 1 ticks.
UEV (update event)
The moment the counter wraps. It can raise an interrupt (setting the flag UIF), start a DMA transfer, or trigger another peripheral such as the ADC.
CCR (capture/compare register)
A second value that is compared with CNT. In output mode it decides when an output pin changes level. In input (capture) mode, hardware copies CNT into CCR when an edge arrives on a pin, recording the exact time.
PWM (pulse-width modulation) and duty cycle
A square wave whose frequency is fixed but whose high time is adjustable. Duty cycle = high time ÷ period. A 25 % duty, 20 kHz PWM is high for 12.5 µs out of every 50 µs. Used to control motor speed, LED brightness and power converters.
Resolution
How finely you can set the duty cycle. If the period is ARR + 1 = 4 200 ticks, the duty can change in steps of 1/4 200, which is log2(4 200) ≈ 12 bits.
Preload / shadow register
A buffered copy. Software writes the preload; hardware copies it into the active shadow at the next update event, so a new value never takes effect halfway through a period.
SysTick
A simple 24-bit down-counting timer built into every Cortex-M core (it is Arm's, not ST's). Usually configured to interrupt every 1 ms to give the system a "tick".
Wait state
An extra CPU clock cycle spent waiting for flash memory, which is slower than the CPU. At 168 MHz and 3.3 V the F407 needs 5 wait states.
IWDG and WWDG
The two STM32 watchdogs. IWDG (Independent Watchdog) runs from LSI and resets the chip if not kicked in time. WWDG (Window Watchdog) runs from PCLK1 and also resets the chip if it is kicked too early.
Kick / feed / refresh
The write that restarts the watchdog countdown. On the IWDG, writing 0xAAAA to IWDG_KR.
Jitter, drift, accuracy
Accuracy: how far the average frequency is from nominal. Drift: how that changes with temperature and age. Jitter: how much individual edges wobble from one cycle to the next.

04One worked example, end to end

The task. A fan controller board has an 8 MHz crystal. We need: (a) the CPU at 168 MHz, (b) a 20 kHz PWM at 25 % duty on pin PA6 to drive the fan, (c) a 1 ms system tick for timeouts, and (d) a watchdog that resets the board about 1 s after the software hangs. We will compute every register value by hand.

Step 1: crystal to 168 MHz with the PLL

The F407 PLL has three rules (card 06): the PLL input after dividing by M must be 1–2 MHz, the VCO frequency after multiplying by N must be 100–432 MHz, and SYSCLK after dividing by P must be at most 168 MHz. A fourth output, divided by Q, must be exactly 48 MHz if USB is used.

336 MHz ÷ P=2  = 168 MHz      SYSCLK ✔
336 MHz ÷ Q=7  =  48 MHz      USB clock ✔

Step 3: the 20 kHz PWM on TIM3 channel 1

The PWM frequency is f_TIM ÷ ((PSC + 1) × (ARR + 1)). We want the best duty resolution, so we use no prescaling (PSC = 0) and put all the division into ARR:

(ARR + 1) = 84 000 000 ÷ 20 000 = 4 200   →  ARR = 4 199

RCC->APB1ENR |= RCC_APB1ENR_TIM3EN;      // 1. give the timer its clock (otherwise writes are ignored)
TIM3->PSC   = 0;                          // 2. no prescaling: 84 MHz counter clock
TIM3->ARR   = 4200 - 1;                   // 3. 4200 states → 20 kHz
TIM3->CCR1  = 1050;                       // 4. high for 1050 of them → 25 %
TIM3->CCMR1 = (6u << 4) | (1u << 3);       // 5. OC1M = 110 (PWM mode 1), OC1PE = 1 (preload)
TIM3->CCER  = 1u;                         // 6. CC1E: connect channel 1 to its pin
TIM3->CR1   = 1u << 7;                    // 7. ARPE: buffer ARR too
TIM3->EGR   = 1u;                         // 8. UG: copy the preloads into the active registers now
TIM3->CR1  |= 1u;                         // 9. CEN: start counting

Step 4: the 1 ms tick with SysTick

SysTick counts down from a value called LOAD to 0 and then reloads, so one period is LOAD + 1 clock cycles. It is clocked from HCLK = 168 MHz.

cycles per 1 ms = 168 000 000 × 0.001 = 168 000
LOAD            = 168 000 − 1 = 167 999
if you forget the −1: period = 168 001 cycles → 1/168 000 = 5.95 ppm slow → 0.51 s lost per day

Step 5: the watchdog with about 1 s timeout

The IWDG divides the LSI by a prescaler and counts a 12-bit reload value down to 0. Timeout = (4 × 2PR) × (RLR + 1) ÷ fLSI.

choose PR = 3  → divider 4 × 2³ = 32 → at 32 kHz one count = 1 ms
choose RLR = 999 → 1 000 counts → 1.0 s nominal
but LSI on the F407 can be 17–47 kHz:
  fastest: 32 × 1000 / 47 000 = 0.68 s       slowest: 32 × 1000 / 17 000 = 1.88 s
software kicks every 250 ms → 0.68 s / 0.25 s = 2.7× margin ✔

The kick must come from a place that proves the whole application is working, such as a supervisor task that checks all other tasks have reported in (card 22), never from a timer interrupt.

Step 6: check what the scope would show

  • PA6: 20.00 kHz square wave, 12.5 µs high, 37.5 µs low.
  • If you had computed ARR from 42 MHz instead of 84 MHz (forgetting the ×2 rule), ARR would be 2 099 and the scope would show 40 kHz. That exact bug is scenario 02.
  • If the main task hangs (or you sit at a breakpoint without freezing the watchdog), the board resets within 0.68–1.88 s and RCC_CSR shows the IWDG reset flag.

05How the pieces connect

  1. The oscillator choice sets the accuracy of everything. If SYSCLK comes from HSI, every timer, UART baud rate and SysTick tick inherits HSI's ±1 % (room temperature) to −8 % (cold) error. That is fine for blinking an LED and fatal for CAN or USB (card 24).
  2. The PLL and prescalers set the frequencies. Change APB1 from ÷4 to ÷2 and every APB1 timer, UART and I2C timing changes. Code must compute periods from the real clock, not from a constant someone typed in.
  3. Flash wait states must follow the CPU clock. They are not a timer feature, but the clock change is unsafe without them (card 08).
  4. Timers turn the clock into events. PSC and ARR set the period, CCR sets edges within the period, the update event drives interrupts, DMA and the ADC. Preload registers make runtime changes clean.
  5. Software time is built on a timer. The 1 ms SysTick interrupt increments a counter; every timeout in the system compares against it, which is why wraparound-safe comparisons matter (card 14).
  6. The watchdog sits outside all of this, on its own LSI clock, so that if the clock tree, the CPU or the software fails, it still resets the chip.
An STM32 clock starts at an oscillator: an RC for fast start-up or a crystal for accuracy. A PLL multiplies it to SYSCLK, and prescalers divide it into bus clocks. Each timer counts its own kernel clock through a prescaler up to ARR, which gives f = f_TIM/((PSC+1)(ARR+1)), and compares against CCR to make PWM edges. SysTick gives the OS tick, and an independent watchdog on LSI resets the chip if the software stops proving that every task is healthy.

BEGINNER

1. What is it?

A clock source is the circuit that produces the steady on/off signal the whole chip runs on. The STM32F407 has four of them, because no single oscillator is best at everything: fast internal ones start instantly but are inaccurate; external crystals are accurate but slow to start and need parts on the board; slow 32 kHz ones use almost no power and keep running in sleep.

HSI — High-Speed Internal
A 16 MHz RC oscillator on the die. The chip always boots from it, because it is running within about 2 µs of power-up.
HSE — High-Speed External
A crystal (4–26 MHz) connected to pins OSC_IN/OSC_OUT, or an external clock signal fed in "bypass" mode (up to 50 MHz). The accurate source for the PLL.
LSI — Low-Speed Internal
An RC oscillator nominally 32 kHz (actually anywhere from 17 to 47 kHz). Clocks the independent watchdog.
LSE — Low-Speed External
A 32.768 kHz watch crystal. Clocks the real-time clock (RTC) calendar.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Power-up. The F407 starts HSI automatically. After about 2 µs RCC_CR.HSIRDY = 1 and the CPU runs your reset handler at 16 MHz.
  2. Software starts HSE by setting RCC_CR.HSEON. The on-chip amplifier drives the crystal; the vibration builds up over a few thousand cycles (~2 ms).
  3. Hardware counts a fixed number of stable cycles, then sets RCC_CR.HSERDY. Only now is it safe to use HSE as the PLL input.
  4. Why an RC drifts: its frequency is f ≈ 1/(k·R·C). On-chip resistors change by thousands of ppm per °C, so the frequency follows temperature and supply voltage. A factory trim (HSITRIM) corrects it only at 25 °C.
  5. Why a crystal does not: quartz is a mechanical resonator with a quality factor (Q) of 104–106: it only resonates strongly in a very narrow band set by its cut and size, so the electronics barely affect it.

3. Worked example: what ppm means in real time

1 ppm = 10−6 = 0.0001 %. 1 % = 10 000 ppm. Seconds lost per day = ppm × 86 400 × 10−6 = ppm × 0.0864.

HSI at −40 °C:      −8 % = −80 000 ppm → 16 MHz × 0.92 = 14.72 MHz → 6 912 s/day (almost 2 hours)
Why 32 768 Hz?      32 768 = 2^15 → a 15-stage binary divider gives exactly 1 Hz

   initial ±20 ppm + temperature (−0.034 ppm/°C² × (−40−25)² = −0.034 × 4225 = −144 ppm)
   + ageing ±3 ppm/year + load-capacitance error

Load capacitors. A crystal is specified at a load capacitance CL. With two caps to ground, CL = C1·C2/(C1+C2) + Cstray. For C1 = C2 this becomes C1 = 2·(CL − Cstray). For CL = 12.5 pF and 5 pF of stray (pins + traces): C1 = 2 × (12.5 − 5) = 15 pF each.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …the load caps are wrong (e.g. 22 pF fitted where 15 pF was needed): the crystal runs tens of ppm off-frequency. An RTC drifts a minute a month; a USB device is fine, but timestamps never match the server.
  • …the oscillator gain margin is too low (ST AN2867): the crystal may not start at cold temperature. Units hang on boot in the winter at while(!HSERDY), then work when warmed.
  • …you run CAN or USB from HSI: works on the bench at 25 °C, fails in the cold (card 24).
"A crystal is exact." No. It is accurate to tens of ppm, and only within its temperature and load-capacitance specification. A 20 ppm RTC loses almost a minute a month.

6. Say it like this

The F407 has four clock sources: HSI, a 16 MHz RC that starts in microseconds but is only ±1 % at room temperature and up to −8 % cold; HSE, a 4–26 MHz crystal accurate to tens of ppm; and two 32 kHz sources, LSI for the watchdog and LSE for the RTC. It boots on HSI and switches to HSE plus the PLL when accuracy matters. 20 ppm is about 1.7 seconds a day.

1. What is it?

The clock tree is the set of multiplexers (switches that pick one of several inputs) and dividers that take one chosen oscillator and deliver a suitable clock to every block of the chip. It exists because the CPU wants to run as fast as possible, but many peripherals cannot, and running slow parts slowly saves power.

HSI 16M ─┐
         ├─[SW mux]─ SYSCLK ≤168 ─[/HPRE]─ HCLK ≤168 ─┬─ Cortex-M4, SRAM, flash, DMA, GPIO (AHB1)
HSE ─────┤                                          ├─ SysTick (HCLK or HCLK/8)
PLL ─────┘                                          ├─[/PPRE1]─ PCLK1 ≤42 ─ APB1 periph ─ (x1|x2) TIM2-7,12-14
  └─ /Q ─ 48 MHz (USB OTG FS, SDIO, RNG)            └─[/PPRE2]─ PCLK2 ≤84 ─ APB2 periph ─ (x1|x2) TIM1,8,9-11

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Pick SYSCLK. RCC_CFGR.SW[1:0] chooses 00 = HSI, 01 = HSE, 10 = PLL. Read SWS (switch status) to confirm the switch has actually happened.
  2. Divide for the core. HCLK = SYSCLK / HPRE (1, 2, 4 … 512). HCLK clocks the CPU, the bus matrix, memories, DMA and GPIO. AHB = Advanced High-performance Bus.
  3. Divide for the slow peripheral bus. PCLK1 = HCLK / PPRE1 (1, 2, 4, 8, 16), max 42 MHz. APB1 carries USART2/3, UART4/5, I2C1–3, SPI2/3, CAN1/2, DAC and TIM2–7, TIM12–14.
  4. Divide for the fast peripheral bus. PCLK2 = HCLK / PPRE2, max 84 MHz. APB2 carries USART1/6, SPI1, ADC1–3, SDIO and TIM1, TIM8, TIM9–11.
  5. Gate each peripheral. Every peripheral also has its own enable bit in RCC_AHB1ENR, RCC_APB1ENR or RCC_APB2ENR. If that bit is 0, the block has no clock: writes to its registers are lost and reads return 0.

3. Worked example

SYSCLK = 168 MHz (PLL)
HPRE  = /1  → HCLK  = 168 MHz
PPRE1 = /4  → PCLK1 = 168/4 = 42 MHz  (exactly at the 42 MHz limit)
PPRE2 = /2  → PCLK2 = 168/2 = 84 MHz  (exactly at the 84 MHz limit)
USART2 (APB1) at 115200 baud: USARTDIV = 42 000 000 / (16 × 115 200) = 22.786
USART1 (APB2) at 115200 baud: USARTDIV = 84 000 000 / (16 × 115 200) = 45.573

Same baud rate, different divider, because the two UARTs sit on different buses. A driver that hard-codes "PCLK = 42 MHz" works for USART2 and is exactly 2× wrong for USART1.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you forget the peripheral clock enable: every register reads 0x00000000 in the debugger, writes "do nothing", and there is no fault or error.
  • …PCLK1 exceeds 42 MHz (e.g. APB1 /2 at 168 MHz = 84 MHz): out of specification. It might even work on the bench, then fail at temperature.
  • …you assume a peripheral runs at the CPU clock: every baud rate, I2C timing and timer period is wrong by the bus divider.
The APB buses have lower maximum frequencies because they connect many slow peripherals over long routes on the die. Running them slower makes timing closure easier and saves dynamic power (P ∝ C·V²·f). The bus bridges handle synchronisation, which is why an APB register access takes a few extra cycles.
"All peripherals run at the CPU clock." No. Every baud rate, I2C timing and timer period is calculated from its own bus clock, and the timers have the additional ×2 rule (card 07).

6. Say it like this

SYSCLK is selected from HSI, HSE or the PLL, then divided into HCLK for the core and memories, and into PCLK1 and PCLK2 for the two APB buses, at most 42 and 84 MHz on the F407. Each peripheral computes its timing from its own bus clock and must have its RCC enable bit set first.

1. What is it?

A general-purpose timer is a hardware counter that counts ticks of a clock, wraps around at a value you choose, and tells you (interrupt, DMA, trigger) each time it wraps. Three registers do all the work: PSC (prescaler) slows the clock down, CNT (counter) holds the current count, and ARR (auto-reload register) is the value at which it wraps. The wrap is called the update event (UEV).

CK_INT ──►[ PSC (16-bit) ]──► CK_CNT ──►[ CNT ]──► compare with ARR ──► UEV (update event)
 (timer       divides by                 counts                        ├─► UIF flag / IRQ (UIE)
  kernel      PSC+1                      0..ARR                        ├─► DMA request (UDE)
  clock)                                                               └─► TRGO to other timers / ADC
                                           └──► compare with CCRx ──► OCxREF / CCxIF

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. The timer kernel clock fTIM (84 MHz for TIM2–7 at the standard F407 setup) enters the prescaler.
  2. The prescaler has its own internal counter that counts 0…PSC. Each time it wraps, it emits one tick of CK_CNT. So fCK_CNT = fTIM / (PSC + 1).
  3. Each CK_CNT tick adds 1 to CNT: 0, 1, 2, … ARR.
  4. On the tick after CNT = ARR, CNT goes back to 0 and the update event happens. Counting 0 as a state, the counter visits ARR + 1 values per cycle.
  5. At the UEV the hardware sets the UIF flag in SR. If DIER.UIE = 1, the NVIC (Nested Vectored Interrupt Controller) raises the timer interrupt.
  6. Your ISR clears UIF and does its work. Meanwhile the counter has already started the next cycle on its own: the period does not depend on how quickly the ISR runs.
f_CK_CNT = f_TIM / (PSC + 1)
f_UEV    = f_TIM / ((PSC + 1) · (ARR + 1))          // edge-aligned (up or down)
f_UEV    = f_TIM / ((PSC + 1) · 2 · ARR)            // center-aligned: one PWM period

3. Worked example: a 1 kHz interrupt on TIM6

f_TIM = 84 MHz (TIM6 is on APB1, PCLK1 = 42 MHz, APB1 prescaler 4 ≠ 1 → ×2)
want a 1 µs tick:      PSC + 1 = 84 000 000 / 1 000 000 = 84      → PSC = 83
want 1 kHz updates:    ARR + 1 = 1 000 000 / 1 000      = 1 000   → ARR = 999
check: 84 000 000 / (84 × 1 000) = 1 000 Hz ✔
CNT goes 0, 1, … 999 (1 000 states × 1 µs = 1 ms), then UEV.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you write PSC = 84, ARR = 1000 (forgetting N−1): period = 85 × 1001 = 85 085 ticks of 11.9 ns = 1.0129 ms. The "1 ms" tick is 1.3 % slow: 18.7 minutes lost per day.
  • …you skip UG: the very first period is 84× too short (PSC still 0), after which it is correct. Easy to miss, visible as one early interrupt.
  • …you clear the flag at the end of the ISR: the write may still be in the bus write buffer when the exception returns, so the NVIC sees the request still asserted and runs the handler again. The ISR runs twice per period; the tick counter goes up 2 ms per ms. Clear early, or read back / __DSB() before returning.
"Setting PSC changes the rate immediately." No. PSC is always preloaded. The new value takes effect at the next update event, so set UG after initialisation.

6. Say it like this

A timer's prescaler divides the kernel clock by PSC+1, the counter counts from 0 to ARR and wraps, so the update rate is f_TIM/((PSC+1)(ARR+1)). Both registers hold N−1 because zero counts as a state. For a 1 kHz tick from 84 MHz I'd use PSC = 83 and ARR = 999, set UG once so the prescaler loads, and clear UIF at the top of the ISR.

1. What is it?

PWM (pulse-width modulation) is a square wave with a fixed period whose high time you can change. Because a motor, a heater or an LED responds to the average, switching fully on and off quickly lets you deliver any fraction of full power with almost no loss in the switch. The fraction is the duty cycle. A timer produces PWM in hardware by comparing CNT with a compare register CCR on every tick.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. CNT counts 0 … ARR, as in card 03. This sets the PWM period.
  2. On every tick the channel compares CNT with CCRx and produces an internal signal OCxREF (output compare reference).
  3. In PWM mode 1 (OCxM = 110 in CCMRx), counting up, OCxREF = 1 while CNT < CCR and 0 otherwise. In PWM mode 2 (111) it is the inverse: 1 while CNT ≥ CCR.
  4. The polarity bit CCxP in CCER optionally inverts OCxREF on its way to the pin (0 = active high, 1 = active low). CCxE connects the channel to the pin at all.
  5. The GPIO must be in alternate-function mode with the right AF number (for example TIM3_CH1 on PA6 is AF2), or the pin never sees the signal.
  6. With OCxPE = 1 (preload), a new CCR value waits for the next update event, so each period has one consistent duty.
duty (edge-aligned, mode 1, up) = CCR / (ARR + 1)
CCR = 0 → 0 %       CCR ≥ ARR + 1 → 100 %
resolution = log2(ARR + 1) bits       with   f_PWM · (ARR + 1) · (PSC + 1) = f_TIM

3. Worked example

TIM3, f_TIM = 84 MHz, target 20 kHz, 25 %:
   ARR + 1 = 84 000 000 / 20 000 = 4 200 → ARR = 4 199, PSC = 0
   CCR1    = 0.25 × 4 200 = 1 050          → high 1 050 × 11.9 ns = 12.5 µs of 50 µs
   resolution log2(4 200) = 12.04 bits

The frequency × resolution trade-off (f_TIM = 168 MHz, TIM1):
    20 kHz → ARR+1 = 8 400 → 13.0 bits
   100 kHz → ARR+1 = 1 680 → 10.7 bits
   656 kHz → ARR+1 =   256 →  8.0 bits

Why PSC = 0? Suppose you chose PSC = 1 (÷2) and ARR = 2 099 for the same 20 kHz: you now have only 2 100 steps (11.0 bits). Every increment of PSC throws away resolution, so use the smallest PSC that keeps ARR within 16 bits.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …the pin is not in AF mode / wrong AF: CNT counts and CC1IF sets in the debugger, but the pin is flat.
  • …it is TIM1 or TIM8 and MOE is 0: advanced timers have a master output enable in BDTR. Same as above: everything counts, no output. This is the most common TIM1 bring-up bug.
  • …CCR > ARR: the compare never matches. Output stuck at 100 % (mode 1) and the CC interrupt never fires.
  • …OCxPE = 0 and you write CCR mid-period: one runt or stretched pulse; a servo twitches.
"PWM on TIM1 does not work, so the pin must be broken." TIM1 and TIM8 are advanced timers: no output appears until BDTR.MOE = 1.

6. Say it like this

In PWM mode 1, counting up, the output is active while CNT is below CCR, so the duty is CCR/(ARR+1) and the frequency is f_TIM/((PSC+1)(ARR+1)). Resolution is log2 of the number of counter states, so I keep PSC as small as possible. I always enable CCR preload, set UG once, configure the pin's alternate function and, on TIM1/TIM8, set MOE.

1. What is it?

SysTick is a small timer built into the Arm Cortex-M core itself, not added by ST, so it is the same on every Cortex-M3/M4/M7 (optional on M0/M0+). It is a 24-bit down-counter at address 0xE000E010, designed to produce the regular "tick" interrupt an operating system or a HAL_Delay() needs. Because it is part of the core, portable code can rely on it.

CTRL 0xE000E010ENABLE b0 · TICKINT b1 · CLKSOURCE b2 (1 = processor clock, 0 = external reference, which is HCLK/8 on STM32) · COUNTFLAG b16 (cleared by a read)
LOAD 0xE000E01424-bit reload value. Period = LOAD + 1 clocks.
VAL 0xE000E018Current value. Any write clears it to 0 and clears COUNTFLAG.
CALIB 0xE000E01CTENMS value supplied by the vendor (often not useful)

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. You write LOAD, clear VAL, then enable with CTRL.
  2. Because VAL is 0, the next clock reloads it from LOAD.
  3. Each clock subtracts 1: LOAD, LOAD−1, …, 1, 0.
  4. On the clock after 0 it reloads from LOAD, sets COUNTFLAG and, if TICKINT = 1, pends the SysTick exception.
  5. So one period visits LOAD+1 values: LOAD … 0. That is why LOAD must be N−1 for an N-cycle period.
  6. The reload is done by hardware, so ISR latency never adds up into drift.

3. Worked example

1 ms at 168 MHz:  N = 168 000 000 × 0.001 = 168 000 cycles → LOAD = 167 999
maximum period:   2^24 = 16 777 216 cycles / 168 MHz = 99.86 ms
                  with HCLK/8 = 21 MHz:  16 777 216 / 21 MHz = 798.9 ms
bug LOAD = 168 000: period 168 001 cycles → error 1/168 000 = 5.95 ppm
                  5.95 × 0.0864 = 0.51 s per day slow
same bug at 16 MHz HSI with 1 kHz tick: 1/16 000 = 62.5 ppm = 5.4 s/day

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …LOAD = N instead of N−1: every unit drifts slow by the same 0.5 s/day at every temperature (scenario 01).
  • …you call SysTick_Config(SystemCoreClock/1000 - 1): the −1 is applied twice; 1 cycle short.
  • …code calling HAL_Delay() runs inside an ISR whose priority is equal to or higher than SysTick (set in SCB->SHPR3): the tick can never increment. The system freezes inside HAL_Delay forever.
  • …the device enters Stop mode: HCLK stops, SysTick stops. Time "disappears" during sleep (card 20).
"SysTick_Config(SystemCoreClock/1000 - 1) is safer." No. That applies the −1 twice. Know which API subtracts it for you.

6. Say it like this

SysTick is the Cortex-M core's 24-bit down-counter. It counts LOAD down to zero and reloads, so the period is LOAD+1 cycles, and for 1 ms at 168 MHz LOAD is 167 999. CMSIS SysTick_Config subtracts the one for you. The maximum period is about 100 ms at 168 MHz, and it stops in Stop mode because HCLK stops.

1. What is it?

The main PLL (phase-locked loop) turns a modest input (8 MHz HSE, or 16 MHz HSI) into the 168 MHz system clock and the 48 MHz USB clock. Inside it is a VCO (voltage-controlled oscillator), a fast oscillator whose frequency is steered by a control voltage. A phase detector keeps comparing "VCO ÷ N" with "input ÷ M" and nudges the voltage until they match exactly, so the VCO runs at exactly N/M times the input, with the input's accuracy.

f_VCO_in  = f_PLLin / M        2 ≤ M ≤ 63      must be 1–2 MHz (2 MHz recommended: lower jitter)
f_VCO_out = f_VCO_in × N       50 ≤ N ≤ 432    must be 100–432 MHz
SYSCLK    = f_VCO_out / P      P ∈ {2,4,6,8}   ≤ 168 MHz (F405/407)
f_48      = f_VCO_out / Q      2 ≤ Q ≤ 15      must be exactly 48 MHz for USB OTG FS; ≤ 48 for SDIO/RNG

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Divide the input by M to get the phase-detector comparison frequency. It must be 1–2 MHz; ST recommends 2 MHz because the loop then corrects more often and jitter is lower.
  2. Multiply by N: the VCO settles at fVCO_in × N. The VCO only works between 100 and 432 MHz.
  3. Divide by P (2, 4, 6 or 8) for SYSCLK, at most 168 MHz on the F407.
  4. Divide by Q for the 48 MHz clock (USB, SDIO, RNG). P and Q both divide the same VCO, so you need one VCO frequency that divides into both.
  5. Wait for RCC_CR.PLLRDY (lock), set flash latency, and only then switch SW to PLL.

  • …fVCO_in is outside 1–2 MHz (e.g. M = 2 with 8 MHz → 4 MHz): the PLL may lock on the bench and lose lock or jitter heavily on some parts or temperatures.
  • …Q gives 50.4 MHz (e.g. VCO 352.8 / 7): USB enumerates sometimes, fails with CRC errors other times.

  • …you forget SystemCoreClockUpdate(): every HAL delay and baud rate computed from 16 MHz; UART garbage at exactly 10.5× the baud.

The F4 PLL divides the input by M to 1–2 MHz, multiplies by N into a 100–432 MHz VCO, and divides by P for SYSCLK and by Q for the 48 MHz USB clock. Since P and Q share one VCO, I pick 336 MHz, which is 2 × 168 and 7 × 48: with an 8 MHz crystal that is M = 4, N = 168, P = 2, Q = 7. Then flash latency first, then the switch.

1. What is it?

On STM32, the clock that feeds a timer (TIMxCLK) is not always the clock of the bus it sits on. If that bus's prescaler is 1, the timer gets PCLK. If the prescaler is anything else, the timer gets 2 × PCLK. It exists so that timers can count fast (fine PWM resolution) even when the bus must be slow.

if (APBx prescaler == 1)  TIMxCLK = PCLKx
else                      TIMxCLK = 2 × PCLKx

F407 @168 MHz, APB1 /4, APB2 /2:
  PCLK1 = 42 MHz  →  TIM2,3,4,5,6,7,12,13,14 run at 84 MHz
  PCLK2 = 84 MHz  →  TIM1,8,9,10,11          run at 168 MHz

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. HCLK (168 MHz) enters the APB1 prescaler, which divides by 4.
  2. The RCC takes the timer clock from one divider stage earlier (effectively HCLK/2 = 84 MHz) whenever the division is 2 or more.
  3. Register reads and writes still go over the 42 MHz bus; only the counting logic runs at 84 MHz.
  4. If the prescaler is 1, there is no earlier stage, so the timer clock equals PCLK.

3. Worked example

Setup A (F407, 168 MHz):  APB1 /4 → PCLK1 = 42 → TIM3 = 84 MHz
   wanted 20 kHz:  ARR+1 = 84 000 000 / 20 000 = 4 200 ✔
   buggy  (used 42 MHz): ARR+1 = 2 100 → actual 84 MHz / 2 100 = 40 kHz ✖

Setup B (low-power, HCLK 16 MHz):  APB1 /1 → PCLK1 = 16 → TIM3 = 16 MHz (×1)
Setup C (HCLK 84 MHz):             APB1 /2 → PCLK1 = 42 → TIM3 = 84 MHz (×2)
   same PCLK1 as setup A, same timer clock: so the rule follows the prescaler, not PCLK.

  • …you compute periods from PCLK: every timer frequency is exactly 2× high, every timer period exactly half; duty cycle is correct. UART and SysTick look fine, which makes it confusing (scenario 02).
  • …someone later changes APB1 from /4 to /1 at a lower clock for power saving: a hard-coded "84 MHz" constant is now wrong by 2× in the other direction.

6. Say it like this

On STM32, if an APB prescaler is 1 the timers on that bus run at PCLK, otherwise at twice PCLK. So on an F407 at 168 MHz the APB1 timers run at 84 MHz and the APB2 timers at 168 MHz. HAL's PCLK function returns the bus clock, so I compute the timer clock with a helper that reads the prescaler, otherwise the PWM comes out at double frequency.

1. What is it?

Flash memory, where your program lives, needs a fixed time (roughly 30–35 ns on the F407) to deliver data after it receives an address. A CPU at 168 MHz wants a new word every 5.95 ns. A wait state is an extra clock cycle during which the CPU waits for the flash. You must tell the flash controller how many to insert, in FLASH_ACR.LATENCY.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. The CPU puts an instruction address on the bus.
  2. The flash controller starts a read and holds the bus for LATENCY extra cycles.
  3. After LATENCY + 1 cycles the data is valid and the fetch completes.
  4. The ART accelerator (ST's flash cache) reads 128 bits (4–8 Thumb instructions) per access, prefetches the next line, and caches 64 instruction lines and 8 data lines. Straight-line code then runs close to zero-wait speed; branches and constant (literal-pool) loads that miss the cache pay the full latency.
  5. Lower supply voltage makes the flash slower, so the table depends on VDD.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …the order is wrong or LATENCY is too low: the CPU latches half-read flash data as instructions. Random HardFaults or wrong values, at addresses that move whenever you change the code or add a printf; often only at full speed, only on some boards, only when warm. (Scenario 09.)
  • …LATENCY is too high: perfectly safe, just slower.
  • …VOS is not scale 1 above 144 MHz: the core logic itself is under-volted; similar random failures.
Code executed from SRAM has no wait states, which is why time-critical ISRs or flash-programming routines are sometimes placed in RAM or in the 64 KB CCM (core-coupled memory).

6. Say it like this

Flash access takes about 30 ns, so at 168 MHz and 3.3 V the F407 needs 5 wait states, and more at lower voltage. The ART accelerator hides most of that for sequential code. When I raise the clock I set LATENCY first and read it back, then switch; when lowering I switch first. The wrong order gives random, moving HardFaults.

1. What is it?

ARR, PSC and CCRx (and RCR on advanced timers) each exist twice. The preload register is the one software writes. The shadow (active) register is the one the counter is actually compared against. With preload enabled, a write sits in the preload until the next update event, then everything is copied at once. This guarantees that a period is never cut halfway by a register change.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  3. CNT reaches 999, wraps: UEV.

  5. The next period is 401 ticks long with the new CCR. No partial period ever existed.

3. Worked example: the ARR glitch (ARPE = 0)

Software writes ARR = 400 with ARPE = 0 → takes effect immediately.
CNT continues 601, 602 … it is already past 400, so CNT == ARR never happens.
CNT runs to 0xFFFF (65 535), wraps to 0, and only then counts up to 400.
Extra time ≈ (65 536 − 600) ticks ≈ 65 536 / 21 MHz = 3.12 ms with the output stuck.
32-bit TIM2 at 84 MHz: 2^32 / 84 MHz = 51 seconds stuck.

6. Say it like this

1. What is it?

A timer can count in three ways. Up: 0 → ARR, wrap. Down: ARR → 0, reload. Center-aligned (also called up/down): 0 → ARR → 0, like a triangle. The mode changes the period formula and, for PWM, where in the period the pulse sits. Center-aligned exists for motors and power converters.

UP        /|/|/|          period ARR+1, UEV at overflow
DOWN      |\|\|\          period ARR+1, UEV at underflow
CENTER    /\/\/\          period 2·ARR, UEV at peak AND valley
PWM (center, CCR = ARR/2):    __‾‾‾‾__     pulse symmetric about the valley

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. CR1.DIR = 0 up, 1 down (only when CMS = 00).
  2. CR1.CMS = 01, 10 or 11 selects center-aligned. The counter goes 0, 1 … ARR−1, ARR, ARR−1 … 1, 0: that is 2·ARR ticks. DIR becomes read-only and shows the current direction.
  3. The three CMS values only select when the compare interrupt flags are set: 01 while counting down, 10 while counting up, 11 both.
  4. The update event happens at both the peak and the valley. On TIM1/TIM8 the repetition counter RCR filters it: RCR = 1 gives one update per PWM period.
  5. In PWM mode 1 the output is active while CNT < CCR on both slopes, so the pulse is centred on the valley.

CCR = 1 050 → duty = 1 050 / 4 200 = 25 %, high from CNT 1 050 down … valley … up to 1 050
   = 2 × 1 050 ticks = 2 100 × 5.95 ns = 12.5 µs of a 50 µs period ✔

5. Why center-aligned for motors, and what goes wrong if…

  • With three phases, edge-aligned PWM switches all three high at the same instant (CNT = 0): one big current step, more EMI and DC-bus ripple. Center-aligned spreads the edges, because each phase's edges depend on its own CCR.
  • At the counter valley (or peak, depending on topology) all three low-side switches are on: the quietest moment to sample shunt currents with the ADC.
  • …you use the edge formula in center mode: PWM comes out at half the expected frequency.
  • …you forget RCR on TIM1: the update ISR runs twice per period and your control loop runs at 40 kHz instead of 20.
"Center-aligned is just the same PWM shifted in time." The period formula changes (2·ARR rather than ARR+1), the duty formula changes (CCR/ARR), update events happen twice per period, and the resolution halves.

6. Say it like this

Up and down counting give a period of ARR+1 ticks; center-aligned counts up then down, so the period is 2·ARR and there are two update events per period, filtered by the repetition counter on TIM1/TIM8. Center-aligned PWM is symmetric, spreads the phase edges, lowers EMI and gives a clean ADC trigger point at the valley, at the cost of one bit of resolution.

1. What is it?

Input capture is the timer's stopwatch. When a chosen edge arrives on a timer input pin, the hardware copies the current CNT into CCRx in the same clock cycle. The CPU reads it later, but the time stamp is already exact. It is how you measure pulse widths, periods and frequencies without interrupt latency in the measurement.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. The pin signal TIx passes a digital filter ICxF (must be stable for N samples) and an edge detector (CCxP/CCxNP: 00 rising, 01 falling, 11 both).
  2. An optional prescaler ICxPSC lets only every 1st/2nd/4th/8th edge through.
  3. On the selected edge, CNT → CCRx and CCxIF = 1 (interrupt if CCxIE).
  4. Reading CCRx clears CCxIF. If another capture arrives before you read it, CCxOF (over-capture) is set: you lost one.
  5. CCMRx.CCxS selects the source: 01 = own pin TIx, 10 = neighbouring pin, 11 = TRC (internal trigger).

3. Worked example: period of a 1 kHz signal with wrap-around

TIM4, f_TIM = 84 MHz, PSC = 83 → 1 µs ticks, ARR = 0xFFFF (free-running, wraps every 65.536 ms)
Edge 1 captured at CCR1 = 65 000.  Edge 2 captured at CCR1 = 464 (the counter wrapped).
(uint16_t)(464 − 65 000) = 464 + 65 536 − 65 000 = 1 000 ticks = 1 000 µs → 1 kHz ✔
Works for any period < 65 536 ticks, because 16-bit unsigned subtraction is modulo 65 536.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you use EXTI + read CNT in the ISR instead: measurements jitter by 12+ cycles of entry latency plus whatever higher-priority ISRs and flash wait states add (often µs).
  • …the signal is slower than one counter wrap: results alias: a 15 Hz signal (66.7 ms) with 1 µs ticks reads as 1.1 ms. Raise PSC or extend with overflow counting.
  • …noisy input, no filter: random very short periods. Use ICxF.
Hardware capture removes interrupt latency from the measurement. The CPU only needs to read the result before the next edge.

In input capture, the selected edge copies CNT into CCR in hardware, so the timestamp is exact regardless of ISR latency. Period is the modular difference of two captures in the counter's own width. PWM-input mode uses two channels on one pin with slave reset mode, giving the period in CCR1 and the high time in CCR2 with no CPU work.

1. What is it?

Output compare is input capture in reverse: instead of recording when an edge happened, you tell the timer when to do something. When CNT equals CCRx, the hardware sets a flag and can change the pin, all on the exact tick. One-pulse mode (OPM) makes the timer stop after a single cycle, so it can produce exactly one pulse after a trigger.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. CNT counts as usual.
  2. On the tick where CNT == CCRx, the channel applies its OCxM action to OCxREF and sets CCxIF.
  3. In toggle mode the pin flips at every match, so one full output period takes two counter cycles.
  4. Several rates from one free-running timer: leave ARR = 0xFFFF and, in each CCx interrupt, add that channel's interval to its CCR. Each channel then has its own exact schedule.

1. What is it?

A quadrature encoder on a motor shaft outputs two square waves, A and B, shifted 90° apart. Which one leads tells you the direction; the number of edges tells you how far it turned. Encoder mode makes the timer count those edges up or down in hardware, so CNT simply is the shaft position.

forward:   A  _‾‾__‾‾__        reverse:  A  _‾‾__‾‾__
           B  __‾‾__‾‾_                  B  ‾__‾‾__‾‾
           count +1 at every edge of A and B (×4)    count −1 at every edge

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Connect A to TIx_CH1 and B to CH2 (must be channels 1 and 2). Set CC1S = CC2S = 01 and a filter ICxF to reject bounce.
  2. Select SMCR.SMS = 001 (count on TI2 edges), 010 (TI1 edges) or 011 (both edges of both: ×4).
  3. At each edge the hardware looks at the level of the other channel to decide up or down, and updates CR1.DIR.
  4. CNT wraps at ARR in either direction. For angle within one revolution set ARR = 4·CPR − 1 (CPR = cycles, i.e. lines, per revolution). For velocity use ARR = 0xFFFF and differences.

  • …unsigned difference: a tiny reverse move shows as a huge forward jump; position control jerks violently.

1. What is it?

Software time is usually a 32-bit variable incremented every millisecond by SysTick. A uint32_t can hold at most 4 294 967 295, so after 232 ms = 49.7 days it wraps back to 0. Any code that compares times must keep working across that wrap, or the product fails after seven weeks of uptime, long after testing finished.

2^32 ms  = 49.71 days       2^32 µs = 71.6 min       2^32 cycles @168 MHz = 25.6 s (DWT_CYCCNT)
2^31 ms  = 24.86 days  ← the maximum timeout the signed-difference trick supports

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. C defines unsigned arithmetic modulo 2N. 3 − 5 as uint32_t is 4 294 967 294, not −2.
  2. So now − start always gives the true number of ticks between them, as long as less than one full wrap has passed, even if now has wrapped to a small number.
  3. Comparing absolute values (now >= start + timeout) fails because start + timeout can itself wrap to a small number.
  4. For deadlines, compute (int32_t)(now − deadline): a positive or zero result means "the deadline is in the past"; negative means "still in the future". This is valid while the deadline is less than 231 ticks away.

3. Worked example with real numbers

        at now = 4 294 967 100 (only 100 ms later):  now ≥ 704 → TRUE  ✖ fires 900 ms early

RIGHT:  now − start = 4 294 967 100 − 4 294 967 000 = 100   → 100 ≥ 1000? no ✔
        later now = 704 (wrapped):  704 − 4 294 967 000 mod 2^32 = 296 + 704 = 1 000 ✔ fires on time

The other failure: next = 4 294 961 000 is set, then now wraps to 1 520:
        now ≥ next is false for another 49.7 days (scenario 04).

1. What is it?

The RTC (real-time clock) keeps the calendar date and time, normally from the 32.768 kHz LSE crystal. On the F4 it lives in the backup domain: a small corner of the chip that can be powered from a coin cell on the VBAT pin when main power is off, and that is not reset by a normal system reset. So the time survives resets and power cuts.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Unlock. The backup domain is write-protected. Enable PWREN, set PWR_CR.DBP (disable backup protection), then write the RTC key sequence 0xCA, 0x53 to RTC_WPR.
  2. Start LSE. RCC_BDCR.LSEON, wait for LSERDY (can take up to 2 s), choose RTCSEL = 01 (LSE; 10 = LSI, 11 = HSE/RTCPRE) and set RTCEN.
  3. Divide to 1 Hz in two stages: an asynchronous 7-bit PREDIV_A and a synchronous 15-bit PREDIV_S.
  4. Each 1 Hz tick advances the calendar, stored in BCD (binary-coded decimal: 0x59 means 59) in RTC_TR and RTC_DR.
  5. Reading TR freezes the shadow DR until DR is read, so read TR first, then DR, and the pair is consistent.

   subseconds counter RTC_SSR counts 255 … 0 → resolution 1/256 s = 3.9 ms
   large PREDIV_A + small PREDIV_S saves power (the synchronous part runs slower)

Smooth calibration (RTC_CALR) masks pulses over a 32 s window: range −487.1 … +488.5 ppm,
   step 0.954 ppm → mask 12 / 0.954 ≈ 13 pulses (CALM = 13) → residual ≈ −0.4 ppm

1. What is it?

The IWDG (Independent Watchdog) is a 12-bit down-counter clocked from LSI. Software must write 0xAAAA to its key register before it reaches zero; if it does not, the IWDG resets the chip. "Independent" means it does not depend on HSE, the PLL or the APB buses, so it still works if the main clock fails, a peripheral clock is turned off, or the code hangs in a broken clock state.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Writing 0xCCCC to IWDG_KR starts it (and forces LSI on). From then on software cannot stop it; only a reset does.
  2. Writing 0x5555 unlocks IWDG_PR (prescaler, 3 bits: ÷4·2PR = ÷4 … ÷256) and IWDG_RLR (reload, 12 bits: 0–4095).
  3. The counter loads RLR and counts down once per prescaled LSI tick.
  4. Writing 0xAAAA (the kick) reloads RLR into the counter.
  5. If the counter reaches 0, the chip resets and RCC_CSR.IWDGRSTF is set so the next boot can see why.
  6. With the option byte WDG_SW = 0 (hardware watchdog), it starts automatically at reset before any code runs. On the F4 it keeps running in Stop and Standby. (L4/G0/G4 have IWDG_STOP/IWDG_STDBY option bits to freeze it; vendor- and family-specific.)

1. What is it?

The WWDG (Window Watchdog) resets the chip if it is refreshed too late or too early. An ordinary watchdog only catches code that stops. The window catches code that runs too fast: a control loop that skips most of its work, or a runaway loop that happens to call the refresh function. That is why safety standards (IEC 60730 class B, ISO 26262) like windowed watchdogs.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Clock: PCLK1 / 4096 / 2WDGTB. It is not independent: it stops if the APB1 clock stops (Stop mode, or a clock failure).
  2. A 7-bit down-counter T[6:0] in WWDG_CR, enabled by WDGA (b7). Like the IWDG, WDGA can only be cleared by a reset.
  3. Reset condition 1 (too late): the counter decrements from 0x40 to 0x3F, i.e. bit T6 becomes 0.
  4. Reset condition 2 (too early): software writes CR while the counter is still above the window value W[6:0] in WWDG_CFR.
  5. EWI (early wakeup interrupt, CFR b9): an interrupt when the counter reaches 0x40, exactly one tick before the reset. Use it to save diagnostics (which task, the PC, a stack snapshot to .noinit), not to refresh.
t_tick     = 4096 · 2^WDGTB / f_PCLK1         (42 MHz, WDGTB=3 → 780 µs)
t_reset    = t_tick · (T[5:0] + 1)             (T=0x7F → 64 ticks → 49.9 ms max)
window opens (refresh allowed) when counter ≤ W:   t_open = t_tick · (T − W)

3. Worked example

A 10 ms control loop that refreshes every cycle → refresh at 10 ms → 12 ticks gone → counter ≈ 115 > 95 → RESET.
So the loop must refresh every 3rd cycle (30 ms), or the window must be adjusted.

1. What is it?

A half-bridge is two power switches (MOSFETs or IGBTs) stacked between the DC supply and ground, with the motor phase connected to the middle. The top one (high side) and the bottom one (low side) must never be on together, or the supply is shorted through them: shoot-through. The advanced timers TIM1/TIM8 drive both from one channel: CHx and its complement CHxN, and insert a dead time (DT) during which both are off.

CHx     ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾\__________________/‾‾‾‾‾‾   rising edge delayed by DT
CHxN    _________|DT|‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾\_______    rising edge delayed by DT
                     both low for DT at every transition

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. The channel computes OCxREF from CNT and CCR as for normal PWM.
  2. The dead-time generator produces CHx = OCxREF with its rising edge delayed by DT, and CHxN = NOT OCxREF with its rising edge delayed by DT.
  3. Falling edges are not delayed, so at every transition one switch turns off first and the other turns on DT later.
  4. DT is counted in tDTS = tCK_INT × {1, 2, 4} (from CR1.CKD), derived from the timer kernel clock, not the prescaled counter clock. It does not change when you change PSC.
  5. MOE (main output enable, BDTR b15) gates all outputs. The break input BKIN clears MOE asynchronously, in hardware, within nanoseconds, sending all outputs to their idle states OISx/OISxN. No CPU or ISR is involved.

1. What is it?

STM32 timers can signal each other directly in hardware. A master timer outputs a trigger signal TRGO; a slave timer can use it to reset, start, gate or clock itself. The same TRGO (or a compare channel) can start an ADC conversion, and the update event can trigger DMA to rewrite the timer's own registers. Together these let hardware do precisely-timed work with zero CPU involvement.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Master: CR2.MMS selects what drives TRGO: 000 reset (UG), 001 enable (CEN), 010 update, 011 compare pulse, 100–111 OC1REF–OC4REF.
  2. Routing: the slave's SMCR.TS selects the trigger input: ITR0–ITR3 (other timers' TRGO), TI1F_ED, TI1FP1, TI2FP2, ETRF. The ITRx-to-timer mapping is device-specific (RM0090 table "TIMx internal trigger connection"). For TIM4 on the F4: ITR0 = TIM1, ITR1 = TIM2, ITR2 = TIM3, ITR3 = TIM8.
  3. Slave action: SMCR.SMS: 100 reset, 101 gated (count while trigger high), 110 trigger (start), 111 external clock mode 1 (each trigger edge is a count).
  4. ADC: ADC_CR2.EXTSEL chooses TIM1_CC1/2/3, TIM2_CC2/3/4/TRGO, TIM3_CC1/TRGO, TIM4_CC4, TIM5_CC1/2/3, TIM8_CC1/TRGO, etc. The sampling instant is then set by hardware with zero jitter.
  5. DMA burst: TIMx_DCR.DBA = first register offset (in 32-bit words from CR1), DBL = burst length − 1. With DIER.UDE, each UEV makes the DMA write N words to TIMx_DMAR, which the timer redirects to consecutive registers.

   table {arr0,rcr0,ccr0, arr1,rcr1,ccr1, …}: every period gets its own length and duty

1. What is it?

A periodic tick is an interrupt every 1 ms that advances the system time and lets the scheduler run. Tickless idle switches that interrupt off when nothing is due: the kernel programs a one-shot timer for the next deadline, sleeps, and on wake-up adds the elapsed ticks in one step. Tickless exists for battery products, where 1 000 wake-ups per second dominate the idle current.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. The idle task sees that the next task is due in, say, 340 ticks.
  2. It stops the tick, programs a low-power timer to fire in 340 ms (minus wake-up latency), and enters sleep.
  3. Either the timer fires, or some other interrupt wakes the core early.
  4. The kernel reads how much time actually passed and calls vTaskStepTick(n) to add n ticks at once.
  5. The periodic tick restarts until the next idle period.
  • FreeRTOS configUSE_TICKLESS_IDLE = 1 uses SysTick by default, but SysTick stops in Stop mode because HCLK stops. Deep-sleep tickless needs a low-power timer: LPTIM on L4/G0/F410/F413, or the RTC wake-up timer on the F407 (which has no LPTIM).

3. Worked example: why drift-free scheduling matters

Task: every 10 ms run control_step() which takes 1.2 ms.
vTaskDelay(10) after the work → period = 10 + 1.2 = 11.2 ms → 89.3 Hz instead of 100 Hz ✖
vTaskDelayUntil(&next, 10) → wake times 0, 10, 20, 30 … ms regardless of run time ✔
Also: vTaskDelay(10) waits 9–10 tick periods, because the first tick may arrive at any moment.
Tickless reload loss: losing 3 µs per sleep × 100 000 sleeps/day = 0.3 s/day drift.

1. What is it?

The CSS (clock security system) is a hardware monitor that notices if the HSE crystal stops, and rescues the system by switching to HSI. MCO (microcontroller clock output) puts an internal clock on a pin so you can measure it with a scope or frequency counter. One handles crystal failure; the other lets you verify the clocks are what you think.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Enable with RCC_CR.CSSON (b19) after HSE is ready.
  2. If HSE stops, hardware switches HSE off. If HSE was feeding SYSCLK, directly or via the PLL, SYSCLK switches to HSI and the PLL is disabled.
  3. RCC_CIR.CSSF is set and an NMI (non-maskable interrupt) is raised.
  4. The clock-failure event also drives the break input of TIM1/TIM8, so motor PWM goes to its safe state in hardware.
  5. The NMI handler must clear the flag by writing CSSC (b23), or the NMI fires again and again.
  6. The system now runs at 16 MHz instead of 168. Every UART BRR, timer PSC and delay loop is wrong: enter a defined degraded mode or reset.

1. What is it?

A watchdog proves that the system is working, not that the CPU is executing instructions. A timer ISR keeps running while every task is deadlocked, so kicking from it proves nothing. The correct strategy is aggregation: every important task reports "I completed a real cycle", and a single supervisor kicks the watchdog only when all of them have reported.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Each monitored task owns one bit in an alive mask.
  2. At the end of each real work cycle, the task sets its bit (a check-in).
  3. A supervisor task wakes every 250 ms, atomically reads and clears the mask.
  4. If all bits are set (and health checks like heap and stack pass), it kicks the IWDG.
  5. If any bit is missing, it records which one in a .noinit RAM variable (not cleared at reset) and deliberately does not kick. The IWDG resets the chip.
  6. At the next boot, code reads RCC_CSR and the record, logs "IWDG reset, LoggerTask missing", and counts consecutive watchdog resets; after N, it boots a safe/recovery image.

3. Worked example: timing

Tasks: Comms (loop ≤ 100 ms), Control (10 ms), Logger (blocks on queue, uses 200 ms timeout)
Supervisor period 250 ms → each task has checked in at least twice per window.
IWDG: PR = 3, RLR = 999 → 0.68–1.88 s → shortest 0.68 s vs 250 ms = 2.7× margin.
A deadlock at t = 0: last kick ≤ 250 ms ago → reset at most 0.25 + 1.88 ≈ 2.1 s later.

  • …kick from SysTick: units hang for days, the LED still blinks, RCC_CSR never shows IWDGRSTF (scenario 05).
  • …kick inside retry or busy loops: a hung retry loop keeps the watchdog happy.
  • …kick from the idle hook alone: idle runs whenever the high-priority tasks are blocked, including blocked forever.
  • …no reset reason recorded: the watchdog only turns a hang into a reboot loop with no evidence.

Safety products (ISO 26262, IEC 61508) add an external watchdog with an independent oscillator, often windowed or challenge-response (e.g. in an automotive system-basis chip such as the NXP FS65 or Infineon TLE9471), because an on-chip watchdog shares failure modes with the MCU it monitors.

"Our watchdog never fires, so the firmware is stable." If the kick comes from SysTick or a timer ISR, the watchdog cannot fire for task deadlocks.

6. Say it like this

I kick the watchdog from exactly one place, a supervisor task, and only when every critical task has checked in since the last window and basic health checks pass. Otherwise I record the missing tasks in .noinit RAM and let it reset. Kicking from a timer ISR proves only that interrupts run, so it hides deadlocks.

1. What is it?

When the debugger halts the CPU at a breakpoint, only the CPU stops. Peripherals keep running by default: the IWDG keeps counting and resets the chip, a UART keeps receiving and overruns, timers keep generating PWM. The DBGMCU (debug MCU) registers let you freeze selected peripherals whenever the core is halted.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. The debugger halts the core (breakpoint, step, pause).
  2. For every peripheral whose freeze bit is 1, the hardware stops its counter clock while the core is halted.
  3. When you resume, the frozen peripherals continue from where they stopped.
  4. Peripherals with freeze bit 0 kept running the whole time.

  • …watchdog not frozen: the board resets a fixed time after every halt; "the debugger draws too much power" theories appear (scenario 06).

A breakpoint halts only the CPU; the IWDG keeps counting and resets the chip one timeout later. 1. What is it?

Three different ways a clock can be "wrong". Accuracy: how far the mean frequency is from nominal (ppm or %). Drift (stability): how it changes with temperature, voltage and age. Jitter: short-term variation of individual edges (ps–ns from a PLL; µs from ISR latency). Communication links only work if the two ends' clocks agree within a tolerance budget; this card shows why UART tolerates a few percent, CAN a fraction of a percent, so CAN needs a crystal.

2. How does it work, step by step? (UART)

  1. The line idles high. The receiver detects the falling edge of the start bit; this edge is its only synchronisation point for the whole frame.
  2. With 16× oversampling it then samples each bit near its centre (samples 8, 9, 10 of 16) and takes the majority.
  3. For an 8N1 frame (start + 8 data + stop = 10 bits), the stop bit's centre is 9.5 bit-times after the start edge.
  4. If the two clocks differ by e, the sampling point slides by 9.5 × e bit-times by the end of the frame. It must stay inside the stop bit (less than about half a bit, minus the start-edge detection uncertainty).
  5. So the rough limit is ~0.4 / 9.5 ≈ 4 % total. ST specifies the exact total tolerance (both clocks plus baud quantisation):
F4 USARTONEBIT = 0ONEBIT = 1
OVER8 = 0, DIV fraction = 03.75 %4.375 %
OVER8 = 0, DIV fraction ≠ 03.41 %3.97 %
OVER8 = 1, DIV fraction = 02.50 %3.75 %
OVER8 = 1, DIV fraction ≠ 01.82 %3.03 %

CAN, step by step

  1. Each bit is divided into time quanta (tq): sync segment (1 tq), PS1, PS2. NBT = nominal bit time in tq.
  2. Nodes re-synchronise only on recessive-to-dominant edges. Bit stuffing guarantees one at least every 10 bits (5 identical bits then a stuff bit).
  3. Each re-sync can correct at most SJW (synchronisation jump width) tq.
  4. Around an error flag, up to 13 bits can pass with no edge. The Bosch / ISO 11898 conditions:
df ≤ min(PS1, PS2) / (2 · (13·NBT − PS2))
df ≤ SJW / (20 · NBT)                       NBT = bit time in time quanta (tq)

Software timing jitter: toggling a GPIO in an ISR adds 12 cycles of entry latency, plus flash wait states, plus whatever a higher-priority ISR or critical section adds (µs). An output-compare or PWM pin has zero software jitter. Generate precise edges in hardware and use ISRs only to change the parameters.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …CAN/UART tested only at 25 °C on HSI: passes every bench test; in a −30 °C cold store or +85 °C sealed box, CRC errors, framing errors (FE/NE flags), then bus-off or a dead link.
  • …OVER8 = 1 "for precision": it reduces tolerance (1.82–2.5 %).
  • …both nodes on RC oscillators: the budget is shared; each side gets only half.

The same reasoning applies to USB FS (48 MHz ±0.25 %) and Ethernet (25 MHz ±50 ppm).

"It works on the bench, so the HSI is fine for CAN/UART." The bench is at 25 °C, where HSI is ±1 %. The field is −30 °C in a cold-store or +85 °C in a sealed enclosure.

6. Say it like this

A UART resynchronises on every start bit, so clock error accumulates only over about 10 bits, and the F4 tolerates around 3.4–3.75 % total. CAN resynchronises only on edges that can be 10 to 13 bits apart and SJW limits each correction, so the Bosch formulas typically allow 0.3–1.5 % shared by both nodes. HSI's −8 to +4.5 % over temperature fails that, so CAN, USB and Ethernet need a crystal.
