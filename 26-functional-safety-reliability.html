1What problem does functional safety solve?

A modern car has 50 to 150 ECUs (Electronic Control Units: small computers, each built around a microcontroller). Some of them control things that can kill people: the steering assist motor, the brakes, the airbag, the throttle, the battery contactors of an electric car. Every ECU can go wrong in two very different ways:

  1. The hardware breaks at random. A transistor in the motor driver shorts after 6 years. A cosmic-ray neutron flips one bit in RAM. A crystal oscillator stops. Nobody did anything wrong: physics simply produces failures at some rate. You cannot prevent all of them, but you can predict how often they happen and detect them quickly.
  2. People made a mistake. A requirement forgot the case "driving in reverse". A developer wrote uint16 * uint16 and it overflowed. A compiler bug, a wrong calibration value. These are systematic: the same input always produces the same wrong result. You cannot put a probability on a bug, so you fight them with a disciplined process (reviews, coding rules, testing, coverage).

Functional safety is the engineering discipline that makes sure that, when either kind of failure happens, nobody is hurt with an unacceptable probability. The official definition in ISO 26262 (the automotive functional-safety standard, 12 parts, 2nd edition 2018) is: "absence of unreasonable risk due to hazards caused by malfunctioning behaviour of E/E systems". E/E = electrical and/or electronic.

Read that definition carefully, because interviewers do. It does not say "the system never fails". It says: when it fails, the risk must be acceptably low. Risk is a combination of how bad the harm is, how often the dangerous situation occurs, and whether the driver can still avoid the accident. This is why the whole method starts by rating those three things.

The one idea to keep: we accept that faults will happen. The job is (a) to make dangerous faults rare and (b) to detect every remaining dangerous fault and put the system into a safe state (for example "motor torque = 0") before the fault can turn into an accident. "Before" has a number: the FTTI, explained below.

2The hardware picture: what is inside a safety-relevant ECU?

Our running example is a column EPS (Electric Power Steering: an electric motor on the steering column that adds torque to help the driver turn the wheel). The diagram shows the real building blocks. Green labels are safety mechanisms: extra hardware or software whose only job is to detect or control faults.

Walk through the picture in the order the signals flow:

  1. The torque sensor measures how hard the driver twists the wheel. It has two output channels with opposite slopes: at 0 Nm T1 = 2.5 V and T2 = 2.5 V; at +5 Nm T1 = 3.5 V and T2 = 1.5 V. A healthy sensor always gives T1 + T2 ≈ 5.0 V. If one channel breaks (open wire, short to ground), the sum is no longer 5 V and software sees it. That is a plausibility check.
  2. The CAN bus brings the vehicle speed (assist is reduced at high speed). Because a gateway or a corrupted frame could deliver wrong data, the frame carries E2E protection (End-to-End: a CRC checksum, a rolling counter, a hidden Data ID and a receive timeout).
  3. The safety MCU computes the assist torque. Inside it, a second checker core repeats every instruction two clock cycles later and a comparator checks the results (lockstep). RAM and flash have ECC (Error-Correcting Code: extra check bits per word that correct a single flipped bit). An MPU (Memory Protection Unit) stops non-safety code from writing safety data. All alarms go to the SMU (Safety Management Unit, Infineon's name for the fault-collection block), which drives the FSP pin (Fault Signalling Protocol, an error output).
  4. The MCU sends PWM (Pulse-Width Modulation) signals to the gate driver, which switches six MOSFETs that drive the 3-phase motor. The motor current is measured and fed back so software can compare "current I commanded" with "current I see" (read-back).
  5. The SBC (System Basis Chip) is a separate chip that supplies the MCU and watches it: it monitors the supply voltages, has its own oscillator for a watchdog that the MCU must service inside a time window, and watches the FSP error pin. If anything is wrong, its SS1 (Safe State 1) output disables the gate driver directly. This path does not need the MCU to cooperate, which is the whole point: the thing being monitored cannot be the thing that saves you.
  6. The phase relay can physically disconnect the motor. Without it, a shorted MOSFET would let the spinning motor act as a generator and produce a braking torque the driver feels as heavy, jerky steering.
Notice the pattern: every important function has a second, independent path that can detect its failure, and the final reaction (cut the motor) can be executed by hardware that does not depend on the possibly-broken MCU. That is functional safety drawn as a block diagram.

3The key terms, defined one by one

Every term below is used throughout this page. Read them in order: each one builds on the previous ones.

Item
The vehicle-level function you are making safe, with its boundary, e.g. "electric power steering system: ECU + motor + sensor + wiring". ISO 26262 starts with an item definition.
Malfunctioning behaviour
What the item does wrong at its boundary: "assist torque in the wrong direction", "no assist", "too much assist".
Hazard
A potential source of harm caused by a malfunctioning behaviour: "unintended steering torque".
Operational situation
Where and how the car is used when the malfunction happens: "highway at 120 km/h", "parking at 5 km/h". The same malfunction is harmless in one situation and deadly in another.
Hazardous event
Hazard × operational situation: "unintended steering torque while driving on a highway".
HARA
Hazard Analysis and Risk Assessment: the table where every hazardous event is rated for Severity, Exposure and Controllability.
S · E · C
Severity S0–S3: how badly people are hurt. Exposure E0–E4: how often the car is in that situation. Controllability C0–C3: how many drivers could still avoid the accident (C3 = fewer than 90 %).
ASIL
Automotive Safety Integrity Level: QM, A, B, C or D, looked up from S, E and C. D is the strictest. QM = Quality Management, meaning "normal good engineering is enough, no special safety requirements".
Safety goal
The top-level safety requirement for a hazardous event, carrying its ASIL: "Prevent unintended steering torque greater than 3 Nm — ASIL D".
Safe state
A mode the system can go to and stay in without unreasonable risk: "assist off, motor disconnected". For column EPS this is safe because the driver still has a mechanical steering column.
FTTI
Fault Tolerant Time Interval: the time from the fault occurring until the accident would happen if nothing reacted. A property of the vehicle physics, e.g. 50 ms for the car to start leaving its lane. Your detection plus reaction must be shorter.
FDTI · FRTI
Fault Detection Time Interval (fault occurs → you know about it) and Fault Reaction Time Interval (you know → safe state reached). Rule: FDTI + FRTI ≤ FTTI.
Fault → error → failure
Fault: the root defect (a stuck bit, a bug). Error: wrong internal state it causes (a corrupted variable). Failure: the element stops doing its job at its boundary (wrong torque output).
Random vs systematic
Random hardware faults happen with a failure rate (physics). Systematic faults are mistakes in specification, design, code or tools. All software faults are systematic.
Safety mechanism
A technical measure that detects a fault or controls a failure to reach or keep a safe state: lockstep, ECC, watchdog, plausibility check, E2E.
DC
Diagnostic Coverage: the percentage of an element's failure rate that a safety mechanism detects. DC 99 % on a 100 FIT part leaves 1 FIT undetected.
FIT
Failures In Time: 1 FIT = 1 failure per 109 device-hours. The unit of hardware failure rate (λ, lambda).
SPF · RF · latent
Single-point fault: dangerous on its own and no mechanism covers it. Residual fault: the part a mechanism misses (λ × (1 − DC)). Latent fault: a hidden fault, usually inside a safety mechanism, that does nothing until a second fault arrives.
SPFM · LFM · PMHF
The three hardware numbers ISO 26262-5 demands: Single-Point Fault Metric (≥ 99 % for ASIL D), Latent Fault Metric (≥ 90 % for D), Probabilistic Metric for random Hardware Failures (< 10 FIT for D).
ASIL decomposition
Splitting one ASIL D requirement into two redundant, independent requirements of lower ASIL, e.g. B(D) + B(D). Only valid if the two cannot fail together.
FFI
Freedom From Interference: proof that low-ASIL software on the same MCU cannot corrupt the memory, steal the CPU time, or garble the messages of high-ASIL software.
Watchdog
An independent timer that forces a reset or a safe state unless software services it in time. A window watchdog also rejects service that comes too early.
Lockstep
Two identical cores run the same code; a comparator flags any difference within a clock cycle or two. Catches random hardware faults in the CPU, not software bugs.
ECC / SECDED
Error-Correcting Code / Single Error Correct, Double Error Detect: extra check bits per memory word (7 for 32 data bits) that correct one flipped bit and detect two.
MISRA C
A set of C coding rules that removes undefined and error-prone constructs. Required practice for safety software.
Safety case
The final written argument, with evidence, that the item is acceptably safe.

4How a fault becomes an accident, and where we break the chain

Every safety mechanism on this page sits somewhere on this chain. Learn the chain and you can place any mechanism an interviewer names.

  FAULT ──────────► ERROR ──────────► FAILURE ──────────► HAZARDOUS EVENT ──► HARM
  (root defect)     (wrong state)     (wrong output)      (in a situation)
  bit 2 of          brake_req         brake pressure      0.4 g braking at
  brake_req flips   = 0x04, not 0x00  is built up         110 km/h, car behind
       │                 │                  │
       │ ECC corrects    │ inverted copy    │ output read-back, SBC cuts the
       │ the bit         │ mismatch found,  │ actuator, driver can still steer
       │                 │ plausibility vs  │
       │                 │ pedal sensor     │
       ▼                 ▼                  ▼
  stopped here = best   stopped here = OK   last chance, must be inside the FTTI
  1. Prevent the fault where you can: derate components, use proven parts, follow MISRA, review requirements. This lowers the failure rate but never to zero.
  2. Detect the error: compare redundant copies, check ranges, check sequence counters, compare two cores. Detection must happen within the FDTI.
  3. React: move to the safe state within the FRTI. The reaction path must be independent of the fault (that is why the SBC, not the MCU, cuts the motor when the MCU itself may be broken).
  4. Make it rare enough: whatever slips through all mechanisms (the residual faults) must add up to less than the PMHF target, e.g. 10 FIT for ASIL D.
  5. Test the testers: a broken safety mechanism is a latent fault. Self-tests at startup (or periodically) prove each mechanism still works. That is what the LFM metric measures.

5One end-to-end worked example: EPS, from hazard to FIT budget

This is the whole ISO 26262 flow on one real-looking example. The numbers are illustrative but realistic; OEMs set their own values.

STEP 1 — RATE THE HAZARDOUS EVENT (HARA)

Hazardous event HE-01: "EPS applies unintended steering torque while driving on a highway at 120 km/h."

  • Severity S3: a car pulled into the next lane at 120 km/h can cause a fatal crash.
  • Exposure E4: highway driving is more than 10 % of the operating time of a typical car.
  • Controllability C3: a sudden torque of several Nm at that speed: driving studies show fewer than 90 % of drivers would hold the lane.
ISO 26262-3 Table 4:  S3, E4, C3  →  ASIL D
Self-check rule:      S + E + C = 3 + 4 + 3 = 10  →  10 = D, 9 = C, 8 = B, 7 = A, ≤6 = QM

Compare HE-02 "loss of assist while parking": S1 (low speed, light injury at worst), E4, C2 (the driver can still steer with more effort) → 1 + 4 + 2 = 7 → ASIL A. The same ECU, a different malfunction, a very different ASIL. Try both in the ASIL Tool tab.

STEP 2 — WRITE THE SAFETY GOAL

SG-01  Prevent unintended steering torque > 3 Nm.          ASIL D
       Safe state: assist off (motor torque 0, motor disconnected).
       FTTI: 50 ms (from vehicle tests: after 50 ms of 3 Nm self-steer
       the lateral offset becomes dangerous).

STEP 3 — DESIGN DETECTION AND REACTION, THEN CHECK THE TIMING

The technical safety concept says: "software compares the torque-sensor channels every 2 ms; if |T1 + T2 − 5.0 V| > 0.2 V for 3 consecutive samples, it disables the gate driver and opens the phase relay". Now do the worst-case arithmetic:

FDTI (detection), worst case:
  fault happens just after a sample             → wait up to 2 ms for the next sample
  debounce: 3 consecutive bad samples needed    → samples at 2, 4, 6 ms
  FDTI ≤ 6 ms

FRTI (reaction):
  software writes PWM = 0, gate-driver ENABLE = 0   0.1 ms
  phase relay opens (datasheet release time)        5.0 ms
  motor current decays (L/R ≈ 2 ms)                 2.0 ms
  FRTI ≈ 7.1 ms

FDTI + FRTI = 6 + 7.1 = 13.1 ms  ≤  FTTI 50 ms   ✓  (36.9 ms margin)

Backup path if the MCU itself is dead (no software runs at all):
  SBC window watchdog: no valid trigger for > 15 ms  → timeout   15 ms
  SBC asserts SS1 → gate driver disabled               1 ms
  relay + current decay                                7 ms
  total ≈ 23 ms  ≤  50 ms   ✓

STEP 4 — BUILD THE HARDWARE FAILURE-RATE BUDGET (FMEDA)

An FMEDA (Failure Modes, Effects and Diagnostic Analysis) lists every safety-related part, its failure rate in FIT, the mechanism that covers it and the DC. The residual rate is λ × (1 − DC). First attempt:

STEP 5 — CHECK THE LATENT FAULTS (LFM) AND THE TOTAL RATE (PMHF)

The 50 FIT of safety-mechanism hardware can fail silently: a lockstep comparator stuck at "equal" never raises an alarm, and nobody notices until the CPU also fails. So at every power-up the software runs self-tests: it injects a compare error to see the comparator fire, writes a word with a deliberate ECC error, misses one watchdog trigger on purpose to see the SBC react, and toggles the relay while measuring. Say these tests catch 95 % of the mechanism faults:

λ latent = 50 × (1 − 0.95) = 2.5 FIT
LFM  = 1 − λlatent / (Σλ − λSPF − λRF) = 1 − 2.5 / (300 − 0 − 2.5) = 1 − 0.0084 = 99.16 %   ✓ (≥ 90 %)
PMHF ≈ λSPF + λRF (+ a small dual-point term) ≈ 2.5 FIT                         ✓ (< 10 FIT)

What does 2.5 FIT mean for real cars?
  1 000 000 cars × 1 h/day × 365 days = 3.65 × 10^8 operating hours per year
  3.65 × 10^8 h × 2.5 × 10^-9 /h ≈ 0.9 undetected dangerous faults per year in the whole fleet

That last line is the honest meaning of "safe": not zero, but a residual risk society has agreed is acceptable for a fatal-severity hazard.

STEP 6 — SOFTWARE: THE SYSTEMATIC SIDE

The plausibility check and the reaction are ASIL D software. You cannot give them a FIT rate, so you build them with ASIL D process: requirements traced to code and tests, MISRA C, static analysis, MC/DC coverage in unit tests, fault-injection tests that disconnect T2 and measure 13 ms to safe state on a HIL (Hardware-In-the-Loop) bench. If the ECU also runs a QM diagnostic or HMI component, you need FFI: MPU partitions, OS timing protection and a watchdog manager.

STEP 7 — ARGUE IT: THE SAFETY CASE

Goal: "SG-01 is met." Evidence: HARA (step 1), safety concept (step 2–3), timing analysis (13.1 ms ≤ 50 ms), FMEDA (SPFM 99.17 %, LFM 99.16 %, PMHF 2.5 FIT), software verification reports, fault-injection results, and a functional safety assessment by an independent person.

6What the firmware engineer actually writes

Interviews for embedded roles rarely ask you to do a HARA. They ask how you would implement the mechanisms. Here are the four patterns that appear in almost every safety ECU, in miniature. Each one is explained fully in the Concepts cards.

A · PLAUSIBILITY CHECK WITH DEBOUNCE (the EPS torque sensor)

#define SUM_NOM_MV   5000u   /* T1 + T2 must be 5.0 V                  */
#define SUM_TOL_MV    200u   /* allowed deviation ±0.2 V               */
#define DEBOUNCE        3u   /* 3 bad samples in a row = fault (6 ms)  */

void TorqueMon_2ms(uint16_t t1_mV, uint16_t t2_mV)
{
    static uint8_t bad = 0u;
    const uint32_t sum = (uint32_t)t1_mV + (uint32_t)t2_mV;   /* widen first  */
    const uint32_t dev = (sum > SUM_NOM_MV) ? (sum - SUM_NOM_MV) : (SUM_NOM_MV - sum);

    if (dev > SUM_TOL_MV) {
        if (bad < DEBOUNCE) { bad++; }
    } else {
        bad = 0u;                          /* healthy sample resets the count  */
    }
    if (bad >= DEBOUNCE) {
        Safety_EnterSafeState(SS_TORQUE_SENSOR);   /* latched: PWM 0, relay open */
    }
}
LineMeaning
(uint32_t)t1_mV + …Widen before adding so the sum cannot overflow 16 bits (MISRA essential-type rules).
dev = …Absolute difference without signed arithmetic, so no undefined behaviour.
bad++ / bad = 0Debounce: one noisy sample (EMC spike) is tolerated, three in a row are not. 3 × 2 ms = 6 ms of FDTI.
Safety_EnterSafeState()Latched reaction: once in the safe state, stay there until the next ignition cycle.

B · SERVICE THE WATCHDOG ONLY IF EVERY TASK IS ALIVE

✖ WRONG — PROVES ONLY THE TIMER IS ALIVE
void SysTick_Handler(void)
{
    WDG_Kick();       /* runs even if
                         main() is hung */
}
✔ RIGHT — PROVES THE TASKS RAN
void Supervisor_10ms(void)
{
    if (alive_torque >= 1u &&
        alive_comm   >= 1u) {
        WDG_Kick();
    }               /* else: let it bite */
    alive_torque = 0u; alive_comm = 0u;
}

C · REDUNDANT STORAGE OF A CRITICAL VARIABLE

s.val = v;  s.inv = ~v;                       /* write both copies          */
if ((s.val ^ s.inv) != 0xFFFFFFFFu) { fault(); } /* every bit must be opposite */

If a cosmic ray flips bit 2 of val, val ^ inv has bit 2 = 0 instead of 1, so the result is 0xFFFFFFFB, not 0xFFFFFFFF: detected.

D · ACT ON THE E2E STATUS, NOT JUST THE CRC

switch (E2E_Check(&rx, frame)) {
    case E2E_OK:            speed = frame_speed; stale = 0u; break;
    case E2E_REPEATED:                                     /* old data again  */
    case E2E_NONEWDATA:     if (++stale >= 3u) { speed = SPEED_SUBSTITUTE; } break;
    default:                speed = SPEED_SUBSTITUTE; Dem_Report(EV_E2E); break;
}

8Say it out loud: the 60-second answer

Functional safety, per ISO 26262, is the absence of unreasonable risk from malfunctioning electronics. We start with a HARA: every malfunction in every driving situation is rated for severity, exposure and controllability, which gives an ASIL from QM to D and a safety goal with a safe state and a fault tolerant time interval. Random hardware faults are handled quantitatively: safety mechanisms like lockstep, ECC, plausibility checks and an external watchdog must give enough diagnostic coverage that, for ASIL D, SPFM is at least 99 %, LFM at least 90 % and PMHF below 10 FIT. Software faults are systematic, so they are handled with process: traceable requirements, MISRA, static analysis, MC/DC coverage. And every detection plus reaction must fit inside the FTTI: for an EPS, for example, 6 ms detection plus 7 ms reaction against a 50 ms FTTI.

Watchdog & Program-Flow Monitor Simulator

A 10 ms control task reports checkpoints CP1 (read) → CP2 (compute) → CP3 (actuate) to an AUTOSAR-style Watchdog Manager. WdgM runs every 10 ms and applies alive, deadline and logical supervision; only while the global status is good does it trigger an external windowed watchdog (TLF35584-style SBC). Inject a fault and watch who notices, and how fast. Then switch the trigger source to "timer ISR" and repeat. Keys: Space run/pause, → step 1 ms.

HOW TO USE THIS LAB — 5 GUIDED EXPERIMENTS (use Step 1 ms and read “What just happened”)

  1. Healthy baseline. Press Run for about 50 ms, then pause. Notice every 10 ms: CP1 → CP2 → CP3, a WdgM status square “O”, and a cyan trigger diamond landing in the green (open) part of the HW window lane. This shows what “all good” looks like.
  2. Hung task, supervised trigger. In WdgM mode click Task hangs, then Step 10 ms repeatedly. Notice alive indications drop to 0, the status goes FAILED (amber) at the next 20 ms evaluation, EXPIRED at the second, then STOPPED; triggers stop and the window times out into the safe state. Read the latency against the 100 ms FTTI.
  3. Same fault, naive trigger. Switch to Timer ISR (naive) and inject Task hangs again. WdgM still goes STOPPED, but the ISR keeps triggering, so the safe state never comes. This shows why a timer ISR must never kick the watchdog (Scenario 01).
  4. Runaway trigger. Inject Runaway trigger: a trigger 2 ms after the previous one hits the red (closed) part of the window and causes an immediate safe state. A timeout-only watchdog would have accepted it.
  5. Dead CPU. Inject CPU dead in either mode. Nothing runs any more, not even the ISR; only the external watchdog, on its own oscillator, notices after 15 ms. This is why the final watchdog must be outside the MCU.

HOW TO USE THIS LAB — 5 GUIDED EXPERIMENTS

  1. Click (8,4) and keep the data 1 0 1 1. The stored codeword reads 0 0 1 1 0 0 1 1 (P0 first, then positions 1–7): the same codeword worked out by hand in Concepts card 19.
  2. Click the stored bit at position 6 (label D2·6). The syndrome becomes 1102 = 6 and the overall parity 1: single error at 6, corrected. In the equations box, notice that exactly the groups s1 and s2, the ones containing position 6, became odd.
  3. Also click position 3. The syndrome is now 3 XOR 6 = 5 but the overall parity is back to 0: a double error, detected and not corrected. The decoder refuses to guess.
  4. Click Clear errors, then flip positions 1, 2 and 4. The syndrome is 7 with odd parity, so the decoder “corrects” position 7 and returns 1010 instead of 1011: a silent miscorrection, the reason three errors are dangerous.
  5. On the E2E side: send three clean frames, pick Repeat old frame and send → REPEATED. Turn counter checking OFF and repeat → the stale value is accepted. Finally pick Masquerade: the CRC fails although no bit was corrupted, because the hidden Data ID differs.
