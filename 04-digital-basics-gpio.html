01BEGINNER

Logic levels, thresholds and noise margin

▶

A digital pin does not really see "1" and "0". It sees a voltage. The datasheet tells you which voltages are guaranteed to read as 1, which are guaranteed to read as 0, and leaves a gap in the middle where nothing is promised. Logic levels are those promises.

1. The four numbers and what each one means

VDD
The supply voltage of the chip's I/O, 3.3 V on our STM32F407 board. VSS is ground (0 V).
VIH(min)
"Input High". The lowest input voltage the chip guarantees to read as logic 1. Anything at or above it reads 1.
VIL(max)
"Input Low". The highest input voltage guaranteed to read as logic 0. Anything at or below it reads 0.
VOH(min)
"Output High". When a chip drives a 1, its pin voltage is at least this value, provided the load current does not exceed the stated test current (for example 8 mA).
VOL(max)
"Output Low". When a chip drives a 0, its pin voltage is at most this value at the stated sink current.
Forbidden band
The voltages between VIL and VIH. A pin sitting there may read 0, read 1, or flicker between them. It also draws extra supply current (card 09).
Noise margin
How much unwanted voltage (noise) can be added to a valid output before the receiving input might misread it.

2. A picture of the voltage scale

3. How to compute noise margin, step by step

  1. Take the driver's guaranteed output levels: VOH(min) and VOL(max).
  2. Take the receiver's guaranteed input thresholds: VIH(min) and VIL(max).
  3. High noise margin: NMH = VOH(min) − VIH(min). It is how far a "1" can be pushed down by noise before it might not read as 1.
  4. Low noise margin: NML = VIL(max) − VOL(max). It is how far a "0" can be pushed up before it might not read as 0.
  5. Both must be positive. If either is negative, the pair is not guaranteed to work, even if it seems fine on your bench at room temperature.

4. Worked example: STM32F407 output driving STM32F407 input at VDD = 3.3 V

The datasheet gives thresholds as formulas of VDD (the "design" values for FT/TTa pins):

  • VIL(max) = 0.35 × VDD − 0.04 = 0.35 × 3.3 − 0.04 = 1.155 − 0.04 = 1.115 V
  • VIH(min) = 0.45 × VDD + 0.3 = 0.45 × 3.3 + 0.3 = 1.485 + 0.3 = 1.785 V
  • VOH(min) = VDD − 0.4 = 2.9 V at 8 mA load. VOL(max) = 0.4 V at 8 mA.

NMH = 2.9 − 1.785 = 1.115 V. NML = 1.115 − 0.4 = 0.715 V. Both positive with lots of room.

The datasheet also gives a stricter "CMOS-guaranteed" pair, VIL = 0.3·VDD = 0.99 V and VIH = 0.7·VDD = 2.31 V. With those, NMH = 2.9 − 2.31 = 0.59 V, still fine.

5. Why is VOH stated "at 8 mA"? (output resistance)

The output transistor is not a perfect switch. It behaves roughly like a small resistor, called Ron (on-resistance). The more current you draw, the more voltage drops across it.

From VOL = 0.4 V at 8 mA: Ron ≈ 0.4 V ÷ 0.008 A = 50 Ω. At 20 mA the drop is about 20 mA × 50 Ω ≈ 1.0 V. The datasheet guarantees at most 1.3 V at 20 mA, which agrees. So an LED drawing 20 mA from a "3.3 V" pin really sees about 2.0–2.3 V.

Why: the input is being driven into the forbidden band, where behaviour is not specified.

Noise margin is VOH minus VIH for the high side and VIL minus VOL for the low side, and both must be positive. For an STM32F4 talking to itself at 3.3 V that gives about 1.1 V high and 0.7 V low."
02BEGINNER

CMOS vs TTL families, and mixing 3.3 V with 5 V

▶

Logic chips come in "families". A family is a set of parts built the same way, so they share input thresholds and output strengths. When you connect chips from different families, or running at different supply voltages, you must check that one side's output levels satisfy the other side's input thresholds (card 01).

1. Terms

TTL
Transistor-Transistor Logic. The original 1960s–70s bipolar logic (74xx, 74LS). Fixed thresholds: VIH = 2.0 V, VIL = 0.8 V. Its inputs push current out when low, and its outputs sink much more current than they can source.
CMOS
Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor. The technology inside every modern MCU. Inputs draw almost no current. Thresholds are usually a fraction of the supply ("ratiometric"), for example 30% and 70% of VCC.
74HC
High-speed CMOS logic. Thresholds are 0.3·VCC / 0.7·VCC.
74HCT
HC with TTL-compatible inputs (VIH = 2.0 V) but CMOS outputs. Powered from 5 V, it accepts a 3.3 V signal and outputs a full 5 V swing. That makes it a cheap level shifter.
LVTTL / LVCMOS
"Low-voltage" 3.3 V standards (JEDEC JESD8-C). Thresholds 0.8 V / 2.0 V.

3. How to check any connection, step by step

  1. Find the driver's VOH(min) and VOL(max) at the current your load will draw.
  2. Find the receiver's VIH(min) and VIL(max) at its actual supply voltage. For HC parts, use the worst-case supply (for example 5.5 V: VIH = 0.7 × 5.5 = 3.85 V).
  3. Check VOH > VIH and VOL < VIL (positive noise margins).
  4. Check absolute maximum ratings: does the driver's high level exceed what the receiver's pin can tolerate? 5 V into a pin rated for 3.6 V maximum is damage, not just a logic problem.

4. Worked examples: the three classic mixes

A. STM32 (3.3 V) → 74HCT at 5 V: VOH = 2.9 V, VIH = 2.0 V, so NMH = 0.9 V. VOL 0.4 V, VIL 0.8 V, so NML = 0.4 V. Works.

B. STM32 (3.3 V) → 74HC at 5 V: VIH = 0.7 × 5.0 = 3.5 V. NMH = 2.9 − 3.5 = −0.6 V. Not guaranteed. Many chips switch at about VCC/2 = 2.5 V in practice, so it "works on the bench" and fails across temperature and units.

C. 5 V CMOS output → STM32 input: 5 V > VIH, so the logic level is fine. But 5 V exceeds VDD + 0.3 = 3.6 V. On a non-tolerant pin, current flows through the internal protection diode into the 3.3 V rail (injection current, card 16). Use an FT pin, a series resistor, or a translator.

You assume "TTL means 5 V and CMOS means 3.3 V". That is a common wrong answer: family describes the transistor structure and thresholds, not the supply. 74HC runs anywhere from 2 to 6 V, and LVTTL is a 3.3 V standard with TTL thresholds. Getting this wrong leads to case B above: a design that fails in the field.
"Before connecting two parts I check the driver's VOH and VOL against the receiver's VIH and VIL, and the receiver's absolute-max voltage. A 3.3 V MCU can drive TTL or HCT inputs, but not a 5 V HC input, because 0.7 × 5 V is 3.5 V. The cheap fix is an HCT buffer or a dual-supply translator. In the other direction a 5 V signal needs a 5 V-tolerant pin, a resistor, or a translator, to avoid injection current."

Anatomy of a GPIO pin: input buffer, Schmitt trigger, output driver, protection

▶

GPIO means General-Purpose Input/Output: a pin whose job is decided by software. Inside the chip, every GPIO pin is the same small circuit: two transistors that can drive the pin high or low, two optional resistors, an input buffer that reads the pin, and protection diodes. Every register in card 05 simply switches parts of this circuit on or off.

1. The circuit

2. Each block, defined

P-MOS
A transistor between VDD and the pin. When switched on, it connects the pin to VDD through about 50 Ω and drives a 1.
N-MOS
A transistor between the pin and ground. When on, it drives a 0.
Push-pull
Both transistors are used: one or the other is on. The pin is always driven.
Open-drain
The P-MOS is never turned on. The pin can only be pulled low or released ("drain" is the N-MOS terminal left "open").
RPU / RPD
Weak pull-up / pull-down resistors (30–50 kΩ, typically 40 kΩ). They give an undriven pin a defined level. A switch in series lets the PUPDR register connect them.
Schmitt trigger
An input buffer with two switching thresholds: a higher one for rising inputs and a lower one for falling inputs. The difference is called hysteresis (about 0.33 V on the F4). Small noise on a slow edge cannot cross back over, so you get exactly one clean transition.
Synchroniser
Flip-flops that sample the pin on the bus clock so the CPU always reads a stable value (card 25). The result lands in IDR (Input Data Register) on every AHB1 clock cycle.
ESD / clamp diodes
Diodes that conduct if the pin goes above VDD or below VSS, to protect the chip from static discharge. ESD = electrostatic discharge.
Analog switch
Connects the pin straight to the ADC (Analog-to-Digital Converter) or DAC (Digital-to-Analog Converter).

3. What happens when software reads and writes the pin

  1. Write: the CPU writes 1 to the pin's bit in ODR (Output Data Register). The output control turns the P-MOS on and the N-MOS off, and the pin rises to about 3.3 V. The OSPEEDR setting decides how quickly the edge rises.
  2. Open-drain write of 1: both transistors are off, and the pin is left to the pull-up (internal or external).
  3. Read: the pin voltage goes through the Schmitt trigger, so it becomes a clean 0 or 1. The synchroniser stores it in IDR, and the CPU reads IDR.
  4. Important: the input path stays connected in output mode too. If you drive a 1 but something external shorts the pin to ground, IDR reads 0. That is how firmware detects a shorted output, or an I²C slave holding the clock low.
  5. Analog mode: the Schmitt trigger and the pulls are switched off, and the analog switch connects the pin to the ADC. IDR reads 0.
Reading the pin at 168 MHz HCLK: IDR is updated every AHB1 clock, every 1/168 MHz ≈ 6 ns, plus about 2 clocks of synchroniser delay. So a level change on the pin is visible in IDR within roughly 12–18 ns, which is much faster than any software can poll.
You read ODR to "check the pin". ODR only tells you what you asked the pin to do. If the pin is shorted, or another chip is fighting it, ODR still says 1. Symptom: firmware believes a line is high while the scope shows it low. Fix: read IDR when you need the real pin level.
"A GPIO is a P-MOS to VDD and an N-MOS to ground for output, switchable weak pull resistors, and a Schmitt-trigger input buffer that is synchronised into IDR every bus clock. Push-pull uses both transistors, open-drain disables the P-MOS. The input path stays active in output mode, which is why reading IDR shows the real pad level. ESD diodes protect the pin, and 5 V-tolerant pins replace the VDD diode with a different structure."

STM32F4 pin modes: input, output, alternate function, analog

▶

Every STM32F4 pin is in one of four modes, chosen by two bits in the MODER register. The mode decides who controls the pin: nobody (input), your code (output), a peripheral such as a UART (alternate function), or the analog circuitry (analog).

1. The four modes

MODER bitsModeWhat is switched onTypical use
00InputSchmitt input only; optional pull-up or pull-downButtons, status signals, interrupt sources. Reset default on F4.
01General-purpose outputOutput driver controlled by ODR/BSRR, plus the inputLEDs, enables, chip selects
10Alternate function (AF)Output driver controlled by a peripheralUART TX, SPI SCK, I²C SDA, timer PWM
11AnalogAnalog switch only; digital input and pulls offADC inputs, DAC outputs, unused pins (lowest power)

Three more registers refine the mode: OTYPER (output type: push-pull or open-drain), PUPDR (pull-up / pull-down / none) and OSPEEDR (edge speed). AFRL/AFRH choose which peripheral owns the pin in AF mode.

2. The seven practical configurations

  1. Input floating (MODER 00, PUPDR 00): no pull. Correct only when another chip always drives the line. Otherwise the pin picks up noise (card 09).
  2. Input pull-up (PUPDR 01): idles at 1. A button to ground pulls it to 0 when pressed. The standard "active-low button".
  3. Input pull-down (PUPDR 10): idles at 0. Used with a button to VDD.
  4. Output push-pull (MODER 01, OTYPER 0): drives both levels. The default for outputs the MCU alone controls.
  5. Output open-drain (MODER 01, OTYPER 1): pulls low or releases. Needs a pull-up resistor. Used for shared lines.
  6. Alternate function (MODER 10): for example USART2 TX on PA2 uses AF7. I²C uses AF with OTYPER = 1 (open-drain).
  7. Analog (MODER 11): ADC/DAC, or parking unused pins.

3. Worked example: reading the reset value of GPIOA→MODER

After reset, GPIOA→MODER = 0xA800_0000. In binary, the top byte is 0xA8 = 1010 1000, bits 31..24.

bits 31:30 = 10 → pin 15 = AF  (JTDI)
bits 29:28 = 10 → pin 14 = AF  (SWCLK)
bits 27:26 = 10 → pin 13 = AF  (SWDIO)
bits 25:24 = 00 → pin 12 = input
all other bits = 0 → pins 0–11 = input (floating)

So on an F4 every pin powers up as a floating input except the debug pins, which come up as alternate function so your debugger can connect. If you reconfigure PA13/PA14, you lose SWD debugging until the next reset.

Vendor difference: newer STM32 families (L4, G0, G4, H7, U5) reset MODER to 0xFFFF_FFFF, so every pin starts in analog mode to save power. The older STM32F1 uses completely different registers (CRL/CRH, 4 bits per pin), so F1 GPIO code does not run on an F4.
You leave a pin in its reset state (floating input) while it drives a MOSFET gate. Between power-on and your init code, the gate floats and the load can switch on randomly (card 17). Or you set an I²C pin to AF push-pull instead of AF open-drain: when a slave pulls SDA low to acknowledge while the MCU drives high, the two drivers fight (contention).
"MODER picks one of four modes: input, output, alternate function or analog. OTYPER picks push-pull or open-drain, PUPDR the pull, OSPEEDR the edge rate, and AFR which peripheral owns the pin in AF mode. On the F4 everything resets to floating input except the SWD/JTAG pins. Newer STM32s reset to analog. Unused pins should go to analog mode to minimise leakage."

The register set: MODER, OTYPER, OSPEEDR, PUPDR, IDR, ODR, BSRR, LCKR, AFR, with code

▶

Each GPIO port (A, B, C…) is a block of ten 32-bit registers in memory. Configuring a pin means writing the right bits for that pin into those registers. The only skill needed is "clear a bit field, then set it" for a field that is 1, 2 or 4 bits wide.

1. Where the registers live

Port base addresses on the F407: GPIOA = 0x4002_0000, GPIOB = 0x4002_0400, GPIOC = 0x4002_0800 … GPIOI = 0x4002_2000. Each port is 0x400 bytes after the previous one. Each register sits at an offset from its port's base address:

Address of GPIOD→ODR = 0x4002_0000 + 3 × 0x400 + 0x14 = 0x4002_0000 + 0xC00 + 0x14 = 0x4002_0C14. That is the number you would type into a debugger's memory window.

2. Finding a pin's bit field

For pin n:

  • 1-bit registers (OTYPER, ODR, IDR): the bit is n. Mask = 1u << n.
  • 2-bit registers (MODER, OSPEEDR, PUPDR): the field starts at bit 2n. Mask = 3u << (2*n).
  • 4-bit AFR: register index n >> 3 (0 for pins 0–7, 1 for pins 8–15). Field starts at bit (n & 7) * 4. Mask = 0xFu << ((n&7)*4).

Pin 5, MODER: field starts at bit 2×5 = 10, so bits 11:10. Mask = 3 << 10 = 0x0000_0C00. Output mode (01) = 1 << 10 = 0x0000_0400.

Pin 10, AFR: index 10 >> 3 = 1, so AFRH. Shift (10 & 7) × 4 = 2 × 4 = 8, so bits 11:8. AF7 = 7 << 8 = 0x0000_0700.

3. Wrong vs right: setting a 2-bit field

✖ WRONG
// Pin 5 is currently AF (10).
// We want output (01).
GPIOA->MODER |= (1u << 10);
// 10 OR 01 = 11 → ANALOG mode!
✔ RIGHT
uint32_t m = GPIOA->MODER;
m &= ~(3u << 10);  // clear both bits → 00
m |=  (1u << 10);  // set 01 → output
GPIOA->MODER = m;   // one write

|= can only turn bits on. If the field already had a 1 where you need a 0, OR leaves it set. The fix is always: clear the whole field with &= ~mask, then OR in the new value.

4. Complete annotated example: PA5 as an LED output

RCC->AHB1ENR |= RCC_AHB1ENR_GPIOAEN;   // (1) turn on GPIOA's clock (bit 0)
(void)RCC->AHB1ENR;                     // (2) dummy read-back: F4 errata, wait 2 AHB cycles
GPIOA->BSRR    = (1u << (5 + 16));      // (3) preload ODR5 = 0 BEFORE making it an output
GPIOA->OTYPER &= ~(1u << 5);            // (4) push-pull
GPIOA->OSPEEDR&= ~(3u << (5*2));        // (5) low speed: enough for an LED, least EMI
GPIOA->PUPDR  &= ~(3u << (5*2));        // (6) no pull
GPIOA->MODER   = (GPIOA->MODER & ~(3u << (5*2))) | (1u << (5*2)); // (7) output, LAST
GPIOA->BSRR    = (1u << 5);             // (8) LED on (atomic set)
StepWhy it is there
(1)Every peripheral is unclocked after reset to save power. Writes to an unclocked GPIO are ignored and reads return 0.
(2)STM32F4 errata: the first access right after enabling a clock can be lost. Reading the register back forces the delay.
(3)Set the output level first. Then the moment the pin becomes an output, it drives the right level. No glitch.
(7)MODER is written last so the pin only starts driving once everything else is configured.

5. Alternate function example: USART2 TX on PA2 (AF7)

GPIOA->AFR[0] = (GPIOA->AFR[0] & ~(0xFu << (2*4))) | (7u << (2*4)); // pin 2 → AF7 (USART2)
GPIOA->MODER  = (GPIOA->MODER  & ~(3u  << (2*2))) | (2u << (2*2));  // 10 = AF, done last

6. LCKR: locking the configuration

LCKR stops a pin's MODER/OTYPER/OSPEEDR/PUPDR/AFR bits from changing until the next reset. A stray pointer write can then no longer turn a safety output into an input. Unlocking needs a special write sequence, so a random write cannot do it.

uint32_t k = (1u << 16) | (1u << 5);  // LCKK key bit + pin 5
GPIOA->LCKR = k;             // step 1: write LCKK=1 + mask
GPIOA->LCKR = (1u << 5);     // step 2: write LCKK=0 + same mask
GPIOA->LCKR = k;             // step 3: write LCKK=1 + same mask
(void)GPIOA->LCKR;           // step 4: read — completes the sequence
if (!(GPIOA->LCKR & (1u << 16))) fault();  // step 5: LCKK reads 1 if locked

The lock covers configuration only. ODR and BSRR still work, so a locked LED pin can still blink.

You forget step (1), the clock enable. Symptom: all your writes "do nothing", and the debugger shows the GPIO registers as 0x0000_0000 no matter what you write. Or you use |= on a 2-bit field: a pin meant to be an output silently becomes analog, so the LED never lights and IDR reads 0.
"Each STM32 port has ten registers. For pin n the 2-bit fields start at bit 2n and the AFR field is in AFR[n/8] at bit (n mod 8)×4. I always clear the field then set it, because OR alone can't clear bits. I enable the port clock first, preload the output level, set type, speed, pull and AF, and write MODER last so the pin never glitches. LCKR can freeze a pin's configuration for safety."

Push-pull vs open-drain, and wired-AND bus sharing

▶

Push-pull means the pin actively drives both high and low. Open-drain means it can only pull low; for a high it lets go and a resistor pulls the line up. Open-drain exists so that several chips can share one wire without damaging each other.

1. Terms

Hi-Z
High impedance. The pin is not driving at all; it behaves like an open circuit.
Pull-up
A resistor from the line to VDD that makes the line high when nobody pulls it low.
Contention
Two outputs on the same wire driving opposite levels. Large current flows from one to the other.
Wired-AND
Open-drain outputs sharing a pulled-up line. The line is high only if every device releases it.

2. Side by side

Push-pullOpen-drain
Drives highYes, through the P-MOS (≈ 50 Ω)No. The pull-up resistor raises the line.
Rising edge speedFast (ns)Slow: Rpullup × Cline
Can share a wireNo: contentionYes: safe by design
High levelAlways VDDWhatever the pull-up connects to (5 V on an FT pin)
Typical usesLEDs, SPI, UART TX, chip selectsI²C, shared interrupt lines, reset lines, level translation

3. How wired-AND works, step by step (three devices on one line)

  1. All three devices release (output "1" = transistor off). No current path to ground, so the pull-up holds the line at VDD: the line reads 1.
  2. Device 2 turns its N-MOS on. The line goes to about 0 V. Current flows VDD → 4.7 kΩ → device 2 → GND: 3.3 V / 4.7 kΩ = 0.7 mA. Harmless.
  3. Device 1 also pulls low. Still 0 V, and still no fight, because both are pulling the same way.
  4. The line returns to 1 only when every device has released. Logically, line = D1 AND D2 AND D3, hence "wired-AND".
  5. Compare push-pull: if device 1 drives high and device 2 drives low, current = 3.3 V / (50 Ω + 50 Ω) = 33 mA through both drivers. That is over the 25 mA absolute maximum, and the line sits at an undefined mid-level.

4. Worked example: rise time of an open-drain line

Pull-up 4.7 kΩ, line capacitance 100 pF (a short I²C bus). τ = R × C = 4700 × 100×10−12 = 470 ns. From 0 V to VIH (≈ 54% of VDD on the F4) takes t = −τ·ln(1 − 0.54) = 470 ns × 0.78 ≈ 365 ns. Fine for 100 kHz I²C (5 µs half period), tight for 1 MHz (500 ns). A stronger pull-up (lower R) gives faster edges but more current when low.

5. Code: driving an open-drain line correctly

✖ WRONG — reads ODR
// release the line, then check it
GPIOB->BSRR = 1u << 7;          // release
if (GPIOB->ODR & (1u << 7))     // always true!
    bus_is_free();
✔ RIGHT — reads IDR
GPIOB->BSRR = 1u << 7;          // release (OD)
delay_rise();                    // allow R·C rise
if (GPIOB->IDR & (1u << 7))     // real line level
    bus_is_free();
else someone_holds_it_low();     // stretch/stuck
You configure I²C SDA as AF push-pull. When the slave pulls SDA low to acknowledge while the MCU is driving it high, the two outputs fight. Symptom: ACK bits read wrong, the bus "sometimes works", the scope shows SDA sitting at 1–2 V during ACK, and the parts get warm. Fix: OTYPER = 1 (open-drain) with external pull-ups.
"Push-pull drives both levels with fast edges but can't share a wire: two push-pull outputs driving opposite levels short VDD to ground through about 100 Ω. Open-drain only pulls low and relies on a pull-up for the high, so any number of devices can share a line safely. The result is a wired-AND, which I²C uses for arbitration, ACK and clock stretching. The cost is a slow RC rising edge, and to see the real line level you read IDR, not ODR."
07BEGINNER

Pull-up and pull-down resistors: choosing values and the current they cost

▶

A pull-up resistor connects a line to VDD so it reads 1 when nothing drives it. A pull-down connects it to ground so it reads 0. Choosing the value is a trade-off: too large and the line is weak and slow; too small and it wastes current and other chips can't pull against it.

1. Terms

Strong / weak
A small resistance (1 kΩ) is a strong pull: it holds the level firmly and charges capacitance fast. A large one (100 kΩ) is weak.
Leakage current
The tiny current every input pin draws or supplies even when idle, up to ±1 µA on the F4. A weak pull-up must overcome all of it.
IOL
The maximum current a device can sink while keeping its output at or below VOL. For I²C devices it is 3 mA in standard and fast mode.
Bus capacitance Cb
The total capacitance of the wire plus every pin attached to it. It is what the pull-up has to charge.

2. The four limits on the value, step by step

  1. Static current (sets the minimum value): when the line is held low, current = VDD / R flows continuously. 3.3 V / 10 kΩ = 330 µA. 3.3 V / 40 kΩ (internal) = 83 µA.
  2. Sink capability (minimum value): the device pulling low must sink that current and still reach VOL: Rmin = (VDD − VOL) / IOL.
  3. Leakage (maximum value): the voltage drop from leakage, Ileak × R, must not drag the line below VIH.
  4. Rise time (maximum value): the line must reach VIH fast enough: tr ≈ k × R × C. For I²C the spec uses 30%→70%, so k = 0.8473.

3. Worked examples

I²C at 100 kHz, 3.3 V, Cb = 400 pF (worst case), tr(max) = 1000 ns:

  • Rmin = (3.3 − 0.4) / 0.003 = 2.9 / 0.003 = 967 Ω
  • Rmax = tr / (0.8473 × Cb) = 1000×10−9 / (0.8473 × 400×10−12) = 1000 / 338.9 kΩ ≈ 2.95 kΩ
  • Pick 2.2 kΩ. At 400 kHz (tr = 300 ns) with 100 pF: Rmax = 300 / 84.7 ≈ 3.5 kΩ, so 2.2 kΩ still works. That is why 2.2 k–4.7 k are the usual values.

Leakage limit with 10 inputs on one line: Ileak = 10 × 1 µA = 10 µA. The line must stay above VIH = 2.31 V, so the allowed drop is 3.3 − 2.31 = 0.99 V. Rmax = 0.99 V / 10 µA = 99 kΩ.

Battery button: a button pressed for 1 s per minute through 10 kΩ averages 330 µA / 60 = 5.5 µA, which could double a 5 µA sleep current. With 100 kΩ it is 0.55 µA.

Driving LEDs and loads: sink vs source, current limits, when to add a transistor

▶

To light an LED from a pin, current must flow through the LED and a resistor that limits it. The pin can either source that current (push it out of the pin while high) or sink it (pull it into the pin while low). Which one you choose decides whether the LED is "on when 1" or "on when 0", and which output types work.

1. Terms

VF
Forward voltage: the voltage an LED drops while conducting. Red ≈ 1.8–2.0 V, green ≈ 2.0–3.0 V, blue/white ≈ 2.9–3.3 V.
Source
Current flows out of the pin: pin → resistor → LED → GND. The LED lights when the pin is high (active-high).
Sink
Current flows into the pin: VDD → resistor → LED → pin. The LED lights when the pin is low (active-low).
Absolute maximum
Limits beyond which the chip may be damaged. Never a design target.
Flyback diode
A diode across an inductive load (relay coil, motor) that gives the coil current a path when the switch opens, so the voltage spike cannot destroy the transistor.

2. The two wirings

3. Sizing the resistor, step by step

  1. Pick the LED current. 2–5 mA is plenty for a modern indicator LED; 20 mA is the old datasheet number and is usually too bright.
  2. Find VF at that current from the LED datasheet.
  3. Estimate the pin's own drop: I × Ron, with Ron ≈ 50 Ω (card 01).
  4. Source: R = (VDD − I·Ron − VF) / I. Sink: R = (VDD − VF − I·Ron) / I. Same arithmetic, because the pin drop appears in the loop either way.
  5. Round to the nearest standard value. Check the pin current is well below 8 mA, so the datasheet's VOH/VOL guarantee still applies.

Red LED, VF = 2.0 V, 5 mA, VDD = 3.3 V. Pin drop = 5 mA × 50 Ω = 0.25 V. R = (3.3 − 0.25 − 2.0) / 0.005 = 1.05 / 0.005 = 210 Ω → 220 Ω. Check: I = 1.05 / 220 = 4.8 mA. ✔

Blue LED, VF = 3.0 V: headroom = 3.3 − 3.0 = 0.3 V for both the resistor and the pin. A 2% change in VF (60 mV) changes the current by 20%: the brightness is poorly controlled. Better: power it from 5 V. With an FT pin in open-drain, internal pulls off, sink wiring: R = (5 − 3.0 − 0.25)/5 mA = 350 Ω → 330 Ω.

6. Why so many old designs sink

Old TTL (74LS) outputs could sink 8 mA but source only 0.4 mA. Engineers therefore wired LEDs and signals as active-low, and the convention survived in names like CS#, RESET# and INT#. Modern CMOS GPIOs are nearly symmetric. Sinking still has one real advantage: the LED can run from a higher rail through an open-drain output.

You drive a 5 V relay coil (70 Ω) directly from a pin: I = 5 V / (70 + 50) Ω ≈ 42 mA. Symptom: the relay chatters or doesn't pull in, and eventually the pin dies (stuck or leaky). Fix: MOSFET plus flyback diode.

Sinking works with push-pull or open-drain, and lets the LED sit on a higher rail. I stay under about 8 mA per pin, well below the 25 mA absolute max. Anything bigger, or inductive, gets a logic-level MOSFET with a gate pull-down and a flyback diode."

Floating inputs: why they read randomly and why they draw current

▶

A "floating" input is a pin that nothing drives and nothing pulls: no output, no resistor. Its voltage is undefined. It drifts with leakage and picks up noise like an antenna. Firmware sees random 0s and 1s, interrupts fire for no reason, and the chip draws extra current.

1. Why the voltage is undefined

  1. The pin is a tiny capacitor, about 5 pF of pin capacitance plus the trace.
  2. The only things connected to it are leakage currents (≤ 1 µA, direction unknown) and stray capacitive coupling to nearby signals, your finger, or mains wiring.
  3. Charge from leakage accumulates. Voltage change rate: dV/dt = I / C = 1 µA / 5 pF = 0.2 V per µs. The pin can wander across the whole 0–3.3 V range in microseconds.
  4. A neighbouring trace toggling 3.3 V, coupled through 0.5 pF onto 5 pF, jumps the floating pin by 3.3 × 0.5/5.5 ≈ 0.3 V on every edge.

2. Why a floating input draws current

A CMOS input stage is an inverter: a P-transistor from VDD and an N-transistor to ground, both gates tied to the pin. At 0 V or 3.3 V exactly one is on, so no current flows. Near mid-rail (≈ 1.65 V) both are partly on, and a "crowbar" current flows straight from VDD to ground through the input stage.

pin voltage:   0 V      1.0 V     1.65 V     2.3 V     3.3 V
P-MOS:         ON       ON        half       weak      OFF
N-MOS:         OFF      weak      half       ON        ON
crowbar I:     ~0       small     MAX        small     ~0     (tens–hundreds of µA per pin, process dependent)
A sleep-mode budget of 2 µA. Ten unused pins left as floating inputs, each drifting to mid-rail and drawing about 50 µA, add 500 µA, which is 250 × the budget. The battery that should last 2 years lasts 3 days.

3. What to do with every unused or optional pin

✖ WRONG — leave the reset default
// F4: all pins reset to floating input.
// "Unused, so I don't touch them."
// EXTI on PB5 with no pull and the
// external resistor not fitted (DNP).
✔ RIGHT — park them
// Unused pins on port B → analog (11):
// Schmitt input OFF, pulls OFF, ~0 leakage

// Or for a used input: enable a pull
GPIOB->PUPDR = (GPIOB->PUPDR & ~(3u<<10))
             | (1u<<10);     // PB5 pull-up

(Setting both MODER bits to 1 with |= is safe here, because the target value 11 has every bit set, so OR cannot produce a wrong combination.)

  1. Analog mode (best on STM32): the digital input buffer is disconnected, so there is no crowbar current regardless of voltage. ST recommends it, and newer STM32 families reset to it.
  2. Output push-pull low: defined and low-power, but risky if the pin is accidentally connected to something that drives high.
  3. Input with internal pull: defined level. Costs current only if the board pulls the opposite way.
An EXTI interrupt is enabled on a floating pin. Symptom: the ISR fires randomly, from a few times a day to thousands per second. It goes away when you touch the board or attach a scope probe, because the probe adds 10 MΩ and about 10 pF, which changes the node. That makes it a maddening "Heisenbug". Fix: give the pin a defined level with a pull resistor, or don't enable the interrupt on an unconnected pin.
"A floating input has only leakage and stray coupling defining its voltage, so it drifts into the undefined band. Reads are random, an EXTI on it fires spuriously, and near mid-rail both input transistors conduct and draw crowbar current, which can wreck a low-power budget. On STM32 I put unused pins in analog mode, which disconnects the Schmitt trigger, and every used input gets a pull either inside or outside the chip."

Switch bounce: the physics and the real timings

▶

When you press a mechanical button, the metal contacts do not close once. They hit, spring apart, hit again, and so on for a few milliseconds before settling. To the microcontroller, one press looks like a burst of many presses. This is called contact bounce, and dealing with it is called debouncing.

1. Why contacts bounce

  1. The moving contact is a spring with mass. When it strikes the fixed contact, it rebounds elastically, like a dropped ball.
  2. Each rebound is smaller than the last, so you get several opens and closes of shrinking duration.
  3. Even once the contacts touch, surface oxide films and microscopic roughness make the resistance flicker for a short time.
  4. On release, the contacts can chatter as they separate: release bounce.

2. What it looks like on the pin (pull-up, button to ground)

3. Real numbers

  • Good tactile switches: 1–5 ms total bounce. Many vendors (for example Omron, C&K) specify ≤ 5 ms.
  • Cheap, worn, toggle or snap-action switches: 10–20 ms is not unusual.
  • Jack Ganssle's measurements of 18 assorted switches: mean bounce about 1.6 ms, worst about 6.2 ms, with rare much longer outliers.
  • Width of individual bounce pulses: from under 1 µs up to about 1 ms.
  • Human timing: nobody presses faster than about 10 times a second, and a delay under about 50 ms feels instant. So a settle time of 10–20 ms is the standard design value.

4. Worked example: how many interrupts does one press cause?

A falling-edge EXTI interrupt on a button with 3 ms of bounce containing 6 make-break cycles. The press produces 6 falling edges, so 6 interrupts. The release produces another 2–3 falling edges from release chatter. A press counter in the ISR increments by about 8 for a single press. The EXTI has no filter and catches pulses well under 1 µs, so it sees every one.

It also bounces on release. EXTI catches every one of those edges, so I sample the pin from a timer and require it to be stable for 10–20 ms before reporting a change."

Hardware debouncing: RC filter + Schmitt trigger, with the maths

▶

A resistor and a capacitor form a low-pass filter: the capacitor voltage cannot jump, it can only change gradually. Fast bounce pulses therefore barely move it, and the pin sees one slow, smooth transition. A Schmitt-trigger input turns that slow ramp into exactly one clean digital edge.

1. Terms

τ (tau)
Time constant = R × C, in seconds. After one τ the capacitor has moved 63% of the way to its target, after 3τ 95%, after 5τ 99%.
Low-pass filter
A circuit that passes slow changes and blocks fast ones.
VT+ / VT−
The Schmitt trigger's rising and falling switching thresholds. In this sim we use 1.82 V and 1.48 V on 3.3 V.

2. The circuit

(Strictly, during the press the cap discharges through R2 while R1 still feeds current in, so the cap settles at 3.3 × 1k/11k = 0.3 V, which is still well below VT−.)

3. What happens on a press, step by step

  1. Before the press, C is charged to 3.3 V and the pin reads 1.
  2. The contact closes: C starts discharging through R2 with τ = 1 ms.
  3. The contact bounces open after 50 µs. In 50 µs (0.05 τ) C has only dropped about 5%, to about 3.1 V. It starts recharging slowly through R1 (τ = 11 ms), barely moving.
  4. The contact closes again, and so on. The average trend is downward; the bounces just add small ripples.
  5. When the cap crosses VT− = 1.48 V, the Schmitt output switches to 0 once. A small ripple back up cannot reach VT+ = 1.82 V, 0.34 V higher, so there is no second edge.

4. Worked example: how long until the pin switches?

Charging from 0 toward VDD, time to reach VT: t = −τ · ln(1 − VT/VDD). Discharging from VDD, time to fall to VT: t = −τ · ln(VT/VDD).

  • Release: VT+/VDD = 1.82/3.3 = 0.552. ln(1 − 0.552) = ln(0.448) = −0.803. t = 0.803 × 11 ms = 8.8 ms.
  • Press: VT−/VDD = 1.48/3.3 = 0.448. ln(0.448) = −0.803. t = 0.803 × 1 ms = 0.8 ms.

Design rule: choose τ at least as long as the expected bounce, so bounce cannot carry the cap across a threshold. For 5 ms of bounce with a symmetric filter, τ ≈ 5–10 ms, for example 47 kΩ × 100 nF = 4.7 ms.

6. Hardware vs software

  • Hardware cost: two resistors and a capacitor per button, plus asymmetric latency (here 0.8 ms on press and 8.8 ms on release).
  • Software cost: no parts, but a timer and a few bytes of RAM per button.
  • Best practice: a small RC (1–10 kΩ + 100 nF, τ = 0.1–1 ms) to kill EMI spikes and ESD energy, plus software debouncing for the bounce. That combination survives both noisy environments and bad switches.
  • Dedicated ICs such as the MAX6816/6817/6818 combine debounce and ESD protection, with a fixed delay of about 40 ms.
You omit R2, so the switch shorts the capacitor directly. The discharge current peak is limited only by the contact resistance (amps for microseconds), which erodes the contacts. And τpress ≈ 0, so press bounce passes straight through to the pin. Or you use an RC on a non-Schmitt input: multiple edges, plus extra supply current during each slow transition.

Software debouncing: five algorithms and their trade-offs

▶

Software debouncing means: look at the pin at regular intervals, and only believe a new level once it has stayed the same for long enough. Every good algorithm has the same three parts. It samples at a fixed rate, requires agreement over time, and reports only changes. Try all of them side by side in the Debounce Lab tab.

1. Terms

Ts
Sample period: how often the debouncer reads the pin, typically 1–5 ms from a timer interrupt.
raw
The pin level read at this sample (from IDR).
stable / out
The debounced level the rest of the program uses.
Latency
Time from the real press to the debounced output changing.
False trigger
The output changes when the user did not press or release: caused by a noise spike, or by bounce getting through.

2. Algorithm A: consecutive-sample counter

Idea: count how many samples in a row disagree with the current stable level. When the count reaches N, accept the new level. Any sample that agrees with the old level resets the count.

static uint8_t stable = 1, cnt = 0;        // pin idles high (pull-up)
void debounce_tick(void) {                 // called every Ts = 1 ms
    uint8_t raw = (GPIOC->IDR >> 13) & 1u;
    if (raw != stable) {                   // disagrees with the accepted level
        if (++cnt >= N) {                  // N in a row → believe it
            stable = raw; cnt = 0;
            post_event(stable ? EV_RELEASE : EV_PRESS);
        }
    } else {
        cnt = 0;                           // agreement with the old level → start over
    }
}
Ts = 1 ms, N = 10. Press bounces for 3 ms, then holds low. Counting only starts to succeed after the last bounce: 3 ms + 10 × 1 ms ≈ 13 ms latency. A noise spike lasting less than 10 ms causes no output change.

3. Algorithm B: integrator (Kenneth Kuhn)

Idea: keep a counter between 0 and MAX. Each high sample adds 1, each low sample subtracts 1 (it saturates at the ends). The output becomes 0 only when the counter reaches 0, and 1 only when it reaches MAX. In between, the output holds: that is hysteresis.

static uint8_t integ = MAX, out = 1;
void debounce_tick(void) {
    if (raw)  { if (integ < MAX) integ++; }   // lean toward 1
    else      { if (integ > 0)   integ--; }   // lean toward 0
    if (integ == 0)        out = 0;           // fully low
    else if (integ == MAX) out = 1;           // fully high
}                                             // otherwise: hold previous out

Difference from the counter: one wrong sample costs one step back, not a full restart. Under heavy chatter (60% low, 40% high) it still drifts to 0 and commits. The counter might never commit.

4. Algorithm C: shift register (Jack Ganssle)

Idea: shift each new sample into a byte. If all 8 bits are 1, the input has been high for 8 samples; if all are 0, low for 8 samples. Otherwise hold.

static uint8_t hist = 0xFF, out = 1;
void debounce_tick(void) {
    hist = (uint8_t)((hist << 1) | raw);   // oldest sample falls off the left
    if (hist == 0xFF)      out = 1;        // 1111 1111
    else if (hist == 0x00) out = 0;        // 0000 0000
}
Samples after a press with bounce, oldest to newest: 1 1 0 1 0 0 0 0 → hist = 0xD0 (1101 0000): not 0x00, hold. Four more low samples: 0x00 → out = 0. Latency = 8 × Ts after the last bounce. It uses one byte of state and no branches in the hot path, so it is cheap for many buttons.

5. Algorithm D: timer lockout (edge interrupt + ignore window)

Idea: react immediately to the first edge, then ignore the pin for Tlock (for example 20 ms). At the end of the window, check the level once more in case it changed during the lockout.

void EXTI15_10_IRQHandler(void) {
    EXTI->PR = 1u << 13;                 // clear pending (write 1)
    EXTI->IMR &= ~(1u << 13);            // mask further edges
    out = pin_level(); post_event(out);  // react NOW: zero latency
    start_oneshot_timer(20);             // lockout window
}
void TIM6_IRQHandler(void) {             // lockout ended
    TIM6->SR = 0;
    if (pin_level() != out) { out = pin_level(); post_event(out); start_oneshot_timer(20); return; }
    EXTI->PR = 1u << 13; EXTI->IMR |= 1u << 13;   // re-arm
}

Strength: zero latency, and the pin interrupt can wake the MCU from sleep. Weakness: it trusts the very first edge, so a 1 µs EMI spike is reported as a full press. Pair it with an RC filter.

6. Algorithm E: timer-sampled state machine (for real UIs)

switch (st) {                        // called every 5 ms; pressed = debounced-ish raw
 case IDLE:       if (pressed) st = MAYBE_DOWN;                       break;
 case MAYBE_DOWN: if (pressed) { st = DOWN; t0 = now; emit(PRESS); }
                  else st = IDLE;                                     break;
 case DOWN:       if (!pressed) st = MAYBE_UP;
                  else if (now - t0 >= 800) { emit(LONG); st = HELD; } break;
 case HELD:       if (!pressed) st = MAYBE_UP;                        break;
 case MAYBE_UP:   if (!pressed) { st = IDLE; emit(RELEASE); }
                  else st = (now - t0 >= 800) ? HELD : DOWN;          break;
}

This one also expresses click, long-press, double-click and auto-repeat, which the plain debouncers cannot.

Edge vs level detection; GPIO interrupts via EXTI vs polling

▶

Level detection asks "is the pin low right now?" Edge detection asks "did the pin change?" Polling means your code checks the pin repeatedly. An interrupt means hardware watches the pin and calls your function when an edge happens. On STM32, that pin-interrupt hardware is the EXTI (Extended Interrupt and Event Controller).

1. Terms

Rising edge
A 0 → 1 transition. Falling edge: 1 → 0.
EXTI line
One edge detector. Lines 0–15 can each be connected to pin n of one port.
SYSCFG
System configuration controller. Its EXTICR registers choose which port feeds each EXTI line.
Pending bit (PR)
A flag the EXTI sets when it sees the selected edge. It stays set until software clears it.
NVIC
Nested Vectored Interrupt Controller: the Cortex-M block that decides which interrupt handler runs.
Write-1-to-clear
A register bit cleared by writing a 1 to it. Writing 0 does nothing.

2. Edge detection in software (from sampled values)

uint16_t now  = GPIOB->IDR;
uint16_t chg  = now ^ prev;      // bits that changed
uint16_t rise = chg & now;       // changed AND now 1  → rising
uint16_t fall = chg & prev;      // changed AND was 1  → falling
prev = now;
prev = 0b0110, now = 0b0011. chg = 0b0101. rise = 0b0101 & 0b0011 = 0b0001 (bit 0 rose). fall = 0b0101 & 0b0110 = 0b0100 (bit 2 fell). Bits 1 and 3 did not change.

3. How a GPIO interrupt reaches your ISR on the F4, step by step

  1. PC13 falls from 1 to 0.
  2. SYSCFG→EXTICR[3] bits 7:4 = 2 (port C) route PC13 to EXTI line 13.
  3. EXTI→FTSR bit 13 is set (falling-edge trigger enabled), so the detector sets EXTI→PR bit 13.
  4. EXTI→IMR bit 13 is set (not masked), so the EXTI raises its request to the NVIC on IRQ 40 (EXTI15_10_IRQn, which is shared by lines 10–15).
  5. If enabled and of high enough priority, the core enters EXTI15_10_IRQHandler after about 12 cycles.
  6. The ISR reads PR to find which of lines 10–15 fired, and clears it by writing 1. If not cleared, the interrupt fires again immediately after return.

4. Key EXTI facts (F4)

  • 23 lines in total: 0–15 for GPIO; 16 PVD; 17 RTC alarm; 18 USB OTG FS wakeup; 19 Ethernet wakeup; 20 OTG HS wakeup; 21 RTC tamper/timestamp; 22 RTC wakeup.
  • One port per line: PA3 and PB3 cannot both interrupt, because both need line 3.
  • Individual vectors for EXTI0–4 (IRQ 6–10). Shared: EXTI9_5 (IRQ 23) and EXTI15_10 (IRQ 40).
  • Registers at 0x4001_3C00: IMR, EMR (event mask: wakes WFE without an ISR), RTSR, FTSR (both can be set for both edges), SWIER (software trigger), PR.
  • No glitch filter: even sub-microsecond pulses set PR.

Why BSRR is atomic and ODR read-modify-write is not

▶

Changing one pin through ODR takes three steps: read the whole register, change one bit, write the whole register back. If an interrupt changes another pin of the same port between the read and the write, your write puts the old value of that other pin back. BSRR avoids this because it changes only the bits you name, in a single write.

1. Terms

RMW
Read-Modify-Write: read a value, change it, write it back. Three separate operations.
Atomic
Happens as one indivisible step. Nothing (interrupt, other task, DMA) can observe or interfere halfway through.
Race condition
A bug where the result depends on the exact timing of two code paths.
BSRR
Bit Set/Reset Register. Writing 1 to bit n sets pin n; writing 1 to bit n+16 clears pin n; writing 0 does nothing.

2. The race, instruction by instruction

Main code turns on the green LED (PD12) with GPIOD->ODR |= (1<<12);. A timer ISR toggles a buzzer (PD13) with GPIOD->ODR ^= (1<<13);.

  1. ldr r2, [r3, #0x14]: main reads ODR = 0x0000 (buzzer off).
  2. The timer interrupt fires. The ISR reads 0x0000, XORs bit 13, writes 0x2000. The buzzer turns on. The ISR returns.
  3. orr r2, r2, #0x1000: main still holds the stale 0x0000 in r2, so it computes 0x1000.
  4. str r2, [r3, #0x14]: main writes ODR = 0x1000. Green on, buzzer off. The ISR's change is lost.

3. Why BSRR fixes it

  1. BSRR is write-only. The CPU never reads it, so there is nothing to go stale.
  2. Each 1 in the written value is an instruction to the hardware ("set pin 12"). Each 0 means "leave alone".
  3. The GPIO hardware applies the change to ODR internally, in the same clock cycle as the single STR instruction. No interrupt can split one store.
  4. So main's BSRR = 1<<12 touches only pin 12, and the ISR's BSRR = 1<<13 or 1<<29 touches only pin 13. Any interleaving gives the right result.
  5. If the same write has both BSn and BRn set, set wins.
Set pins 0 and 3, clear pins 1 and 7, all in one write: set mask = 0x0009, clear mask = 0x0082. BSRR = 0x0009 | (0x0082 << 16) = 0x0082_0009.
✖ WRONG — RMW on ODR from two contexts
// main
GPIOD->ODR |=  (1u << 12);
GPIOD->ODR &= ~(1u << 12);
// ISR
GPIOD->ODR ^=  (1u << 13);
// volatile does NOT help: the race is
// between instructions, not caching
✔ RIGHT — BSRR
// main
GPIOD->BSRR = (1u << 12);         // set
GPIOD->BSRR = (1u << (12 + 16));  // clear
// ISR: toggle via BSRR (safe for OTHER pins)
uint32_t o = GPIOD->ODR & (1u << 13);
GPIOD->BSRR = o ? (1u << 29) : (1u << 13);

4. Alternatives and vendor notes

  • Bit-banding (Cortex-M3/M4 only): every bit in 0x4000_0000–0x400F_FFFF has its own word address. Alias = 0x4200_0000 + (offset × 32) + bit × 4. GPIOA→ODR is offset 0x2_0014, so PA5 = 0x4200_0000 + 0x40_0280 + 20 = 0x4240_0294. A write there is a locked, atomic RMW. It does not exist on Cortex-M0, M7 or M33, so BSRR is the portable choice.
  • Disable interrupts around an ODR RMW: works, but adds interrupt latency for no reason.
  • Other vendors have dedicated set/clear/toggle registers: NXP Kinetis PSOR/PCOR/PTOR, Microchip PIC32 LATxSET/CLR/INV, RP2040 GPIO_OUT_SET/CLR/XOR. Newer STM32 families also have a separate BRR register.
  • HAL_GPIO_WritePin uses BSRR, so it is safe. HAL_GPIO_TogglePin reads ODR then writes BSRR: safe for other pins, but two contexts toggling the same pin can still race.
Symptom of the RMW race: a pin you never touched in this code "randomly" changes state, perhaps once an hour, and only in the optimised build or under load. Adding a printf or a breakpoint changes the timing and hides it. Root cause: two contexts doing RMW on the same port register.
"Writing ODR with |= or &= is a read-modify-write of three instructions. If an ISR changes another pin of the same port between the read and the write, the stale value is written back and the ISR's change is lost. BSRR is write-only: ones set or reset only the named pins, applied in a single store, so it's atomic with respect to other pins. volatile doesn't help, because it's an instruction-level race. Bit-banding is an M3/M4-only alternative."

OSPEEDR: slew rate, EMI, ringing and the I/O compensation cell

▶

OSPEEDR does not make your code toggle the pin faster. It sets how steep each edge is: how quickly the voltage swings from 0 to 3.3 V. Steeper edges are needed for fast buses, but they radiate noise, ring on long traces, and bounce the chip's ground. The rule is: use the slowest setting that still meets your timing.

1. Terms

Slew rate
How fast the output voltage changes, in V/ns. A faster slew rate gives a shorter rise time tr.
tr / tf
Rise and fall time, usually measured from 10% to 90% of the swing.
EMI
Electromagnetic interference: unwanted radio-frequency energy that your board emits, or that disturbs it. Products must pass EMC (electromagnetic compatibility) tests.
Ringing
Damped oscillation after an edge, caused by trace inductance and capacitance. It can overshoot above VDD or below GND.
Ground bounce
When many outputs switch at once, their current spikes flow through the package's ground pin inductance (V = L·di/dt), so the chip's internal ground jumps momentarily.

3. Why a fast edge causes EMI even at a slow toggle rate

  1. A digital signal is a trapezoid. Its frequency spectrum has energy at the toggle frequency and its harmonics.
  2. The harmonics fall at −20 dB/decade up to a first corner (set by the period), then at −40 dB/decade above a second corner frequency f = 1/(π·tr).
  3. So the edge time, not the toggle rate, decides how high in frequency significant energy reaches.
An LED toggled once per second. At OSPEEDR = 11, tr ≈ 2.5 ns, so f = 1/(π × 2.5 ns) = 1/7.85 ns ≈ 127 MHz. The edge has strong content up to 127 MHz, which is the FM band and in the middle of the EMC test range. At OSPEEDR = 00, tr ≈ 100 ns gives f ≈ 3.2 MHz, so the energy above that falls off fast. Same blink, very different radiated emissions.

4. Ringing: when a trace becomes a transmission line

Rule of thumb: if the trace's one-way propagation delay is longer than about tr/6, reflections matter. On FR-4 PCB, signals travel at about 15 cm/ns (roughly half the speed of light).

tr = 2.5 ns, so tr/6 ≈ 0.42 ns, and 0.42 ns × 15 cm/ns ≈ 6 cm. A 10 cm SPI clock trace at OSPEEDR = 11 will ring, possibly producing a double clock edge. At OSPEEDR = 01 (≈ 10 ns), the critical length becomes 25 cm, so the same trace is clean.

The output transistors' strength varies with manufacturing process, voltage and temperature. For speeds above 50 MHz, ST recommends enabling the compensation cell (SYSCFG→CMPCR.CMP_PD = 1, then wait for READY). It trims the output impedance to reduce overshoot and ringing. It is only needed for the 50/100 MHz settings with VDD between 2.4 and 3.6 V.

Every pin set to maximum speed. "OSPEEDR sets the output slew rate, not how fast software can toggle the pin. So I pick the slowest setting that meets each signal's timing: low for LEDs and enables, medium for UART and slow SPI, high only for buses like SDIO or Ethernet, with the F4 compensation cell enabled above 50 MHz."

5 V-tolerant pins, protection diodes and injection current

▶

A 3.3 V chip normally must not see more than about 3.6 V on any pin. Many STM32 pins are specially built to accept up to 5 V: these are called five-volt tolerant (FT). On other pins, a higher voltage pushes current through an internal protection diode into the 3.3 V supply. That is called injection current, and it can damage the chip or power it up when it should be off.

1. Terms

FT
Five-volt Tolerant pin. On the F4 it accepts up to VDD + 4 V, with an absolute maximum of 5.5 V.
TT / TTa
3.3 V-only pins. "a" = has an analog function (PA4/PA5 are the DAC outputs). Maximum about VDD + 0.3 V.
Injection current (IINJ)
Current forced into a pin through its protection diodes when the voltage goes outside VSS…VDD. F4 limits: ±5 mA per normal pin, −5/+0 mA on FT pins (no positive injection allowed), ±25 mA total.
Phantom (back) powering
When injection current through the VDD diode raises an unpowered chip's VDD rail enough for it to partly run.
Latch-up
A parasitic thyristor inside CMOS that, once triggered by injection, shorts VDD to ground until power is removed. It can destroy the chip.

2. What happens when 5 V meets a normal (non-FT) pin, step by step

  1. A 5 V sensor output connects directly to PA4 (a TTa pin) on a 3.3 V MCU.
  2. The pin rises above VDD + ≈0.6 V, about 3.9 V, and the upper ESD diode starts conducting.
  3. Current flows: sensor 5 V → its output resistance (say 50 Ω) → diode → VDD rail. I ≈ (5 − 3.9) / 50 Ω ≈ 22 mA, more than four times the 5 mA limit.
  4. If the 3.3 V regulator cannot sink current (most can't), the injected current lifts the 3.3 V rail.
  5. If the MCU is powered off while the sensor is on, the MCU is now powered through its pin: phantom powering. It may half-boot, corrupt flash, or latch up.

3. Worked example: making it safe with a series resistor

Add 4.7 kΩ between the 5 V signal and the TTa pin. Injection current = (5 − 3.9) / 4700 ≈ 0.23 mA, well within the 5 mA limit. With 10 such pins, the total is 2.3 mA, within the 25 mA total. Speed cost: 4.7 kΩ × 5 pF pin capacitance = 23.5 ns time constant, fine for slow signals.

Even better: use an FT pin, which needs no resistor for 5 V as long as the caveats below are respected.

4. The four FT caveats people miss

  1. Not in analog mode. In analog mode (MODER = 11) an FT pin loses its tolerance, because the analog switch connects it to circuitry that is not 5 V safe.
  2. Internal pull-up and pull-down must be off to hold more than VDD + 0.3 V (F4 datasheet note). With the pull-up on, current flows from the 5 V pin back through the ≈ 40 kΩ resistor into VDD.
  3. Push-pull outputs only reach VDD. To output a 5 V high you must use open-drain plus an external pull-up to 5 V.
  4. VDD = 0 V: whether the pin tolerates 5 V while the MCU is unpowered depends on the datasheet notes. TTa pins certainly do not.

Reset state of pins and boot glitches: why enables need pull resistors

▶

From power-on until your code configures a pin, that pin is not under your control. On the STM32F4 it is a floating input. If it controls something dangerous (a relay, a motor driver, a heater), that something can turn on by itself during start-up. The only reliable fix is a resistor on the board that holds the safe level when the MCU is not driving.

1. The timeline from power-on to your GPIO init

  1. VDD ramp + power-on reset: the internal reset is held until VDD is valid, plus a delay (tRSTTEMPO ≈ 2.5 ms on the F4). All pins are in their reset configuration.
  2. External reset supervisor may hold NRST low for up to 200 ms.
  3. Bootloader (yours, or the ST ROM bootloader if BOOT0 is high) runs. It may never touch your pin, and it can take seconds while waiting for a firmware update.
  4. Reset_Handler copies .data, zeroes .bss, and SystemInit configures the PLL.
  5. main() runs HAL_Init and clock setup, and finally the GPIO init configures the pin.

Total: typically 5–300 ms, and unbounded during a firmware update. A brown-out (VDD dipping below the BOR threshold) resets the chip and starts the whole window again, even in the middle of operation.

2. What a floating gate does

A low-side N-MOSFET driving a relay, with the gate connected directly to PB12. The gate is a capacitor (say 1 nF). Leakage of ±1 µA charges it at 1 µA / 1 nF = 1 V per ms. A gate threshold of 1.5 V can be reached within 1.5 ms of floating. Capacitive coupling from the 12 V supply ramp through the drain–gate capacitance (Miller capacitance) can also kick the gate up at power-on. The relay clicks.

3. The fix: hardware first, firmware second

✖ NOT ENOUGH — firmware only
int main(void){
  // "first thing in main"
  GPIOB->BSRR  = 1u << (12+16);
  GPIOB->MODER |= 1u << 24;
  ...
}
// still floats during POR, NRST,
// the bootloader and startup code
✔ RIGHT — pull-down + early init
// HARDWARE: 100 kΩ gate→GND (or a
// pull-down on an active-high EN)
// FIRMWARE: as early as possible, and
// in the right order:
void SystemInit(void){
  RCC->AHB1ENR |= RCC_AHB1ENR_GPIOBEN;
  (void)RCC->AHB1ENR;
  GPIOB->BSRR  = 1u << (12+16); // ODR=0 first
  GPIOB->MODER = (GPIOB->MODER & ~(3u<<24))
               | (1u << 24);     // then output
  ...
}

4. Design rules

  • Every enable, gate, chip select and strap pin gets an external pull to its safe state. Internal pulls are not active during reset.
  • Choose the polarity so that "pulled" means "safe". Power stages: active-high enable with a pull-down. Chip selects: active-low with a pull-up.
  • Know the debug pins: PA13 (pull-up), PA14 (pull-down), PA15 (pull-up), PB4 (pull-up) are active at reset. Don't use PA15 for an active-high enable: it is pulled high from the moment of reset.
  • Know what the bootloader does with pins. The ST ROM bootloader drives USART/CAN/USB pins as AF while scanning.
  • Shift-register outputs (74HC595) are random at power-up. Hold OE# high with a resistor until the first valid latch.

Level shifting basics

▶

A level shifter (or level translator) connects two chips that run at different voltages, for example a 3.3 V MCU and a 5 V or 1.8 V sensor. It converts the high level of one side into the high level of the other, without overvoltage on either side. Which method you use depends on the direction, the speed, and whether the signal is push-pull or open-drain.

1. Decision procedure, step by step

  1. Is the signal unidirectional or bidirectional? SPI, UART and most GPIO are one-way. I²C SDA and 1-Wire are two-way.
  2. Push-pull or open-drain? I²C is open-drain; it needs a shifter that works with pull-ups.
  3. Going up or down? High-to-low can be a resistor divider. Low-to-high needs a receiver that accepts the low side's VOH.
  4. How fast? A divider or BSS138 is limited by RC; buffer ICs reach 100+ Mbps.
  5. Can a pin do it for free? An FT pin reads 5 V directly. An FT pin in open-drain with a pull-up to 5 V outputs 5 V levels.

Keypad matrix scanning, ghosting and diodes

▶

A keypad matrix puts buttons at the crossings of row wires and column wires. That way 16 keys need only 8 pins instead of 16. The MCU finds pressed keys by activating one row at a time and checking which columns respond. With several keys pressed, current can sneak through other keys and make a key look pressed when it isn't. That is called ghosting.

1. Terms

Row / column
The two sets of wires. Each key connects one row to one column when pressed.
Scan
Visit each row in turn, drive it active, read all columns, then release it.
Ghosting
A phantom key reported because current flowed through three real pressed keys.
N-key rollover
Every combination of pressed keys is reported correctly.

2. How a scan works, step by step (4×4, rows PB0–3, columns PB4–7)

  1. Columns are inputs with pull-ups, so they read 1 when no key connects them to an active row.
  2. All rows are open-drain outputs, released (hi-Z).
  3. Pull row 0 low (BSRR reset bit 0).
  4. Wait about 1–5 µs for the column lines to settle. Pull-up × wiring capacitance: 40 kΩ × 20 pF = 0.8 µs.
  5. Read the columns: cols = ~(GPIOB->IDR >> 4) & 0xF. A 1 in bit c means key (row 0, col c) is pressed.
  6. Release row 0 and repeat for rows 1–3. A full scan every 5–10 ms feeds a per-key debouncer.
Key (row 2, col 1) pressed. During row 2's turn, column 1 is connected to the low row, so IDR bit 5 = 0. cols = ~(0b1101) & 0xF = 0b0010, so col 1. Key index = row × 4 + col = 2 × 4 + 1 = 9.

3. Ghosting, step by step

  1. Keys (R0,C0), (R0,C1) and (R1,C0) are pressed: three corners of a rectangle.
  2. The scan drives R1 low. C0 goes low through (R1,C0): correct.
  3. But current also flows R1 → (R1,C0) → C0 → (R0,C0) backwards → R0 → (R0,C1) → C1. Every pressed key conducts in both directions.
  4. C1 reads low as well, so the firmware reports (R1,C1) pressed even though it isn't: a ghost.

4. Fixes

  • A diode in series with every key: current can only flow from column to row. The backward step 3 is blocked, giving true N-key rollover. Gaming keyboards do this.
  • Ghost masking in firmware: if three corners of a rectangle are seen, refuse to report the ambiguous fourth key. Cheap keyboards do this ("2-key rollover").
  • Open-drain rows, or switching inactive rows to input. If inactive rows are driven high push-pull, two keys in the same column on different rows short a high row to the low row: contention.

5. Code: wrong vs right row drive

✖ WRONG — push-pull rows
// rows push-pull, inactive rows HIGH
GPIOB->ODR = 0x0F & ~(1u << r);
// keys (r,c) and (r2,c) pressed:
// row r (0 V) shorted to row r2 (3.3 V)
// through the column → 33 mA fight
✔ RIGHT — open-drain rows
// OTYPER bits 0–3 = 1 (open-drain)
GPIOB->BSRR = 0x000F;              // release all
GPIOB->BSRR = 1u << (r + 16);     // pull row r low
delay_us(2);                       // settle
uint8_t c = ~(GPIOB->IDR >> 4) & 0xF;

Low-power trick: pull all rows low, enable EXTI on the columns, and sleep. Any key press pulls a column low and wakes the MCU, which then scans normally.

No settle delay between driving a row and reading the columns. At 168 MHz the read happens within about 10 ns, before the column has discharged through the pressed key and wiring capacitance. Symptom: the key appears one row late (it is detected when the next row is driven), or presses are intermittent.
"A matrix uses R+C pins for R×C keys: columns are pulled-up inputs, and I pull one row low at a time through an open-drain output, wait a microsecond or two, and read which columns went low. With three keys at the corners of a rectangle, current sneaks backwards through a pressed key and the fourth corner ghosts. A diode per key blocks the reverse path for N-key rollover; otherwise firmware masks ambiguous patterns. The scan runs every few milliseconds into a per-key debouncer."

Charlieplexing: n·(n−1) LEDs from n pins using tristate

▶

Charlieplexing drives many LEDs from few pins by using a third pin state: besides high and low, a pin can be an input, which is effectively disconnected (hi-Z). An LED sits between every pair of pins in each direction. To light one LED, drive one pin high, one low, and set all the others to input so no current flows through them.

1. Counting LEDs

  1. With n pins there are n × (n − 1) / 2 pairs of pins.
  2. Each pair can hold two LEDs, one in each direction (anti-parallel).
  3. Total = n × (n − 1). n = 3 gives 6, n = 4 gives 12, n = 5 gives 20, n = 10 gives 90.
  4. Compare a plain row/column matrix: 5 pins as 2 × 3 = 6 LEDs. 10 pins as 5 × 5 = 25.

2. Three-pin example: lighting LED P0→P1

MODER: P0 = output, P1 = output, P2 = INPUT (hi-Z)
ODR:   P0 = 1,      P1 = 0
Current: P0 → LED(P0→P1) → P1.  LED(P1→P0) is reverse-biased. Nothing flows through P2.

3. Multiplexing and brightness

Only one LED (or the group sharing one high pin) can be lit at a time, so the code cycles through them fast enough that the eye sees all of them on.

20 LEDs on 5 pins, each lit one at a time. For no visible flicker each LED needs at least about 100 Hz refresh, so the step rate is 20 × 100 = 2 kHz: a timer ISR every 500 µs. Duty cycle per LED = 1/20 = 5%. To look as bright as a steady 2 mA LED you need a peak of about 20 × 2 = 40 mA, which exceeds the pin limit. So in practice you light one high pin's whole group at once (up to n − 1 LEDs) for 1/n duty, keep the per-pin total under about 20 mA, and accept dimmer LEDs.

4. Code: one refresh step

typedef struct { uint8_t hi, lo; } cled_t;
static const cled_t MAP[6] = {{0,1},{1,0},{0,2},{2,0},{1,2},{2,1}}; // pins on PA0–PA2
void charlie_show(uint8_t i) {                       // called from a timer ISR
    uint32_t m = GPIOA->MODER & ~0x3Fu;              // pins 0–2 → input (all hi-Z)
    GPIOA->MODER = m;                                // step 1: everything off first
    GPIOA->BSRR  = (1u << MAP[i].hi) | (1u << (MAP[i].lo + 16)); // preload levels
    GPIOA->MODER = m | (1u << (2*MAP[i].hi)) | (1u << (2*MAP[i].lo)); // step 2: two outputs, ONE write
}

Turning everything off first, then enabling both outputs in a single 32-bit MODER write, avoids a transient moment where the wrong LED lights (ghost flashes).

Sneak paths: with P0 high and P1 low, current could also flow P0 → LED → P2 → LED → P1, through two LEDs in series. If 2 × VF < VDD, those two glow faintly. Red LEDs at 1.8 V on 3.3 V: 3.6 V > 3.3 V, so OK. IR LEDs (VF 1.2 V) on 3.3 V: 2.4 V < 3.3 V, so ghosting. Also, a single shorted LED disturbs many others, which makes field diagnosis hard.
"Charlieplexing uses tristate: with n pins you put an LED each way between every pair, giving n(n−1) LEDs, so 20 from 5 pins. To light one, drive its anode pin high, its cathode pin low, and every other pin to input. Since only one pin pair is active at a time you multiplex, so brightness drops with the duty cycle, and you need 2·VF above VDD to avoid sneak-path glow."

Shift registers: 74HC595 (more outputs) and 74HC165 (more inputs) over SPI

▶

A shift register converts between serial (one bit after another on one wire) and parallel (many bits on many wires at once). The 74HC595 takes serial data from the MCU and presents it on 8 output pins. The 74HC165 reads 8 input pins and sends them back serially. Both chain together, so 3 MCU pins can control or read dozens of signals.

1. 74HC595 pins

SER
Serial data in.
SRCLK
Shift-register clock. On each rising edge, all bits move one place and SER enters at the start.
RCLK
Register (latch) clock. A rising edge copies the shift register to the output storage register. The outputs change only now.
OE#
Output enable, active low. High = outputs hi-Z.
SRCLR#
Clears the shift register when low.
QA…QH
The 8 parallel outputs. QH' is the serial output used to chain the next chip.

2. Sending one byte, step by step

  1. Put bit 7 on SER, pulse SRCLK. Then bit 6, pulse, and so on down to bit 0. That is 8 pulses.
  2. After 8 clocks, the first bit sent (bit 7) has travelled to the last stage, which is QH. Bit 0 sits in QA.
  3. During shifting, the outputs do not change: they are held by the storage register.
  4. Pulse RCLK: all 8 outputs update at the same instant. No ripple, no glitches on the loads.
  5. With two chips chained (QH' → SER of chip 2), send 16 bits. The first byte sent ends up in the far chip.
Want outputs QA=1, QB=0, QC=1, others 0 → byte (QH…QA) = 0b0000_0101 = 0x05. SPI sends MSB first: bits 0,0,0,0,0,1,0,1. After 8 clocks, the first bit sent (0) is in QH and the last bit sent (1) is in QA. ✔. With SPI at 10 MHz, 8 bits take 0.8 µs, plus the latch pulse.

3. Mapping onto SPI

MCU SPI1   MOSI ─► SER        SCK ─► SRCLK      GPIO PB6 ─► RCLK
SPI mode 0 (CPOL=0, CPHA=0), MSB first. 74HC595 shifts on SRCLK rising edge = mode 0 sample edge.

4. 74HC165: reading 8 inputs

  1. Pull SH/LD# low: the 8 inputs A–H are copied into the register immediately (asynchronous load).
  2. Set SH/LD# high: the register is now in shift mode.
  3. Input H is already present on QH before any clock. Read it first.
  4. Each CLK rising edge shifts the next bit (G, F, …) onto QH. CLK INH must be low.
  5. With SPI mode 0, the MCU samples MISO on the first rising edge, before the chip shifts, so the first bit read is H. ✔ Load must be done before SCK starts.

5. Electrical limits and power-up

  • 74HC595 outputs: ±6 mA specified at 4.5 V; 35 mA absolute max per pin, 70 mA total through VCC/GND. For LEDs or relays use the TPIC6B595 (open-drain DMOS outputs, 150 mA sink each).
  • Clock rate: tens of MHz at 5 V, much less at 2 V. Check the datasheet's fmax at your supply.
  • Power-up state is random. Pull OE# high with a resistor (outputs hi-Z) and pull-downs on critical outputs, then enable OE# after the first valid latch.
You pulse RCLK as soon as TXE is set. TXE only means the data register has been moved into the shift logic; the bits are still going out. Symptom: outputs show the previous byte, or a half-shifted value, especially at low SPI clock rates.
"A 74HC595 is serial-in, parallel-out with a separate latch. I shift 8 bits per chip over SPI MOSI and SCK, wait for SPI BSY to clear, then pulse RCLK so all outputs change together. Chips daisy-chain through QH'. The 74HC165 is the reverse: pulse SH/LD# low to load the inputs, and H is already on QH before the first clock. Watch the 6 mA output rating and the random power-up state, which needs OE# pulled high."

Quadrature encoder decoding: state table, ×4 counting, hardware encoder mode

▶

A rotary encoder reports rotation with two square-wave signals, A and B, shifted a quarter cycle (90°) apart: "in quadrature". By watching which signal changes first, you learn the direction. By counting changes, you learn how far it turned.

1. Terms

PPR / CPR
Pulses (lines) per revolution: full cycles of A per turn. With ×4 decoding you get 4 counts per pulse.
Gray code
A sequence where only one bit changes per step. The AB pair follows 00 → 01 → 11 → 10, which is Gray code.
×1 / ×2 / ×4
Counting one edge per cycle, both edges of A, or every edge of A and B.
Invalid transition
Both bits changing at once (00↔11 or 01↔10). It cannot happen physically if every state was seen, so it means a sample was missed.

2. The waveforms and states

Our convention (used in the table, the FSM tab and the Q&A): +1 for 00 → 01 → 11 → 10 → 00 (B leads), and −1 for the reverse. Which physical direction is "clockwise" depends on wiring. Swap A and B to flip it.

3. Decoding with a 16-entry table, step by step

  1. Read A and B and form curr = (A<<1) | B, a number 0–3.
  2. Form an index from the previous and current states: idx = (prev<<2) | curr, a number 0–15.
  3. Look up the step: pos += QDEC[idx].
  4. Set prev = curr.
static const int8_t QDEC[16] = {
 /* curr:   00  01  10  11      prev */
             0, +1, -1,  0,  /* 00: 00→01 = +1, 00→10 = −1, 00→11 = invalid */
            -1,  0,  0, +1,  /* 01: 01→00 = −1, 01→10 = invalid, 01→11 = +1 */
            +1,  0,  0, -1,  /* 10: 10→00 = +1, 10→01 = invalid, 10→11 = −1 */
             0, -1, +1,  0   /* 11: 11→00 = invalid, 11→01 = −1, 11→10 = +1 */
};
prev = 01 (1), curr = 11 (3). idx = (1 << 2) | 3 = 4 + 3 = 7. QDEC[7] = +1. ✔ (01 → 11 is a forward step.)
prev = 10 (2), curr = 01 (1). idx = 8 + 1 = 9. QDEC[9] = 0, an invalid transition: both bits changed, so we missed a state. A robust decoder also counts errors at indices 3, 6, 9 and 12.

4. How fast must you sample? (the critical calculation)

600 PPR encoder at 3000 rpm with ×4 decoding. Revolutions per second = 3000 / 60 = 50. Counts per second = 600 × 4 × 50 = 120 000. Time per state = 1 / 120 000 = 8.3 µs. To see every state you must sample faster than every 8.3 µs (in practice ≤ 4 µs for margin, because the phase is never exactly 90°). A 1 ms SysTick poll is 120 × too slow.

5. What missed samples do (aliasing)

  1. Miss 0 states: each step is ±1. Correct.
  2. Miss 1 state: the decoder sees two bits change, an invalid transition. Count +0 (lost) or flagged.
  3. Miss 2 states: three steps forward look identical to one step backward. The count goes −1 instead of +3. The position runs backwards at high speed.
  4. This is the quadrature version of the Nyquist rule: sample faster than the state rate, or the direction aliases.

6. Do it in hardware: STM32 timer encoder mode

RCC->APB1ENR |= RCC_APB1ENR_TIM3EN;        // TIM3, CH1 = PA6 (A), CH2 = PA7 (B), AF2
TIM3->CCMR1 = (1u << 0) | (1u << 8)        // CC1S = 01 (TI1), CC2S = 01 (TI2)
            | (0xFu << 4) | (0xFu << 12);   // IC1F = IC2F = 1111: strongest digital filter
TIM3->CCER  = 0;                            // no inversion (CC1P = CC2P = 0)
TIM3->SMCR  = 3u;                           // SMS = 011: encoder mode 3 = count on TI1 AND TI2 edges (×4)
TIM3->ARR   = 0xFFFF;                       // full 16-bit range
TIM3->CR1   = TIM_CR1_CEN;                  // start

int16_t delta = (int16_t)(TIM3->CNT - last);   // signed 16-bit difference handles wrap
last = TIM3->CNT; position += delta;

The timer counts every edge in hardware with no CPU involvement, filters glitches, and keeps direction in CR1.DIR. Software only needs to read CNT often enough that it moves less than 32768 counts between reads.

Mechanical knobs bounce. The state table tolerates a bounce because it becomes +1 then −1, which cancels. Detented knobs usually give 4 counts per click, so divide by 4.

Polling an encoder in a 1 ms tick. Symptoms: correct at slow speed; at high speed the count lags, then jumps backwards; position drifts every run; the error counter climbs. Root cause: missed states (sampling slower than the state rate).
"A and B are 90° apart and walk a Gray-code sequence, so I decode with a 16-entry table indexed by (prev<<2)|curr giving +1, −1 or 0, where a two-bit change means a missed sample. The sampling rate must exceed the state rate, PPR × 4 × revolutions per second. At 600 PPR and 3000 rpm that's 120 k states/s, so polling at 1 ms is hopeless, and skipping two states even reverses the count. On STM32 I use the timer's encoder mode (SMS = 011) with the input filter, and just read CNT with a signed 16-bit difference."

PWM on GPIO vs timer, bit-banging, timing jitter and toggle-speed limits

▶

Software can make a pin do almost anything by toggling it at the right moments. That is "bit-banging". But software timing is disturbed by interrupts and memory delays, so the edges wobble (jitter). Hardware peripherals (timers, SPI, DMA) produce exact timing with no CPU. This card puts numbers on both.

1. Terms

PWM
Pulse-Width Modulation: a square wave whose high-time fraction (the duty cycle) represents a value, for example LED brightness or motor power.
Bit-banging
Implementing a protocol (SPI, I²C, WS2812, UART) by toggling GPIOs in software instead of using a peripheral.
Jitter
Variation in when an edge occurs compared with when it should.
HCLK
The AHB bus clock. On the F407 it is up to 168 MHz, and it clocks the GPIO ports.
DWT→CYCCNT
A free-running CPU cycle counter in the Cortex-M4 debug unit. Useful for precise software delays.

Software: 8-bit resolution (256 steps) at 1 kHz PWM means an ISR at 256 × 1 kHz = 256 kHz, every 3.9 µs. Each ISR entry + exit + body ≈ 40 cycles = 0.24 µs, so about 6% of the CPU for one channel. Timer: TIM3 at 84 MHz, PSC = 0, ARR = 255 gives 84 MHz / 256 = 328 kHz PWM with 8 bits. Duty = CCR1 / 256. Edges are exact to 12 ns. CPU = 0%.

4. Bit-banging: which protocols tolerate it?

  1. Synchronous, master-clocked (SPI, I²C, 74HC595): the receiver samples on your clock edge. Jitter just makes the transfer slower. Safe to bit-bang.
  2. Asynchronous / self-timed (UART, 1-Wire, WS2812, DHT22): the receiver times the pulses itself. Jitter corrupts data.
  3. WS2812 example: a "0" bit is 0.4 µs high and 0.85 µs low, a "1" bit is 0.8 µs high and 0.45 µs low, with about ±150 ns tolerance. A single 1 µs SysTick interrupt in the middle of a bit breaks the frame.

For those I repurpose SPI or timer+DMA, or as a last resort disable interrupts for a short burst timed with DWT->CYCCNT."

Combinational vs sequential logic for firmware engineers: latches, flip-flops, setup/hold, clock domains

▶

All digital hardware is built from two kinds of logic. Combinational logic computes outputs from its current inputs only (gates, adders). Sequential logic also remembers: its output depends on past inputs, stored in latches and flip-flops. Firmware engineers need this to understand wait states, clock domains, synchronisers and FSMs.

1. Terms

Propagation delay tpd
Time from an input change to the resulting output change of a gate or logic path.
Glitch / hazard
A brief wrong output while inputs change at slightly different times.
Latch
Level-sensitive storage. A D latch is "transparent" (output follows input) while its enable is high, and holds while it is low.
Flip-flop (FF)
Edge-triggered storage. A D flip-flop copies D to Q only at the clock's rising edge.
Setup time tsu
How long D must be stable before the clock edge.
Hold time th
How long D must stay stable after the clock edge.
Clock-to-Q tcq
Delay from the clock edge to Q changing.
Clock domain
All logic driven by one clock. Signals passing between domains with unrelated clocks are "asynchronous" to the receiver.

2. Latch vs flip-flop, on one timeline

CLK/EN  ____|‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾|________|‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾|____
D       __|‾‾|__|‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾|____|‾|_____
Latch Q ____|‾|__|‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾|__|‾|____   ← follows D while EN high (glitches pass!)
FF Q    ____|‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾|____________   ← samples D only at rising edges

3. Timing of one clocked path, step by step

  1. A clock edge arrives at the launching flip-flop. After tcq, its Q output changes.
  2. The signal travels through combinational logic, taking tpd.
  3. It must arrive at the next flip-flop's D at least tsu before the next clock edge.
  4. So the clock period must satisfy Tclk ≥ tcq + tpd + tsu (plus clock skew).
  5. Separately, the fastest path must not change D within th after the edge (the hold check).
Why the F4 needs flash wait states. Flash read access time is about 30 ns. At 168 MHz, Tclk = 5.95 ns. The "combinational path" (the flash array read) is far longer than one clock, so the flash interface must wait. ⌈30 / 5.95⌉ = 6 clocks, so 5 wait states (FLASH_ACR.LATENCY = 5) at 3.3 V. Same formula, real consequence: the ART accelerator and prefetch exist to hide those wait states.

4. Where a firmware engineer meets clock domains

  • RTC: clocked by the 32.768 kHz LSE crystal, while the CPU runs at 168 MHz. Its registers are read through shadow registers, and after wake-up you must wait for RSF (registers synchronised) before reading.
  • Peripheral writes through bridges: an APB write can still be in flight when the next instruction runs. Clear an interrupt flag at the end of an ISR and it may re-fire, because the clear hasn't arrived yet.
  • Reading a free-running counter in another domain: bits can change mid-read. Read twice until two reads match, or use the peripheral's latch.
  • GPIO inputs: already synchronised for you (card 25). A multi-bit parallel bus read from IDR is not coherent if the source changes during the read.
You use a level-sensitive latch-like design (for example an enable signal gating a register) and a glitch on the data input while the enable is high passes straight through to the output. Or, in firmware: you read a 32-bit timer as two 16-bit halves from another clock domain. The low half wraps between the reads and you get a value 65536 off.
"Combinational logic is a pure function of its current inputs with some propagation delay; sequential logic stores state in latches, which are level-sensitive and transparent, or flip-flops, which sample on a clock edge. A flip-flop needs D stable for setup time before and hold time after the edge, so the clock period has to cover clock-to-Q plus logic delay plus setup. That's exactly why flash needs wait states at high clock. Signals between unrelated clock domains will eventually violate setup/hold, so they need synchronisers."

Metastability, synchronisers (2-FF) and glitch filtering

▶

If a flip-flop's input changes at almost exactly the moment the clock samples it, the flip-flop can get "stuck" halfway between 0 and 1 for an unpredictable time before falling one way. That is metastability. It cannot be prevented when sampling a signal from another clock domain, but it can be made astronomically rare by giving the flip-flop time to settle: the 2-flip-flop synchroniser.

1. Why it happens, step by step

  1. Inside a flip-flop, two cross-coupled inverters form a bistable loop, like a ball on a hill with a valley on each side (0 and 1).
  2. Normally the input pushes the ball clearly into one valley before the clock closes the loop.
  3. If D changes inside the setup/hold window, the ball can be left balanced near the hilltop.
  4. It eventually rolls into one valley, but the time is random, with an exponentially decaying probability of still being undecided.
  5. Meanwhile Q may sit at a mid-level voltage that downstream gates read differently (one sees 1, another 0), or it may switch late, after the next stage has already sampled it.

2. How rare? The MTBF formula

MTBF = e(tr/τ) / (T0 · fclk · fdata)
  tr   = resolution time allowed before the next stage samples (≈ one clock period minus logic)
  τ, T0 = flip-flop constants from the silicon vendor (τ is tens of ps in modern CMOS)
  fclk = sampling clock,  fdata = rate of async input changes
Illustrative numbers: τ = 50 ps, T0 = 100 ps, fclk = 100 MHz, fdata = 1 MHz.
  • One flip-flop, with logic after it leaving tr = 1 ns: e1000/50 = e20 ≈ 4.9 × 108. Denominator = 10−10 × 108 × 106 = 104. MTBF ≈ 4.9 × 104 s ≈ 13 hours. A failure every half day!
  • Two flip-flops: the second adds about a full 10 ns period, so tr ≈ 11 ns. e220 ≈ 1095, so MTBF ≈ 1091 s. That is far longer than the age of the universe (4 × 1017 s).

3. The 2-FF synchroniser

  1. FF1 samples the asynchronous input and may go metastable.
  2. FF1 gets a whole clock period to resolve before FF2 samples it.
  3. FF2's output is clean with overwhelming probability. Cost: 1–2 clock cycles of latency.
  4. Rule: the raw async signal must feed exactly one flip-flop (FF1). Nothing else may look at it.

4. Wrong vs right (Verilog-style, as on an FPGA or CPLD)

  case (st)
   IDLE: if (btn) st <= RUN;  // btn async!
   ...

// through different logic delays → one
// bit flips, the other doesn't → ILLEGAL
// state (e.g. 2'b11)
✔ RIGHT — synchronise first
reg s1, s2;
always @(posedge clk) begin
  s1 <= btn;   // FF1 (only reader of btn)
  s2 <= s1;    // FF2
end
// FSM uses s2 only, then debounce s2,
// and add a default: st <= IDLE;

5. Multi-bit signals need different tools

Synchronising each bit of a bus separately is wrong. If several bits change together, skew means some flops see the new value and some the old, so the result is a mixture that never existed (for example counter 0111 → 1000 read as 1111).

  • Gray code: only one bit changes per step, so a mixture is always either the old or the new value. Used for async FIFO pointers.
  • Handshake: hold the data stable, send a single-bit "valid" through a synchroniser, and get an "ack" back the same way.
  • Async FIFO for streams.

6. What this means on an MCU

  • GPIO inputs are synchronised into IDR by ST's hardware, so reading one pin is safe.
  • Reading an 8-bit parallel bus from IDR while the source is changing is the multi-bit problem. Use a strobe/handshake, or read until two reads agree.
  • Glitch filtering is a different problem. A synchroniser passes a clean but real 50 ns spike. To reject spikes use N-sample agreement (a debouncer), STM32 timer input filters (ICxF: requires N consecutive equal samples at fDTS/k), the I²C analog filter (rejects < 50 ns) and digital filter (DNF), or an RC.
An FPGA samples an asynchronous request line directly into its state-machine logic. Symptom: once every few hours or days, the FSM locks up in a state that "can't be reached", or two outputs that should be mutually exclusive are both on. It is never reproducible on the bench, and the error rate rises with clock frequency and temperature.
"Metastability happens when a flip-flop samples an input that changes inside its setup/hold window: the output can hover or resolve late, and different gates may read it differently. It can't be prevented, only made improbable, because MTBF grows exponentially with resolution time. So every async single-bit input goes through a 2-flip-flop synchroniser, and the raw signal feeds only the first flop. Multi-bit values need Gray code, a handshake or an async FIFO. On an MCU, GPIO is already synchronised, but glitches still need filtering."

Finite state machines: Moore vs Mealy, state encoding and C implementations

▶

A finite state machine (FSM) is a system that is always in exactly one of a fixed set of states, and moves between them in response to inputs (events) according to fixed rules. Traffic lights, protocol parsers, button handlers and encoder decoders are all FSMs. Writing them explicitly turns tangled if/else flags into something you can draw, review and test.

1. Terms

State
A situation the system can be in, for example GREEN, YELLOW, RED.
Event / input
Something that can cause a change: a timer expiry, a button press, a received byte.
Transition
A rule: "in state S, on event E, go to state S'".
Moore machine
Outputs depend only on the current state. Example: "in RED, the red lamp is on".
Mealy machine
Outputs depend on state and input, so they belong to transitions. Example: "on TIMER in RED, start the 20 s timer".
Encoding
How states are stored as bits: binary, one-hot or Gray.

2. Classic comparison: a "101" sequence detector (overlapping)

Input: one bit per clock. Output 1 whenever the last three bits were 1, 0, 1.

state  meaning    out  in=0  in=1

3. Implementing an FSM in C: three styles

A. switch/case: the most readable; the compiler turns it into a jump table.

typedef enum { S_GREEN, S_YELLOW, S_RED, S_N } state_t;
typedef enum { EV_TIMER, EV_PED, EV_N } event_t;
static state_t s = S_RED;
void fsm_step(event_t e) {
    switch (s) {
    case S_GREEN:  if (e == EV_PED)   { lamps(Y); timer(3);  s = S_YELLOW; } break;  // Mealy actions
    case S_YELLOW: if (e == EV_TIMER) { lamps(R); timer(10); s = S_RED;    } break;
    case S_RED:    if (e == EV_TIMER) { lamps(G);            s = S_GREEN;  } break;
    default:       lamps(R); s = S_RED; break;   // corrupted state → safe state
    }
}

B. table-driven: the behaviour is data. Easy to review against a diagram, to unit-test exhaustively, and to generate.

typedef struct { uint8_t next; void (*act)(void); } cell_t;
static const cell_t FSM[S_N][EV_N] = {
    /*            EV_TIMER                 EV_PED                */
    [S_GREEN]  = { {S_GREEN,  NULL     }, {S_YELLOW, go_yellow} },
    [S_YELLOW] = { {S_RED,    go_red   }, {S_YELLOW, NULL     } },
    [S_RED]    = { {S_GREEN,  go_green }, {S_RED,    NULL     } },
};
void fsm_step(event_t e) {
    if (s >= S_N || e >= EV_N) { s = S_RED; return; }  // range check = robustness
    const cell_t *c = &FSM[s][e];
    if (c->act) c->act();                            // Mealy action on the transition
    s = c->next;
}

C. function pointer per state: each state is a function, which scales to large machines and gives natural entry/exit hooks.

typedef struct fsm fsm_t;
typedef void (*state_fn)(fsm_t *, event_t);
struct fsm { state_fn st; };
static void st_red(fsm_t *m, event_t e);
static void st_green(fsm_t *m, event_t e) { if (e == EV_PED)   { go_yellow(); m->st = st_yellow; } }
static void st_yellow(fsm_t *m, event_t e){ if (e == EV_TIMER) { go_red();    m->st = st_red;    } }
static void st_red(fsm_t *m, event_t e)   { if (e == EV_TIMER) { go_green();  m->st = st_green;  } }
void fsm_dispatch(fsm_t *m, event_t e) { m->st(m, e); }

5. Running FSMs in a real system

  1. ISRs do not run the FSM. They post events to a queue: button pressed, timer expired, byte received.
  2. One task or main loop takes events from the queue and calls fsm_step() to completion, one at a time ("run-to-completion"). No reentrancy, no shared-state races.
  3. Log every transition (state, event, next) to a ring buffer. It is invaluable for field debugging.
  4. For large machines with repeated transitions, use hierarchical state machines (UML statecharts, for example the QP framework): substates inherit their parent's transitions.
An FSM table with undefined cells left as zero, so the "missing" transition silently jumps to state 0, which might be "MOTOR_ON". Or the FSM is called from both an ISR and main: an ISR event arrives while main is halfway through a transition, and the state variable is overwritten with an inconsistent value.
"An FSM is a set of states, events and transitions. In a Moore machine outputs depend only on the state; in a Mealy machine they depend on state plus input, so they live on transitions. Mealy usually needs fewer states and reacts a step earlier: a 101 detector is three states Mealy versus four Moore. In C I use a switch for small machines, a const table of {next, action} for reviewable, testable ones, and a function pointer per state for big ones. I always handle invalid states and drive the machine from an event queue."

Tristate, bus contention and how to detect it

▶

On a shared wire, exactly one device may drive at a time; all others must be disconnected (tristated). If two devices drive opposite levels at once, they short the supply through their output transistors. That is bus contention. It wastes power, corrupts data, and slowly damages parts.

1. Terms

Tristate output
An output with three states: drive 1, drive 0, and high impedance (Z, disconnected). An output-enable signal chooses between driving and Z.
Bus
A wire or group of wires shared by several devices: a parallel memory bus, SPI MISO with several slaves, a bidirectional data line.
Turnaround
A deliberate idle gap when the driving device changes, so the old driver is off before the new one turns on.
Bus keeper
A weak latch that holds the last driven level when nobody drives, preventing a floating bus.

2. What happens during contention, step by step

  1. Device A's P-MOS drives high (≈ 50 Ω to 3.3 V). Device B's N-MOS drives low (≈ 50 Ω to GND).
  2. Current flows VDD → A's P-MOS → wire → B's N-MOS → GND: I = 3.3 V / (50 + 50) Ω = 33 mA. That exceeds the 25 mA per-pin absolute maximum.
  3. The wire voltage is the divider point: 3.3 × 50/100 = 1.65 V, right in the undefined band. Receivers read random values.
  4. The supply sees a 33 mA spike per contending line. Eight lines of a data bus give 264 mA, enough to droop the rail and reset the chip.
  5. Repeated contention heats the output transistors and causes electromigration, so the pins fail weeks later.

3. Classic causes

  • Two SPI slaves' chip selects asserted together (a firmware bug, or both CS pins floating low at reset).
  • A slave that does not tristate MISO when deselected (some SD cards before initialisation, some cheap modules).
  • A GPIO left as an output on a line another chip also drives.
  • A bidirectional bus switching direction without a turnaround gap.
  • A 74HC245 transceiver's DIR changed while OE# is active.
  • I²C or another open-drain bus misconfigured as push-pull.

4. Detecting it

Add a 33 Ω series resistor at the MCU end of the shared line during bring-up. With the MCU driving 1 and another chip driving 0, the current is about 3.3 / (50 + 33 + 50) = 25 mA, and the voltage across the 33 Ω is 0.8 V: clearly visible on a scope as a step between the two sides of the resistor. Without contention, the drop is near 0 V. The series resistor also limits the damage.
/* Firmware self-test: compare what we drive with what the pin actually is */
GPIOB->BSRR = 1u << 3;                       // drive PB3 high
__NOP(); __NOP(); __NOP(); __NOP();          // let it settle (and pass the IDR synchroniser)
if (!(GPIOB->IDR & (1u << 3)))               // pin reads LOW while we drive HIGH
    report_fault(CONTENTION_OR_SHORT_PB3);   // someone is fighting us, or a short to GND
  • On a scope: a level that is neither high nor low (1–2 V on a 3.3 V bus) during the overlap.
  • Supply current spikes that line up with bus activity.
  • Parts warm to the touch with no load.

5. Prevention

  1. Protocol: only one enable active at a time. Make CS lines pull-up so they are inactive during reset.
  2. Turnaround cycles when the driver changes. Disable the old driver before enabling the new one.
  3. Use open-drain where several devices must drive the same line.
  4. Series resistors on shared lines to limit fault current.
  5. Pull resistors or bus keepers so an undriven bus does not float (the opposite problem).
Two SPI slaves both have CS pulled low by default and you power up. Both drive MISO, and the MCU reads garbage from the "first" device. Symptom: one slave works alone and fails when the second board is plugged in; the 3.3 V rail shows dips during SPI reads.
"A shared line must have exactly one active driver. If two push-pull outputs drive opposite levels, current is limited only by their on-resistances, about 33 mA at 3.3 V with 50 Ω each, and the line sits at mid-rail and reads randomly. I prevent it with protocol rules, turnaround gaps, pulled-inactive enables, and open-drain where sharing is intended. I detect it by comparing IDR with what I drive, by looking for mid-level voltages on the scope, or by measuring the drop across a small series resistor."
