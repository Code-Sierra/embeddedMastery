The big picture: how a microcontroller measures and makes voltages

Read this tab top to bottom before anything else. It assumes you know what a voltage is and roughly what a microcontroller is, and nothing more. Every word is defined the first time it appears. By the end you will have followed one real measurement all the way from a battery terminal to a number in C, with every piece of arithmetic shown. The reference part is the STM32F407 (an ARM Cortex-M4 microcontroller from ST running at 168 MHz).

1What problem do ADCs and DACs solve?

The physical world is analog: a battery voltage, a temperature, a current, a sound pressure can take any value and can change at any moment. A microcontroller is digital: it only stores and computes with whole numbers (integers such as 0, 1, 2 … 4095), and it only does anything at the ticks of its clock.

So we need two translators:

  • ADC = Analog-to-Digital Converter. It looks at a voltage on a pin and produces a whole number proportional to it. Example: 1.65 V in → the number 2048 out. This is how firmware "measures" things.
  • DAC = Digital-to-Analog Converter. The reverse: firmware writes a whole number and the DAC produces a proportional voltage on a pin. Example: write 2048 → about 1.65 V comes out. This is how firmware "makes" an analog signal: a test tone, a reference level for a comparator, a set-point for a power supply.

Translating a continuous thing into whole numbers forces two compromises, and nearly everything on this page is about managing them:

  1. We can only look at certain moments. The ADC takes a "snapshot" (a sample) every so often, for example 1000 times a second. Anything the signal does between snapshots is invisible. If the signal changes too fast relative to the snapshot rate, the numbers lie about it (this is aliasing, section 3).
  2. We can only use certain values. A 12-bit ADC has 4096 possible output numbers. A voltage that falls between two of them gets rounded to the nearer one. That rounding is quantisation, and the leftover difference is quantisation error.
Mental picture: an ADC is a ruler (the reference voltage) with 4096 tick marks, and a camera that photographs the signal against that ruler at regular moments. A DAC is the same ruler used backwards: you name a tick mark and it produces that voltage.

2The physical picture: what is actually on the chip

Here is the path a voltage travels inside an STM32F407 when you measure it, and the reverse path for the DAC. Read it left to right.

Each block in words:

  1. The source is whatever you measure. It is never ideal: it has a source impedance (its internal resistance, plus any resistors you put in series). This matters a lot later.
  2. The pin (PA1 here) must be put in analog mode in the GPIO registers (GPIOA->MODER bits = 11). That disconnects the digital input buffer so it does not disturb or get disturbed by the voltage.
  3. The multiplexer (MUX) is a rotary switch. The STM32F407 has three ADCs but each ADC can be connected to any of 16 pins plus three internal signals. Firmware tells it which one to look at next.
  4. The sample-and-hold (S/H): a tiny switch and a tiny capacitor (about 4 pF = 4 × 10−12 farad). While the switch is closed, the capacitor charges up to the pin voltage ("tracking"). Then the switch opens and the capacitor "holds" that voltage frozen while it is measured. The time the switch is closed is the sampling time.
  5. The SAR logic (Successive-Approximation Register) does the actual measuring: it plays a 12-question game of "higher or lower?" against the held voltage, one question per clock tick, and ends up with a 12-bit number. Section 4 walks through it.
  6. The reference VREF+ is the ruler. The number means "what fraction of VREF+ was the input?" On most STM32F407 boards VREF+ is tied to VDDA (the analog supply, usually 3.3 V). If the ruler is wrong by 2 %, every reading is wrong by 2 %.
  7. ADCCLK is the clock that paces the ADC (at most 36 MHz on F4; 21 MHz in our examples). The trigger (here timer TIM2) decides when each conversion starts.
  8. DR (Data Register) receives the result. DMA (Direct Memory Access, a hardware copier) moves each result from DR into an array in RAM without the CPU doing anything, so firmware later just reads buf[i].

3Key terms, one at a time

Learn these in order; each one builds on the previous.

Code
The whole number the ADC outputs (or the DAC accepts). A 12-bit converter's codes run from 0 to 4095.
N (resolution, bits)
How many bits the code has. N bits give 2N codes: 12 bits → 212 = 4096 codes. More bits = finer steps. It does not automatically mean more accurate.
VREF (reference)
The voltage that corresponds to "full scale". The ADC answers: Vin is what fraction of VREF? On a 3.3 V STM32F407 board, VREF = VDDA ≈ 3.3 V.
LSB (Least Significant Bit)
The voltage size of one step between codes: LSB = VREF / 2N. For 12 bits and 3.3 V: 3.3 / 4096 = 0.000 805 66 V = 805.7 µV. So code 1 ≈ 0.8 mV, code 1000 ≈ 0.806 V.
Quantisation error
The rounding error. An ideal converter rounds to the nearest code, so it is wrong by at most half a step: ±½ LSB = ±403 µV for our 12-bit, 3.3 V case.
Sample
One snapshot: one code from one moment in time.
fs (sample rate)
Snapshots per second, in samples per second (S/s). 1 kS/s = 1000 samples each second, one every 1 ms.
Nyquist frequency
Half the sample rate, fs/2. The sampling theorem (Nyquist–Shannon) says: you can capture a signal faithfully only if it contains nothing at or above fs/2. At 1 kS/s, the limit is 500 Hz.
Aliasing
What happens when you break that rule: a fast signal pretends to be a slow one. 1010 Hz sampled at 1000 S/s gives exactly the same numbers as a 10 Hz signal. Once sampled, it is impossible to tell them apart. Prevented only by an analog anti-alias filter (a low-pass filter) before the ADC.
Sample-and-hold (S/H)
The switch + capacitor that freezes the input voltage while it is being converted.
Sampling time (tS)
How long the S/H switch stays closed so the capacitor can charge. Set per channel on STM32F4 in the SMPR registers: 3, 15, 28, 56, 84, 112, 144 or 480 ADC clock cycles.
Conversion time
Sampling time + the time for the 12 "higher or lower?" decisions. On STM32F4, 12-bit: Tconv = SMP + 12 cycles. Example: 15 + 12 = 27 cycles at 21 MHz = 1.29 µs.
Source impedance (RS)
The resistance between the "true" voltage and the pin. The 4 pF capacitor must charge through it in the sampling time. Large RS + short sampling time = the capacitor does not finish charging = readings too low and noisy. The #1 real-world ADC bug.
Resolution vs accuracy
Resolution = how small a step the converter can show (805.7 µV). Accuracy = how close the reading is to the truth (limited by reference error, offset, gain, non-linearity, noise). A 12-bit ADC with a 2 %-wrong reference is only about 2 % accurate.
Trigger
The event that starts a conversion: software writing a bit (SWSTART) or a hardware event such as a timer overflow (TRGO = trigger output). Hardware triggers give perfectly regular sample spacing.
DMA
Direct Memory Access controller: hardware that copies data (here: ADC result → RAM array) on its own, so the CPU is free.
ENOB
Effective Number Of Bits: how many bits of a real, noisy ADC are actually meaningful. The STM32F407's 12-bit ADC has an ENOB of about 10.3 bits.
DAC output buffer
A small amplifier after the STM32 DAC so it can drive a load. It cannot reach the rails: its output stays roughly between 0.2 V and VDDA − 0.2 V.

4What happens during one conversion, step by step

Say the pin is at exactly 1.000 V, VREF = 3.3 V, 12 bits. The ADC does this:

  1. Track. The S/H switch closes. The 4 pF capacitor charges through the source and switch resistance towards 1.000 V. This lasts the programmed sampling time (say 15 cycles = 714 ns at 21 MHz).
  2. Hold. The switch opens. The capacitor now holds 1.000 V. The pin can change; this conversion no longer cares.
  3. Question 1 (bit 11, worth 2048). The SAR asks "is Vin ≥ 2048 × LSB = 1.650 V?" The comparator says no → bit 11 = 0.
  4. Question 2 (bit 10, worth 1024). "Is Vin ≥ 1024 × LSB = 0.825 V?" Yes → keep bit 10 = 1. Running code = 1024.
  5. Question 3 (bit 9, worth 512). "Is Vin ≥ (1024 + 512) × LSB = 1.2375 V?" No → bit 9 = 0.
  6. … and so on for 12 questions. Each answer halves the range in which the answer can lie. After 12 questions only one code is left: 1241 (hex 0x4D9). Check: 1.000 / 0.000 805 66 = 1241.2 → rounds to 1241. ✔
  7. Done. 1241 is written into DR, the EOC (End Of Conversion) flag sets, and the DMA copies it to RAM. Total time: 15 + 12 = 27 cycles = 1.29 µs.

This is just a binary search, exactly like guessing a number between 0 and 4095 with "higher/lower" answers: 12 guesses always suffice because 212 = 4096. The SAR Stepper tab lets you do it one click at a time.

5End-to-end worked example: measuring a 12 V battery

Task: firmware must report a lead-acid battery voltage (normally 11–14.4 V) once per millisecond, to about 10 mV, using ADC1 on pin PA1 of an STM32F407 with VDDA = 3.3 V.

Step 1 · The battery is too big for the pin, so divide it

The ADC can only read 0 … 3.3 V, and pins can be damaged above VDDA. We use a resistor divider: R1 = 100 kΩ from the battery to the pin, R2 = 27 kΩ from the pin to ground.

  • Divider ratio k = R2 / (R1 + R2) = 27 / 127 = 0.212 60.
  • Largest battery voltage we can read = 3.3 V / 0.2126 = 15.52 V. That covers 14.4 V charging with margin. ✔
  • Current drawn from the battery at 13.2 V: 13.2 / 127 kΩ = 104 µA. Acceptable for a device that is powered anyway.

Step 2 · Pin voltage and the code we expect

  • Battery at 13.20 V → pin voltage = 13.20 × 0.2126 = 2.806 V.
  • Code = 2.806 / 3.3 × 4096 = 3483.2 → the ADC reports 3483.
  • One LSB at the pin is 805.7 µV, so one LSB at the battery = 805.7 µV / 0.2126 = 3.79 mV. Our 10 mV target is about 2.6 LSB. ✔

Step 3 · Will the 4 pF capacitor charge in time? (source impedance)

The ADC "sees" the divider as a single resistor, its Thevenin resistance Rth = R1 ∥ R2 = (100 × 27)/(100 + 27) kΩ = 21.26 kΩ. Inside the chip there is also up to RADC = 6 kΩ of switch resistance, and the capacitor CADC ≈ 4 pF.

  • Time constant τ = (21.26 kΩ + 6 kΩ) × 4 pF = 27 260 × 4 × 10−12 = 109 ns.
  • To get within ½ LSB of the final voltage for 12 bits, wait ln(213) = 9.01 time constants: 9.01 × 109 ns = 982 ns.
  • At ADCCLK = 21 MHz one cycle is 47.6 ns, so we need 982 / 47.6 = 20.6 cycles. The next available setting is 28 cycles (1.33 µs). ✔ 15 cycles (714 ns) would be too short.
  • We also fit 100 nF at the pin. It acts as a charge reservoir for the 4 pF capacitor (100 nF is 25 000 × bigger) and as a low-pass filter with τ = 21.26 kΩ × 100 nF = 2.1 ms (cut-off ≈ 75 Hz), which kills noise and prevents aliasing. A battery voltage does not change in microseconds, so this slowness is harmless.

Step 4 · Timing: who starts each conversion?

  • TIM2 runs from 84 MHz. ARR = 84 000 − 1 makes it overflow every 84 000 / 84 MHz = 1.000 ms. Its overflow event is routed to TRGO, and ADC1 is told to start on a rising TRGO edge.
  • Each conversion takes 28 + 12 = 40 cycles = 1.90 µs, far less than the 1 ms between triggers.
  • DMA2 Stream0 copies each result from ADC1->DR into RAM. The CPU is not involved per sample.

Step 5 · Turning the code back into millivolts, in integers

Vbatt = code × (3.3 V / 4096) / k = code × 3300 mV × 127 / (4096 × 27). Do the multiplications first and the division last, so no precision is lost.

Step 6 · Sanity checks a senior engineer would do

  • Reference: VDDA from a ±2 % LDO means ±2 % of 13.2 V = ±264 mV, much worse than our 10 mV target. So we would also measure the internal reference VREFINT each scan and correct (card 13), or use a precision reference.
  • Resistor tolerance: two 1 % resistors can shift the ratio by up to about 1.6 % here. Use 0.1 % parts or calibrate each board once at a known voltage.
  • Noise: sample a steady battery 1000 times. If the codes spread over ±2 codes, that is your noise. Averaging 16 samples cuts random noise by √16 = 4.
  • Overvoltage: a 24 V jump-start would put 5.1 V on the pin. Add a clamp or larger R1 so the pin never exceeds VDDA.

6The other direction: making a voltage with the DAC

Task: produce a 1.250 V reference for an external comparator on PA4 (DAC channel 1).

  1. Choose the code. code = Vout / VREF × 4096 = 1.250 / 3.3 × 4096 = 1551.5 → write 1552. Expected output = 1552 × 805.7 µV = 1.2504 V (0.4 mV high: that is the DAC's own quantisation error, under ½ LSB).
  2. Check the buffer range. 1.25 V is well inside 0.2 … 3.1 V, so the output buffer can be left on (it lets the pin drive a 5 kΩ load).
  3. Write the register. DAC->DHR12R1 = 1552; With no trigger enabled, the value moves to the output register DOR one APB1 clock later, and the pin settles in about 3 µs.
  4. For a waveform instead of a fixed level, a timer triggers the DAC at a fixed rate and the DMA feeds it from a table (card 15). The output is a staircase, so a small RC low-pass after the pin smooths it.

7How all the pieces connect

Every ADC design decision falls into one of five links of a chain. If any link is weak, the whole measurement is only as good as that link:

  1. Front end (divider, amplifier, filter, protection): gets the signal into 0 … VREF, removes content above fs/2, and keeps the source impedance low enough. → cards 05, 10, 18, 21, 22.
  2. Sampling (sample rate, trigger, sampling time): when and how long we look. → cards 04, 09, 10, 12, the Aliasing Lab.
  3. Conversion (architecture, resolution, errors): turning the held voltage into a code, and how wrong that can be. → cards 01, 02, 06, 07, 08, the SAR Stepper.
  4. Reference (VDDA, VREFINT, ratiometric tricks): what the code is a fraction of. → cards 03, 13, 14.
  5. Firmware (DMA, scaling, filtering, calibration): turning codes into trustworthy engineering units. → cards 11, 12, 19, 20, 24.

The DAC is the same chain run backwards: firmware → code → converter → output buffer → reconstruction filter → load (cards 15, 16).

01

Sampling, quantisation and the LSB

BEGINNER▶

1. What is it?

An ADC (Analog-to-Digital Converter) turns a voltage into a whole number. To do that it has to make the signal discrete in two separate directions, and each direction has its own kind of error:

  • Sampling makes time discrete. The ADC takes one value every Ts = 1/fs seconds (fs = sample rate). The error this creates is aliasing: anything above fs/2 folds back and pretends to be a lower frequency (card 04).
  • Quantisation makes amplitude discrete. The voltage is rounded to one of 2N codes, where N is the resolution in bits. The error is quantisation error, at most ±½ LSB on an ideal converter (card 02).

The LSB (Least Significant Bit) is the voltage worth of one code step. It is the single most-used number in converter work:

LSB = VREF / 2N    12-bit, 3.3 V → 805.66 µV

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. The reference VREF (on STM32F4: the VREF+ pin, usually tied to VDDA = 3.3 V) defines full scale.
  2. That range is divided into 2N equal slices. For N = 12: 4096 slices of 3.3/4096 = 805.66 µV each.
  3. The ideal staircase puts the first transition (code 0 → 1) at ½ LSB = 403 µV, and the last (4094 → 4095) at VREF − 1.5 LSB. That way each code sits centred on its nominal voltage code × LSB, so the converter rounds to nearest: code = round(Vin/LSB), clamped to 4095.
  4. Everything from VREF − 1.5 LSB upwards reads 4095. The ADC cannot tell you how far over range you are.
  5. Going back: V = code × VREF / 2N. Datasheet offset and gain errors (card 07) are defined against this ideal line.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you divide before multiplying: the integer quotient 3300/4096 is 0, so every reading is 0 mV.
  • …you forget the rounding term: every result is biased about 0.4 mV low on average (truncation always rounds down).
  • …you use a hard-coded 3300 when VDDA is really 3.25 V: every reading is 1.5 % high.
A display that shows 0 mV for every input, or a reading that is consistently a fraction of an LSB low, or a reading that tracks the supply rail rather than the signal.

You will still see /4095 in many app notes (ST's own VREFINT notes included). The difference is 0.024 %, under 1 LSB, and disappears into gain error. Be consistent and be able to explain the choice.

6. Say it like this

An ADC does two things: it samples, which discretises time and risks aliasing, and it quantises, which discretises amplitude with ±½ LSB of error. The LSB is VREF/2N, so a 12-bit converter on 3.3 V has 806 µV steps. In firmware I convert with integers, multiply first and add half the divisor to round: (code × 3300 + 2048) >> 12.
02

Quantisation error: ±½ LSB and LSB/√12

BEGINNER▶

1. What is it?

Quantisation error is the difference between the real input voltage and the voltage that the output code stands for. Even a perfect converter has it, because it must round to one of a finite number of codes. It is the "rounding error" of the converter.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Slowly sweep Vin upwards from one code centre to the next.
  2. At the code centre the error is 0. As Vin rises, the error grows to +½ LSB just before the next transition.
  3. At the transition the code jumps by one, so the error jumps to −½ LSB and starts rising again.
  4. Plotted against Vin, the error is a sawtooth between −½ and +½ LSB.
  5. If the signal is "busy" (crosses many codes and is not locked to the sample clock), each sample lands at a random point on that sawtooth. The error then behaves like uniformly distributed white noise of width 1 LSB.
  6. The variance of a uniform distribution of width q is q²/12, so the rms (root-mean-square) error is q/√12:
erms = LSB / √12 ≈ 0.289 LSB   12-bit @ 3.3 V → 233 µV rms

3. Worked example

  • 12-bit on 3.3 V: LSB = 805.66 µV. Peak error = ±402.8 µV. rms = 805.66/3.4641 = 232.6 µV.
  • Derivation of 1/√12: for error e uniform on [−q/2, +q/2], mean-square = (1/q)∫e² de = (1/q)·[e³/3] from −q/2 to q/2 = (1/q)·(q³/12) = q²/12. Square root → q/√12.
  • Input exactly 1.0005 V (DC, perfectly quiet): 1.0005/0.000 805 66 = 1241.85 → code 1242 every single time. Error = 1.0005 − 1242 × 0.000 805 66 = −0.13 mV, and it is the same −0.13 mV in every sample. Averaging 1000 samples still gives 1242. That is the important exception below.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …the input is DC and noise-free: the error is not noise at all; it is a fixed function of the input. Averaging cannot remove it, and oversampling gives no extra bits (card 19, scenario 04). The fix is dither.
  • …the signal frequency is a simple ratio of fs (e.g. exactly fs/8): the error pattern repeats every 8 samples and shows up as harmonic spurs in an FFT rather than a flat noise floor.
  • …the converter truncates instead of rounds (first transition at 1 LSB instead of ½ LSB): the error runs 0 … −1 LSB, a constant −½ LSB bias. Same noise, but now an offset. Toggle the "½-LSB offset" box in the SAR stepper to see it.
An "oversampled 16-bit" output that only moves in steps of 16 counts; or FFT spurs at harmonics of a test tone that a better (dithered) setup would show as flat noise.
"Quantisation error is ±1 LSB." On an ideal converter it is ±½ LSB. The ±1 LSB figure people quote is a real converter's DNL or total error, not quantisation.

6. Say it like this

Quantisation error is a sawtooth between −½ and +½ LSB. For a busy signal uncorrelated with the clock, it behaves like uniform white noise of rms LSB/√12, about 0.29 LSB, which is where 6.02N + 1.76 dB comes from. For a quiet DC input it is deterministic, so averaging does nothing unless there is noise or dither.
03

Resolution, accuracy, precision and the reference

BEGINNER▶

1. What is it?

Three words that interviewers expect you to keep apart:

  • Resolution: the number of codes (12 bits = 4096), i.e. the smallest step the output can show. A property of the output format.
  • Precision / repeatability: how much readings scatter when the input is constant. This is noise. You measure it by sampling a DC input many times and taking σ (the standard deviation) of the codes.
  • Accuracy: how close the reading is to the true value. It is set by offset, gain, INL (card 07), the reference, the source impedance and the front end.
  precise, not accurate     accurate, not precise     both
        ·····                    ·    ·                  ···
        ·····      ⊕ truth     ·   ⊕   ·               ·⊕·
                                 ·    ·                  ···

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. The ADC reports Vin/VREF × 2N. It does not know volts; it knows fractions of its reference.
  2. Firmware multiplies by an assumed VREF (say 3.300 V) to get volts.
  3. If the real VREF is 3.234 V (2 % low), the code is 2 % higher than firmware expects, and every reported voltage is 2 % high.
  4. On the STM32F4 the reference is VREF+. On 64-pin packages it is bonded internally to VDDA, so the reference is literally the analog supply rail.

3. Worked example

  • VDDA from a 3.3 V LDO (Low-DropOut regulator) with ±2 % tolerance: anywhere from 3.234 V to 3.366 V.
  • At full scale, 2 % of 4096 codes = ±82 LSB of error. 82 LSB ≈ 26.4, so 6.4 of your 12 bits are consumed by the reference; the absolute measurement is only about 5.6 bits accurate.
  • Precision can still be excellent: on the same board σ might be 0.6 LSB. The readings are tightly clustered, just in the wrong place.
Three ways to fix a bad reference: (1) a dedicated precision reference on VREF+ (100-pin+ packages, e.g. a 2.5 V reference IC with 0.1 % accuracy); (2) measure VDDA with the factory-calibrated VREFINT and correct in software (card 13); (3) make the measurement ratiometric, so the reference cancels out (card 14).

Nyquist and aliasing: the basics

BEGINNER▶

1. What is it?

Sampling theorem (Nyquist–Shannon): a signal band-limited to B Hz (containing nothing above B) is completely described by its samples if fs > 2B. fs/2 is called the Nyquist frequency.

Aliasing is what happens when content exists above fs/2: it does not disappear, it shows up in the samples disguised as a lower frequency. Like a car wheel in a film that seems to turn slowly backwards.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Sample a sine of frequency f at instants t = n/fs. Sample n is sin(2π·f·n/fs).
  2. Now take a sine at f + k·fs for any whole number k. Its sample n is sin(2π(f + k·fs)·n/fs) = sin(2π·f·n/fs + 2π·k·n).
  3. 2π·k·n is a whole number of turns, so it changes nothing: the two sines give identical samples.
  4. So the firmware sees every frequency folded into the range 0 … fs/2:
falias = | f − fs·round(f / fs) |   always lands in 0 … fs/2
 0        fs/2        fs        3fs/2       2fs
 |  zone 1  |  zone 2  |  zone 3  |  zone 4  |
 |  -->     |     <--  |  -->     |     <--  |   even zones fold back mirrored
 everything ends up between 0 and fs/2 after sampling

3. Worked example

  • f = 1010 Hz, fs = 1000 S/s: round(1.01) = 1 → |1010 − 1000| = 10 Hz. The firmware sees a clean 10 Hz sine that does not exist.
  • f = 990 Hz: round(0.99) = 1 → |990 − 1000| = 10 Hz, but f − fs = −10 is negative, so the phase is reversed: a 10 Hz sine running backwards.
  • f = 20 003 Hz (PWM ripple), fs = 1000: round(20.003) = 20 → |20 003 − 20 000| = 3 Hz (scenario 03).
  • f = 700 Hz, fs = 1000: round(0.7) = 1 → |700 − 1000| = 300 Hz.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you sample at exactly 2B: a sine at exactly fs/2 can be sampled at its zero crossings and vanish completely. The theorem says strictly greater.
  • …you have "no signal" above fs/2: broadband noise above fs/2 still folds in. An unfiltered input aliases its noise into your band, raising the noise floor.
  • …you rely on real filters at exactly 2B: no analogue filter is a brick wall. In practice sample at 5–10× B.
  • …you try to remove an alias with a digital filter: impossible. After sampling, the 10 Hz alias is numerically identical to a real 10 Hz signal.
A slow oscillation in logged data that is not visible on a scope at the sensor; a "beat" whose frequency changes when you change the sample rate slightly (a real signal would not move).
"I'll fix the aliasing with a digital low-pass filter." The alias is indistinguishable from a real in-band signal. Only an analogue filter, or a higher fs followed by digital decimation, prevents it.

6. Say it like this

If a signal contains nothing at or above fs/2, its samples describe it completely. Anything above folds to |f − k·fs|, and after sampling it is numerically identical to a real in-band signal, so the anti-alias filter must be analogue and before the sampler. In practice I sample at 5–10× the bandwidth and also think about noise, not just signals, above Nyquist.

Input range, bipolar signals and overvoltage

BEGINNER▶

1. What is it?

The STM32F4 ADC is single-ended (it measures one pin against ground) and unipolar (it only converts positive voltages): it reads VSSA (analog ground, 0 V) … VREF+. Anything outside that range either clips or, worse, forces current into the chip. This card is about getting real-world signals (which may be negative, larger, or bidirectional) into that window safely.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Too big? Scale it down with a resistor divider (Learn tab, section 5) or an attenuating amplifier.
  2. Negative or bipolar? (AC voltage, bidirectional current) Shift it up so that 0 V input lands at mid-scale, VREF/2 = 1.65 V. Firmware subtracts 2048 to get a signed value.
  3. Derive the shift from the same reference the ADC uses (e.g. a divider from VDDA, or the ADC's VREF+). Then supply variation moves both the offset and the ruler together: the measurement is ratiometric.
  4. Protect the pin. Each pin has ESD (electrostatic discharge) diodes to VDDA and VSS. If the pin goes above VDDA + ~0.3 V or below −0.3 V, a diode conducts. That current is called injection current. A series resistor limits it.
  5. Optionally choose left alignment (CR2.ALIGN = 1): the 12-bit result is placed in DR[15:4]. It reads as a Q15-like fraction, or you can take just the top byte as a quick 8-bit value.

Many other parts (STM32G4/H7, most external ADCs) offer differential inputs, which measure the difference between two pins: common-mode noise cancels and the range doubles.

3. Worked example

Measure ±10 V with a passive three-resistor network: R1 from the signal, R2 from 3.3 V (VDDA), R3 to ground, all meeting at the pin. We want −10 V → 0.15 V and +10 V → 3.15 V (0.15 V margin at each rail).

  • Slope needed: (3.15 − 0.15) / 20 V = 0.15 V/V. Mid-point: 0 V in → 1.65 V.
  • With G = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3, the node voltage is (Vin/R1 + 3.3/R2)/G. So (1/R1)/G = 0.15 and 3.3·(1/R2)/G = 1.65 → (1/R2)/G = 0.5, which leaves (1/R3)/G = 0.35.
  • Choose Thevenin resistance 1/G = 10 kΩ: R1 = 66.7 kΩ, R2 = 20 kΩ, R3 = 28.6 kΩ (use 66.5k / 20k / 28.7k E96).
  • Check at +10 V: (10/66.7k + 3.3/20k) × 10k = 1.50 + 1.65 = 3.15 V ✔. At −10 V: −1.50 + 1.65 = 0.15 V ✔.
  • Firmware: Vin = (Vpin − 1.65) / 0.15.

SNR = 6.02N + 1.76 dB, SINAD and ENOB

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

SNR (Signal-to-Noise Ratio) compares the power of a signal with the power of the noise, in decibels (dB): SNR = 20·log10(signal rms / noise rms). For an ideal ADC, the only noise is quantisation noise, and the SNR for a full-scale sine is the famous 6.02N + 1.76 dB. Real converters are judged with SINAD and ENOB, defined below.

2. How does it work, step by step? (the derivation interviewers ask for)

  1. A full-scale sine spans all 2N codes, so its peak amplitude is A = 2N·LSB/2 = 2N−1·LSB.
  2. The rms of a sine is peak/√2: signal rms = 2N−1·LSB/√2.
  3. Quantisation noise rms = LSB/√12 (card 02).
  4. Ratio = (2N−1/√2)·√12 = 2N·√12/(2√2) = 2N·√1.5. The LSB cancels.
  5. In dB: 20·log10(2N) + 20·log10(√1.5) = N·6.0206 + 1.761.
SNR = 20·log10( (2N−1·LSB/√2) / (LSB/√12) ) = 20·log10(2N·√1.5) = 6.02·N + 1.76 dB
  • Each bit adds 6.02 dB (a factor of 2 in amplitude). 12 bits → 74.0 dB, 16 bits → 98.1 dB.
  • This assumes a full-scale sine. A signal at −20 dBFS (20 dB below full scale) has 20 dB less SNR, because quantisation noise does not shrink with the signal.
  • The noise is spread evenly over 0 … fs/2. If you only care about a bandwidth B, filtering gains 10·log10(fs/2B). That is the oversampling processing gain (card 19).

3. Real converters: SINAD, THD, SFDR, ENOB

  • SINAD (SIgnal-to-Noise-And-Distortion) includes everything that is not the signal: thermal noise, quantisation noise, harmonics and spurs.
  • THD (Total Harmonic Distortion) counts harmonics only. SFDR (Spurious-Free Dynamic Range) is the gap from the carrier to the single biggest spur, and matters most in radio and FFT work.
  • ENOB (Effective Number Of Bits) asks: which ideal converter would have this SINAD? Invert the formula:
ENOB = (SINAD − 1.76) / 6.02   SINAD 64 dB → 10.3 bits

Worked example: the STM32F407 datasheet quotes ENOB around 10.3 bits typ. (64 − 1.76)/6.02 = 10.34. So of the 12 output bits, about 1.7 bits are noise and distortion. If the test tone was at −1 dBFS instead of full scale, add the shortfall back: ENOB = (SINAD − 1.76 + 20·log(FS/Ain))/6.02 = (64 − 1.76 + 1)/6.02 = 10.5 bits.

Precision ΣΔ datasheets usually quote noise-free resolution instead: log2(FSR / (6.6·σ)). Peak-to-peak noise is about 6.6σ, so these are the codes that never flicker. Example: σ = 2 codes on a 24-bit part → log2(224/13.2) = 20.3 noise-free bits.

  • …you quote 74 dB for a small signal: a sensor using 10 % of the range (−20 dBFS) gets 54 dB at best.
  • …you measure ENOB at 1 kHz and use it at 500 kHz: ENOB falls with frequency (jitter, settling, distortion).

Static errors: offset, gain, DNL, INL, missing codes

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

"Static" errors are the ways a real converter's staircase differs from the ideal staircase of card 01, measured with slow (DC) inputs. There are four, and each has a different shape:

  OFFSET            GAIN               DNL                INL
  whole staircase   slope wrong        uneven step        staircase bows away
  shifted sideways  (rotates about 0)  widths             from a straight line
     _|‾              _|‾‾              _|‾|_‾‾|            ___/‾‾‾
   _|               _|               _|                  _/
  • Offset error: how far the first transition is from its ideal ½ LSB. It shifts the whole staircase.
  • Gain error: after removing offset, how far the last transition is from ideal. It changes the slope. Reference error shows up here.
  • DNL(k) (Differential Non-Linearity) = (actual width of code k / 1 LSB) − 1. It measures how uneven the individual steps are. DNL = −1 means a zero-width step: a missing code. DNL > −1 LSB everywhere guarantees no missing codes.
  • INL(k) (Integral Non-Linearity): how far transition k is from a straight line, drawn either through the end points or as a best fit (datasheets say which). INL is the running sum of DNL, and it is the error you cannot calibrate out with two points.
  • Monotonic: the output never goes down as the input goes up. That matters inside control loops, where a non-monotonic step can cause limit cycles.
  • TUE / ET (ST's "total unadjusted error") lumps it all together. STM32F407 typ / max: ET ±2 / ±5 LSB, EO ±1.5 / ±2.5, EG ±1.5 / ±3, ED ±1 / ±2, EL ±1.5 / ±3 LSB, under the datasheet's stated conditions.

2. How does it work, step by step? (why SAR DNL spikes at mid-scale)

  1. A SAR's internal DAC uses binary-weighted capacitors: the MSB capacitor is 2048 units, the next 1024, and so on.
  2. Going from code 2047 (0111 1111 1111) to 2048 (1000 0000 0000) switches every capacitor: all small ones off, the MSB on.
  3. That step is correct only if the MSB capacitor equals the sum of all the others plus exactly 1 unit.
  4. A 0.05 % mismatch in a 2048-unit capacitor is 2048 × 0.0005 ≈ 1 unit = 1 LSB of DNL error at that single step.
  5. Smaller spikes appear at every power-of-two boundary (1023→1024, 3071→3072, …) for the same reason.

2. How does each work, step by step?

  • SAR (Successive Approximation Register): sample, then a binary search with one comparator, one bit per clock, MSB first (card 09). N clocks for N bits. Trades time for bits.
  • ΣΔ (sigma-delta): (1) a very coarse quantiser, often 1 bit, runs much faster than needed (e.g. 64× to 256×); (2) a feedback loop with an integrator pushes ("shapes") the quantisation noise to high frequencies; (3) a digital low-pass filter removes that noise and lowers the rate (decimation).   • Flash: 2N−1 comparators, each with its own threshold from a resistor ladder, all compare at once. An 8-bit flash has 255 comparators. Trades area and power for speed.

  • Dual-slope: integrate the input for a fixed time (e.g. exactly 20 ms = one 50 Hz cycle, so mains averages to zero), then de-integrate with a reference and count how long it takes.

  1. Motor phase currents, 20 kHz PWM, 3 channels: need a sample at a precise instant (PWM centre) and no delay in the loop → SAR. The F4 ADC converts 3 channels in 3 × 15 cycles = 2.1 µs.

  3. Ultrasound echo, 40 MS/s, 12 bits: far beyond any MCU SAR → pipelined; the few-clock latency does not matter for an image.

SAR conversion, step by step

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

A SAR (Successive-Approximation Register) ADC finds the code with a binary search: it guesses, asks a comparator "is the input higher or lower than my guess?", and refines, one bit per clock, starting from the most significant bit (MSB). It is the converter inside almost every microcontroller, including all three ADCs of the STM32F407.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Track (sample). The input switch closes and the sampling capacitor (in a charge-redistribution SAR, the whole binary-weighted capacitor array) charges to Vin through Rsource + Rswitch. On the STM32F4 this lasts the programmed SMP cycles (3–480).
  2. Hold. The switch opens. The instant it opens is the sampling instant (also called the aperture). From here the input is disconnected; the multiplexer can already move on to the next channel.
  3. Bit trials, MSB first. For k = N−1 down to 0: set bit k in the SAR register, so the internal DAC (Digital-to-Analog Converter) outputs VDAC = trial_code × LSB. The comparator asks whether Vin ≥ VDAC. If yes, keep the bit; if no, clear it. N decisions, one clock each.
  4. Done. The register holds the code. It is copied to DR (data register) and the EOC (End Of Conversion) flag is set. On the STM32F4, 12 bits take 12 ADCCLK cycles after sampling.

5. What the silicon does: charge redistribution

The capacitors are weighted C, C, 2C, 4C … 2N−1C. During sampling their bottom plates connect to Vin, so the cap array is the sample-and-hold; there is no separate S/H amplifier. Then the top plate floats. Each bit trial switches one bottom plate to VREF and the rest to ground, which moves the top-plate node to VDAC − Vin. The comparator only checks its sign.

  • The input sees the whole array capacitance (about 4 pF on the F4) at the start of every sample, plus a charge "kick" left over from the last conversion. The source has to absorb that kick and settle (card 10).
  • The reference pin draws charge at every bit trial, so VREF+ needs a local reservoir capacitor.

6. What goes wrong if…

  • …noise or an unsettled reference flips an early decision: it cannot be undone. If bit 11 is wrongly set for a 1.649 V input, every later bit is cleared and the result is 2048 instead of 2047. Some SARs add redundant decision cycles to correct this.
  • …the input changes during the bit trials (no S/H): early and late decisions refer to different voltages; errors of many LSB.
  • …Vin ≥ VREF: every trial is kept and the code saturates at 4095.
Codes that jump between two values far apart (2047 vs 2048 is one LSB, but a wrong MSB with a slow reference can give 2048 vs 1900) only near major transitions such as mid-scale.

7. Say it like this

A SAR samples the input onto its capacitor array, opens the switch, then does a binary search MSB first: set the bit, let the capacitor DAC produce the trial voltage, compare, keep or clear. N bits take N clocks, so on the STM32F4 a 12-bit result costs the sampling time plus 12 ADCCLK cycles, with no latency. Its weaknesses are the switched-capacitor input that needs settling and the fact that an early wrong decision is never revisited.

Sample-and-hold vs source impedance (the #1 real-world ADC bug)

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

During the sampling window the ADC's capacitor must charge to the input voltage. It charges through the resistance of whatever drives the pin (the source impedance RS) plus the internal switch. If the window is too short for that resistance, the capacitor stops short of the true voltage, and the reading is wrong. Most "my ADC is inaccurate" bugs are this.

  Vsource ──[ R_S ]──o PIN ──[ R_ADC ≤ 6 kΩ ]──┬── to SAR
                     │                         │
                  [C_EXT]                  [C_ADC ≈ 4 pF]
                     │                         │
                    GND                       GND
   switch closes for t_S = SMP cycles, then opens

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Model: RS in series with the switch RADC (up to 6 kΩ on STM32F4) charging CADC (about 4 pF typ.). A single RC with time constant τ = (RS + RADC)·CADC.
  2. At the start of sampling, CADC still holds whatever it had: the previous channel's voltage, worst case 0 V or VREF.
  3. The remaining error decays as e−t/τ. For the worst case (a full-scale step), it must fall below ½ LSB = 2−(N+1) of full scale before the switch opens:
e−t/τ ≤ 2−(N+1)  ⇒  tS ≥ τ · ln(2N+1) = τ·(N+1)·ln2   12-bit: 9.01 τ

3. Worked example

RS = 10 kΩ: τ = (10k + 6k) × 4 pF = 64 ns. tS = 9.01 × 64 ns = 577 ns. At ADCCLK 21 MHz (47.6 ns per cycle): 577/47.6 = 12.1 cycles → nearest setting ≥ 12.1 is 15 cycles (714 ns).

ST's datasheet uses a slightly stricter form, RAIN ≤ (k − 0.5) / (fADC·CADC·ln(2N+2)) − RADC. It aims for ¼ LSB, and the −0.5 cycle covers uncertainty in when sampling starts. Use the datasheet's table for sign-off.

4. The external capacitor trick

Put a capacitor CEXT from the pin to ground. When the switch closes, CADC takes charge from CEXT, not from the slow source. Charge sharing: the worst-case droop is ΔV·CADC/(CEXT + CADC). To keep it under ½ LSB:

CEXT ≥ CADC · 2N+1   4 pF × 8192 ≈ 33 nF → fit 47–100 nF

The cost: the source must refill CEXT between samples. Each conversion removes charge CADC·ΔV, where ΔV is the difference from whatever the sampling cap last held (typically the previous channel in a scan). That is an average current CADC·ΔV·fs,ch through RS, which gives a DC error of:

Verr ≈ RS·CADC·ΔV·fs,ch   100 kΩ · 4 pF · 3.3 V · 10 kHz = 13 mV = 16 LSB

On a single channel ΔV is close to 0 and the trick works well. In a scan after a channel at a very different voltage, it does not (scenario 05). CEXT also limits bandwidth (τ = RS·CEXT), so it doubles as a simple anti-alias filter.

The symptom of getting it wrong is low, noisy, order-dependent readings.

1. What is it?

The concrete hardware you program. The STM32F407 has three identical 12-bit SAR ADCs (ADC1, ADC2, ADC3), sharing a common control block (ADC_CCR). Each is configured through a handful of registers: CR1/CR2 (control), SMPR1/2 (sampling times), SQR1–3 (the regular sequence), JSQR (the injected sequence), SR (status), DR (data).

2. How does it work, step by step? (the modes)

  1. Single: one conversion (or one sequence with SCAN) per trigger.

  4. Discontinuous (DISCEN, DISCNUM): converts n channels of the sequence per trigger.
  5. External trigger: CR2.EXTEN (which edge) + EXTSEL (which source), e.g. 0110 = TIM2_TRGO, 1000 = TIM3_TRGO. Injected: JEXTEN + JEXTSEL.
  6. Overrun (SR.OVR): a new regular result arrived before DR was read. Detected when DMA = 1 or EOCS = 1. Once OVR is set, DMA requests stop. To recover: re-initialise the DMA stream (address, NDTR), clear OVR, re-trigger.

Timer-triggered scan with circular DMA (register level)

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

The production pattern for continuous acquisition. A timer sets fs, so samples are perfectly evenly spaced. The ADC scans the channel sequence on each trigger. DMA moves each result into a circular buffer, and half-transfer (HT) / transfer-complete (TC) interrupts hand the CPU one half while the DMA fills the other ("ping-pong" buffering).

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. TIM2 counts at 84 MHz up to ARR = 8399 and overflows every 100 µs (10 kHz). On each overflow ("update event") it pulses TRGO.
  2. ADC1 is configured with EXTEN = rising edge, EXTSEL = TIM2_TRGO. Each pulse starts one scan of the 3-channel sequence.
  3. After each conversion, the ADC raises a DMA request. DMA2 Stream0 copies ADC1->DR into adc_buf[i] and increments i.
  4. After half the buffer (32 scans × 3 = 96 samples) the DMA raises HT: "first half is complete, I am now writing the second half".
  5. After the full buffer it raises TC and wraps back to the start (circular mode). The CPU processes whichever half the DMA is not writing.
TIM2 TRGO:  |     |     |     |     |      (every 100 us)
ADC1 scan:  [0 1 17][0 1 17][0 1 17] ...  (30 us each)
adc_buf:    [ half A: 32 scans ][ half B: 32 scans ]
                               ^HT              ^TC -> wrap
CPU:        process B ......... process A .........

  • Each scan: IN0 and IN1 at 56 cycles, IN17 at 480: (56 + 12) + (56 + 12) + (480 + 12) = 628 cycles = 29.9 µs at 21 MHz, well inside the 100 µs trigger period. (If a trigger arrives while a scan is still running, it is ignored.)

  • DMA load: 30 000 half-word transfers per second, negligible on an 84 MHz bus.

Values that occasionally sit between an old and a new level; channel values swapping positions in the array after a while; a buffer that never updates (DDS missing).

VREFINT calibration and the temperature sensor

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

VREFINT is an internal bandgap reference (a circuit that produces a nearly constant voltage regardless of supply), about 1.21 V (1.18–1.24 V across parts), connected to ADC1_IN17 and enabled by ADC_CCR.TSVREFE. At the factory, ST converted it with VDDA = 3.3 V (at 30 °C) and stored the raw code in read-only system memory. Because you know what code it gave at exactly 3.3 V, you can work out what VDDA is now. The same mechanism gives two calibration points for the on-chip temperature sensor.

Ratiometric measurement

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

A measurement is ratiometric when the sensor is powered ("excited") from the same voltage the ADC uses as its reference. The ADC measures Vin/VREF; if Vin is itself a fixed fraction of VREF, then VREF cancels and the code depends only on a ratio of resistances.

   VDDA ──┬──────────────── VREF+ (ADC reference)
          │
       [R_fix 10k]
          │
          ├──────────────── PA2 (ADC)
          │
       [R_ntc]
          │
         GND           code = 4096 * R_ntc / (R_fix + R_ntc)   (VDDA cancels)

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. The divider output is Vout = VDDA · Rntc/(Rfix + Rntc).
  2. The ADC code is 2N · Vout/VREF, and VREF = VDDA.
  3. Substitute: code = 2N · Rntc/(Rfix + Rntc). VDDA has disappeared.
code = 2N · Vout/VREF = 2N · Rntc/(Rfix+Rntc)   VREF cancels

Pots, NTC/PTC dividers, Wheatstone bridges (load cells, pressure sensors) and many ratiometric Hall sensors all work this way. A 5 % sag on VDDA does not change the reading at all. You no longer need a precise reference, only a quiet one.

3. Worked example

  • NTC 10 kΩ at 25 °C, B = 3435, Rfix = 10 kΩ. At 25 °C: code = 4096 × 10/20 = 2048, whether VDDA is 3.30 V or 3.10 V.
  • Non-ratiometric comparison: if the divider were fed from a separate 3.3 V rail while the ADC reference sagged to 3.10 V, the code would read 1.65/3.10 × 4096 = 2180, i.e. a temperature error of several °C.
  • At 50 °C the B-equation gives R = 10 kΩ × e3435·(1/323.15 − 1/298.15) = 4.10 kΩ: code = 4096 × 4.10/14.10 = 1191 → back-calculated R = 10 000 × 1191/(4096 − 1191) = 4100 Ω → 50 °C.

  • …the measurement is not really ratiometric: battery voltage through a divider, shunt-amplifier outputs, 4–20 mA loops and externally supplied sensors all produce an absolute voltage. They need an absolute reference or VREFINT correction.

PWM as a DAC: resolution, ripple and settling

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

PWM (Pulse-Width Modulation) switches a pin between 0 and VDD, high for a fraction D of each period (the duty cycle). Its average is D·VDD. An RC low-pass filter extracts that average, giving a cheap "DAC" from any timer pin. Three numbers trade against each other: resolution, ripple and settling time.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. A timer counting at ftimer with ARR + 1 = 2N steps gives N bits of duty resolution, at a PWM frequency of ftimer/2N.
  2. More bits means a longer period, i.e. a lower PWM frequency, i.e. ripple that is harder to filter.
  3. The RC filter's ripple (peak-to-peak) is about VDD·D(1−D)/(fPWM·RC), worst at D = 0.5.
  4. A bigger RC lowers the ripple but makes the output slower to follow changes: settling to ½ LSB takes RC·ln(2N+1).
fPWM = ftimer / 2N   84 MHz, 12-bit → 20.5 kHz; 8-bit → 328 kHz
Vripple,pp ≈ VDD·D(1−D)/(fPWM·RC)   worst at D = 0.5: VDD/(4·fPWM·RC)
tsettle (to ½ LSB) ≈ RC · ln(2N+1)

3. Worked example

12-bit at 20.5 kHz, first-order RC. For ripple under ½ LSB: VDD/(4·20 508·RC) < VDD/8192 gives RC > 8192/(4 × 20 508) = 0.1 s, so fc ≈ 1.6 Hz and settling takes 0.1 × 9.01 = 0.9 s. A first-order filter is far too slow for 12 bits.

Same job at 8 bits: fPWM = 328 kHz; ripple < ½ LSB needs RC > 512/(4 × 328 125) = 0.39 ms → fc ≈ 410 Hz, settling 0.39 ms × 6.24 = 2.4 ms. Often fine for an LED level or a bias set-point.

  • Use a 2nd/3rd-order filter (RC-RC or Sallen-Key). At 40 dB/decade the same ripple allows a much higher fc.
  • Dual-PWM: two 8-bit PWMs summed through resistors weighted 1 : 256 make a 16-bit DAC. Matching limits the accuracy.
  • Dithered PWM / ΣΔ modulation of the duty cycle spreads the ripple to higher frequencies.

Multi-ADC modes, injected triggering and the analog watchdog

ADVANCED▶

1. What is it?

Three features that turn the STM32F4 ADC from "measure a pin" into a control-system tool: (a) multi-ADC modes, where ADC1/2/3 cooperate to sample simultaneously or take turns; (b) the injected group, a high-priority mini-sequence fired by a timer at an exact instant (motor control); (c) the analog watchdog (AWD), hardware that flags any conversion outside a window without CPU involvement.

2. How does it work, step by step?

Dual / triple mode (ADC_CCR.MULTI):

  1. Regular simultaneous (dual 00110, triple 10110): one trigger starts ADC1 and ADC2 at the same instant. Use it for V and I of the same phase (instantaneous power), or for two motor phase currents.
  2. Interleaved (dual 00111, triple 10111): the ADCs take turns on one channel, offset by DELAY (5–20 cycles). Triple interleaved at 36 MHz with 3-cycle sampling gives 7.2 MS/s: each ADC still converts every 15 cycles, staggered by 5.
  3. Alternate trigger (injected): successive triggers go to ADC1, ADC2, ADC3 in turn.
  4. Results are read from the common CDR. DMA mode 1 = one 16-bit half-word per conversion, mode 2 = two results packed per 32-bit word, mode 3 = packed 8-bit pairs (6/8-bit resolution).
  5. Interleaving artefacts: offset mismatch between ADCs gives a spur at fs/M (M ADCs). Gain and timing mismatch give spurs at k·fs/M ± fin. Calibrate or avoid interleaving where SFDR matters.

Injected group for motor control (FOC = Field-Oriented Control):

  1. TIM1 runs centre-aligned PWM: the counter goes up then down, and the PWM is symmetric around the counter's peak.
  2. At the centre of the low-side ON time, all low-side switches conduct and the shunt currents are valid and far from switching edges.
  3. TIM1_CH4 is set to compare exactly there; its rising edge triggers the injected group.
  4. Two phase currents are converted into JDR1–2 with JOFRx (e.g. 2048) subtracted, so results are signed.
  5. Meanwhile the regular group keeps scanning slow channels (temperature, bus voltage) through DMA; the injected conversion pre-empts it and the regular scan resumes.

Anti-alias filter design

ADVANCED▶

1. What is it?

An anti-alias filter is an analog low-pass filter placed before the ADC so that nothing above fs/2 (or, more precisely, nothing that would fold onto your band) reaches the sampler with enough amplitude to matter. Designing it means answering: how much attenuation, at which frequency, with what filter order, and what does it cost in delay and settling?

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Define the band you care about, 0 … B.
  2. Find which input frequencies fold onto it: everything in fs − B … fs + B (and around 2fs, 3fs …).
  3. Decide how big an interferer could be there and how small it must become. For 12 bits, full scale to ½ LSB is 20·log(8192) = 74 dB.
  4. The filter must give that attenuation at fs − B while passing B nearly untouched.
  5. Each filter order adds 20 dB/decade of roll-off above fc.
1st order: |H(f)| = 1/√(1 + (f/fc)²)    n-th order Butterworth: 1/√(1 + (f/fc)2n)

fs = 10 kHz, band 0–1 kHz, 12-bit. Anything between 9 kHz and 11 kHz folds onto 0–1 kHz. Worst case: a full-scale interferer at 9 kHz needs 74 dB.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you design for an imaginary full-scale interferer: a 30 mV PWM ripple on a 3.3 V range is already 41 dB below full scale, so you need far less attenuation. Quantify the real spectrum at the pin with a scope FFT.

I first work out what folds onto my band, fs ± B and its multiples, and how large the real interferers are there. I synchronise to known interferers like PWM, use C0G capacitors, and account for the filter's phase lag in control loops.

Oversampling, decimation, dither and noise shaping

ADVANCED▶

1. What is it?

Oversampling means sampling faster than the signal needs, then combining several samples into one. Decimation is that combining (filter + reduce the rate). Done right, it trades sample rate for extra resolution: a 12-bit ADC can deliver 14 or 16 useful bits on a slow signal. Rule: to gain n bits, add up 4n samples and shift right by n.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Each sample = true value + random noise. If the noise is uncorrelated from sample to sample, averaging M samples reduces its rms by √M.
  2. One extra bit means the noise must halve. Halving needs √M = 2 → M = 4. n extra bits → √M = 2n → M = 4n. Equivalently: SNR improves by 10·log10(M) dB, i.e. 3 dB (half a bit) per doubling.
  3. Adding 4n 12-bit samples makes a number 2n bits wider. The new low bits are partly meaningful because noise "spread" the true value over neighbouring codes.
  4. Shift right by n (not by 2n) to keep the n meaningful new bits. Dividing by M would return to 12 bits and discard the gain.
  5. Throughput falls by 4n: 16× for +2 bits, 256× for +4 bits.

3. Worked example

  • True input 1.0003 V = 1241.6 LSB. With 0.8 LSB rms noise, samples land on 1240, 1241, 1242, 1243 in proportions centred on 1241.6.
  • Sum of 16 samples ≈ 16 × 1241.6 = 19 865.6, say 19 866. Shift right by 2: 19 866 / 4 = 4966 (14-bit code). 14-bit LSB = 3.3/16384 = 201.4 µV → 4966 × 201.4 µV = 1.0002 V. ✔ The quarter-LSB detail survived.
  • Same input with no noise: every sample is 1242 → sum 19 872 → 4968 → 1.0006 V. Nothing gained; the 14-bit result is just the 12-bit result × 4 (scenario 04).

5. What goes wrong if… (the conditions people forget)

  • …noise is below about ½–1 LSB rms: every sample returns the same code and the average equals that code. The fix is dither: add about 1 LSB of known noise, or a triangle waveform injected through a high-value resistor from a DAC/PWM, then average.
  • …the interference is correlated (mains hum, PWM ripple locked to the sample clock): it does not average away. It aliases or biases.
  • …the signal moves during the 4n samples: averaging is a low-pass filter (a sinc response with its first null at fs/M).
  • …you expect better accuracy: oversampling does not fix INL, gain or offset. Extra resolution does not add accuracy.
  • STM32 G0/L4/G4/H7 have a hardware oversampler (OVSR ratio up to 256×, OVSS shift). The F4 does not; do it in software or with DMA blocks.
An "oversampled" output that moves only in steps of 4 (for +2 bits) or 16 (for +4 bits), with nothing in between.

6. Noise shaping (why ΣΔ gets 24 bits)

A ΣΔ modulator puts the quantiser inside a feedback loop with an integrator. The signal passes through, while quantisation noise is high-pass filtered and pushed out of band. The digital decimation filter (sincL+1 + FIR) then removes it. Gain per doubling of OSR (OverSampling Ratio):

order L: (6L + 3) dB per octave of OSR   L=0 (plain): 3 dB · L=1: 9 dB · L=2: 15 dB

A 2nd-order 1-bit modulator at OSR 256 gives 8 octaves × 15 dB = 120 dB, beyond 19 bits from a 1-bit quantiser. (This counts slope only; real designs lose a few dB to constant terms.)

7. Say it like this

Averaging M samples cuts uncorrelated noise by √M, so each extra bit needs 4× the samples: sum 4n and shift right by n. It only works if there is at least about half an LSB of random noise or added dither, the signal is steady over the window, and it never improves INL, offset or gain. Sigma-delta goes further by shaping the noise out of band, gaining 6L + 3 dB per octave of oversampling.

ADVANCED▶

1. What is it?

Raw ADC codes are noisy; firmware smooths them with simple digital filters, in integer ("fixed-point") arithmetic because it is fast and deterministic. Three filters cover 90 % of embedded needs: the moving average (boxcar), the exponential moving average (EMA, a first-order IIR = Infinite Impulse Response filter), and the median (for spikes). The traps are integer overflow and rounding.

2. Accumulator sizing, step by step

  1. One 12-bit sample is at most 4095 (12 bits).
  2. The sum of M samples is at most M × 4095, which needs 12 + ⌈log2M⌉ bits.
  3. 16 samples → 16 bits (max 65 520: just fits a uint16_t). 64 → 18 bits. 1024 → 22 bits.
  4. So the 17th sample can already wrap a uint16_t. With 64 samples it wraps whenever the average is above 65 535/64 = 1023 codes, i.e. above 0.82 V. Low readings look fine and high ones collapse (scenario 07).
  5. Watch for C's integer promotion too: uint16_t a + b is computed as int, but storing it back into a uint16_t truncates.
bits needed = Nadc + ⌈log2(M)⌉   12-bit × 16 = 16 bits · × 64 = 18 bits · × 1024 = 22 bits

ADVANCED▶

1. What is it?

To measure a high voltage (a 60 V pack) with a 3.3 V ADC you divide it down with two resistors. Small resistors waste battery current; huge resistors create a high source impedance that the ADC's switched-capacitor input, its leakage current, and resistor tolerances all turn into error. This card is the full trade-off with numbers.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Ratio k = R2/(R1 + R2); pin voltage = k·Vbatt.
  2. The ADC sees the divider as one source with Thevenin resistance Rth = R1 ∥ R2 = R1·R2/(R1 + R2).
  3. Settling: τ = (Rth + RADC)·CADC; need 9τ of sampling (card 10).
  4. Leakage: the pin's leakage current Ilk flows through Rth and creates an error voltage Ilk·Rth.
  5. Everything at the pin is multiplied by 1/k at the battery.

  1. Capacitor at the pin, ≥ CADC·2N+1 ≈ 33 nF (use 100 nF). The divider then only has to supply the average current. τ = 48.5 kΩ × 100 nF = 4.9 ms, which is fine for battery voltage. Watch the charge-sharing error if it shares a scan with other channels (card 10).
  2. Longer sampling time on that channel only (per-channel SMPR).
  3. Calibrate the ratio per board in production (one known voltage, code above), or use 0.1 % resistors.
  4. Op-amp buffer (rail-to-rail, low bias current) when you need bandwidth too.
  5. Switched divider (high-side P-FET enabled by a GPIO) for ultra-low drain. Allow settling time after enabling it.

Also fit a series resistor and clamp in front of the pin, so pack transients cannot inject current. The 1 MΩ helps here. Size the resistor's voltage rating too: 60 V across one 0603 is within rating (typically 75 V), but higher voltages need several resistors in series.

6. What goes wrong if…

  • …3-cycle sampling and no cap: 143 ns ≈ 0.65τ: low, noisy, order-dependent (scenario 02).
  • …you divide the reading by the nominal ratio without calibrating: up to ±1.1 V error at 60 V, which can be the difference between "full" and "90 %" on a pack.
  • …a cheap clamp diode with µA leakage at the pin: another leakage term through 48.5 kΩ.
Pack voltage reading 1 V low compared with a DMM; error that changes with temperature (leakage doubles every ~10 °C) or with scan order.

7. Say it like this

A 1 MΩ / 51 kΩ divider drains only 57 µA on 60 V, but the ADC sees 48.5 kΩ, so it needs about 2 µs of sampling or a reservoir capacitor of at least 33 nF; worst-case leakage times that resistance is about 60 LSB, and two 1 % resistors give about 2 % ratio error. I put 100 nF at the pin, lengthen that channel's sampling, calibrate the ratio per board, and protect the pin against transients.

Layout: AGND, VDDA, VREF+ and decoupling

ADVANCED▶

1. What is it?

An ADC measures the voltage between its pin and its ground, against its reference. If digital currents disturb that ground, or switching noise reaches VDDA or VREF+, the measurement is wrong no matter how good the firmware is. Layout is how you keep the analog "ruler" and "zero" quiet.

2. How does it work, step by step? (the rules and the reason for each)

  1. One solid ground plane is usually best. Return current at high frequency flows directly under its signal trace (the path of least inductance). An unbroken plane keeps loops tiny. Partition the placement (analog parts on one side, digital on the other) rather than cutting the plane. If AGND and DGND pins must be separate, join them at one point under the MCU or ADC, and never route a signal across the gap: its return current would detour around it.
  2. VDDA: feed it from VDD through a ferrite bead (or 10–47 Ω), with 1 µF + 100 nF at the pin. ST's ADC-accuracy note (AN2834) recommends 1 µF + 10 nF on VREF+ and VDDA, as close to the pins as possible.
  3. VREF+ draws charge at each SAR bit trial, so it needs a local reservoir capacitor. An external reference needs its own decoupling per its datasheet; some references oscillate with the wrong capacitor.
  4. Analog inputs: put the RC filter at the MCU pin, not at the connector, so the trace between filter and pin cannot pick up noise. Keep traces short, away from SMPS (switch-mode power supply) switch nodes, clocks, SWD and PWM lines, and guard them with ground.
  5. Digital activity during conversion: GPIOs next to an analog pin toggling at high OSPEEDR (output speed setting) couple straight in. Lower the slew rate, avoid toggling adjacent pins during sampling, or sleep the core during conversion.
  6. Thermal: keep precision dividers and references away from hot parts. A tempco mismatch between R1 and R2 shows up as drift.

3. Worked example: how much noise is "too much"?

  • 1 LSB at 12 bits, 3.3 V = 806 µV. A 50 mA digital current step through 20 mΩ of shared ground copper = 1 mV, more than 1 LSB.
  • A 3.3 V GPIO edge with 1 ns rise time coupling through 0.1 pF of stray capacitance into a 10 kΩ source: injected current ≈ C·dV/dt = 0.1 pF × 3.3 V/1 ns = 0.33 mA for 1 ns. Into the 4 pF sampling cap that is 0.33 pC / 4 pF ≈ 80 mV of disturbance at that instant, which a 100 nF pin capacitor reduces 25 000-fold.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you split the plane and route an SPI clock across the split: the return current loops around the gap, making an antenna right next to the ADC.
  • …decoupling caps are 10 mm away with thin traces: the trace inductance (≈ 1 nH/mm) makes them useless above a few MHz.
  • …the input RC is at the connector: the long trace between filter and pin picks up noise after the filter.
Noise that changes when the code does something else (e.g. SPI transfers, LED PWM); σ of 3–5 LSB on a quiet input; a spur at the SMPS frequency in an FFT of a DC input.
"Split the ground plane into AGND and DGND islands everywhere." Unless the plane is joined in exactly one place and nothing crosses the split, this makes things worse: return paths get longer and loops become antennas.

6. Say it like this

I use one solid ground plane and separate analog and digital by placement rather than by cutting the plane. VDDA gets a ferrite or small resistor from VDD plus 1 µF and 100 nF right at the pin, VREF+ gets its own local reservoir, and the input RC sits at the MCU pin with short, guarded traces away from switch nodes and clocks. In firmware I keep neighbouring pins slow and can sleep the core during conversion.

Error budget, verification and aperture jitter

ADVANCED▶

1. What is it?

An error budget is a table that lists every source of error in a measurement chain, in the same unit (LSB or % of reading), and adds them up to see whether the design meets its accuracy requirement. Verification is how you measure the real errors on a board. Aperture jitter is the timing uncertainty of the sampling instant, which turns into amplitude error on changing signals.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. List every source: reference tolerance and drift, offset, gain, INL, noise σ, settling error, leakage × Rth, divider tolerance, amplifier offset and gain, and the temperature range.
  2. Convert each to the same unit.
  3. Worst case: add the systematic terms linearly. Statistical: combine independent terms as a root sum of squares (RSS), √(a² + b² + …).
  4. Decide which terms calibration removes (offset, gain, ratio) and which remain (INL, drift, noise).
  5. Compare with the requirement; if it fails, the biggest term tells you what to change.

4. Verifying an ADC on a real board

  • DC histogram: 10 000 samples of a quiet DC input. σ in LSB is your noise, and a lopsided histogram points to interference.
  • Code-density (histogram) test: a slow ramp or a slightly overdriven sine. The hits per code give DNL, and their running sum gives INL.
  • FFT test: a pure sine sampled coherently (M cycles in an N-point record, M odd and coprime with N), or windowed. Gives SNR, SINAD, ENOB, THD, SFDR. The source must be cleaner than the ADC; use a filtered generator.

5. Aperture / sampling jitter

A sine A·sin(2πft) changes fastest at its zero crossing, with slope 2πf·A. A timing error Δt there causes an amplitude error 2πf·A·Δt. Averaging over the sine gives:

SNRjitter = −20·log10(2π · fin · tj,rms)
  • 12-bit (74 dB) at 100 kHz needs tj < 10−74/20/(2π × 105) = 317 ps. At 1 MHz, < 32 ps.
  • Software-triggered sampling with 1 µs rms jitter on a 1 kHz signal: −20·log(2π × 1000 × 10−6) = 44 dB, about 7 effective bits. A timer trigger removes that error source entirely.
  • Jitter error scales with input slew rate, so DC measurements do not care and high-frequency ones care a lot.

6. What goes wrong if…

  • …you budget only the ADC datasheet numbers: the reference and the resistors usually dominate.
  • …you RSS systematic terms for a safety limit: RSS is a probability statement; a protection threshold needs the worst case.
  • …the FFT test is not coherent and not windowed: spectral leakage smears the tone and fakes a terrible SINAD.
A product that passes on the bench at 25 °C and fails accuracy at 60 °C (drift not budgeted); an FFT with a broad "skirt" around the tone (leakage, not the ADC).

7. Say it like this

I list every error term in one unit, add systematic ones linearly for the worst case and independent random ones as RSS, then see what calibration removes and what remains, which is usually INL, drift and noise. I verify with a DC histogram for noise, a code-density test for DNL and INL, and a coherent FFT for SINAD and ENOB. For AC signals I also budget jitter, SNR = −20·log(2πf·tj), which is why sampling must be timer-triggered.

  1. An ADC samples (time → aliasing) and quantises (amplitude → ±½ LSB, rms LSB/√12).
  2. LSB = VREF/2N. 12-bit on 3.3 V = 806 µV.
