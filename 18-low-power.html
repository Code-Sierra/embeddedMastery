Low-Power Design from Zero

Read this tab from top to bottom once before anything else. It builds the whole topic step by step: what problem low-power design solves, what is physically inside the chip that eats current, the key words, one complete worked example with real numbers, and how every other tab on this page fits together. No prior knowledge is assumed beyond "a microcontroller runs code from flash".

STEP 1

The problem: a battery is a fixed bucket of charge

A battery does not store "hours". It stores a fixed amount of electric charge. The label tells you how much, in mAh (milliamp-hours): a CR2032 coin cell holds about 225 mAh. That means it could deliver 225 mA for 1 hour, or 1 mA for 225 hours, or 1 µA (one microamp, a millionth of an amp) for 225 000 hours. The product current × time is fixed. So the only thing firmware can control is how fast the bucket drains, and that drain rate is the average current.

Let's put real numbers on it. An STM32L476 microcontroller (an ST Cortex-M4 part designed for low power, which this page uses as its main example) running code at 80 MHz draws roughly 10 mA. The same chip in its "Stop 2" sleep mode draws roughly 1.4 µA with its clock (RTC) running. That is a factor of about 7 000.

That is what "low-power design" means: make the average current as small as possible while still doing the job. Almost every product that runs on a battery for months (a door sensor, a smart watch, a tracker, a utility meter, a BMS sleeping in a parked vehicle) depends on it.

The one idea to keep: a battery-powered device spends almost all of its life asleep. It wakes up, does a small job as fast as possible, and goes back to sleep. Battery life is decided by (1) how little current it draws while asleep, (2) how long and how often it is awake, and (3) how much current it draws while awake.
STEP 2

The physical picture: where does the current actually go?

Before any registers, picture the hardware. Current leaves the battery, passes through a regulator (a circuit that turns the battery's 3.0–3.6 V into the steady voltages the chip needs), and is used by several separate blocks. Each block can be switched off or slowed down independently, and that is what all the "power modes" really do.

Inside a CMOS chip (CMOS = Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor, the transistor technology of every modern MCU), current is consumed in only a few physical ways:

  1. Switching current. Every time a clock edge makes a transistor flip, a tiny capacitor (the wire plus the next transistor's gate) is charged or discharged. Billions of these per second add up to milliamps. This current exists only while clocks are running, so stopping a clock removes it.
  2. Leakage current. A transistor that is "off" still lets a tiny current through. Millions of transistors × a tiny current = microamps. Leakage flows as long as the block has power, even with every clock stopped, and it grows quickly with temperature. Only removing power from a block removes it.
  3. Analog bias current. An ADC, a comparator, a voltage reference or an oscillator needs a steady bias current to work at all. Stopping a digital clock does not stop it; you must power the block down explicitly.
  4. I/O current. Current that flows out of pins: an LED, a pull-up resistor pulled low, or a "floating" input pin whose input circuit is stuck half-on (Step 3 explains this one; it is the most common bug in the whole topic).

Every power-saving technique on this page attacks one of those four. Sleep modes stop clocks (removing switching current) and, in the deepest modes, cut power to whole blocks (removing their leakage). Clock gating stops the clock to peripherals you are not using. Voltage scaling lowers VCORE, which reduces switching energy with the square of the voltage. Pin configuration removes I/O current.

RetentionWhether something keeps its contents in a mode. "SRAM retained in Stop 2" means your variables survive; "SRAM1 lost in Standby" means they do not.

Race-to-sleepThe strategy of finishing a job at a high clock speed so the chip can return to sleep sooner. Usually, but not always, the most energy-efficient choice.
Derating, self-dischargeDerating: assuming you only get 60–80 % of the label capacity (cold, pulses, cut-off voltage). Self-discharge: the battery slowly losing charge on its own (≈ 1 %/year for a lithium coin cell).
STEP 4

How a chip actually goes to sleep and wakes up

Here is the complete sequence for the most common case, an STM32L476 entering Stop 2 and waking on its RTC timer. Every later card zooms into one of these steps.

  1. Finish the work and prepare. Firmware stops anything that would wake it immediately (the 1 ms SysTick interrupt), sets unused pins to analog mode, and turns off peripherals it does not need.
  2. Choose the depth. Firmware writes the vendor register PWR_CR1.LPMS = 010 ("when deep sleep is requested, use Stop 2"). The Arm core knows nothing about "Stop 2"; this is ST's power controller.
  3. Ask for deep sleep. Firmware sets SCB->SCR.SLEEPDEEP = 1 (Arm register) and executes WFI.
  4. Hardware shuts down. The core stops. The power controller switches off the PLL, HSE, HSI16 and MSI, switches from the main regulator to a tiny low-power regulator, and keeps SRAM and register contents powered. Current falls from ≈ 10 mA to ≈ 1.4 µA (with the RTC running on its 32.768 kHz crystal).
  5. Time passes. Only the RTC (and anything else in the always-on list for Stop 2) is ticking.
  6. Wake-up event. The RTC wake-up timer reaches zero. It signals the EXTI controller on line 20, which tells the power controller to wake.
  7. Hardware restarts. The main regulator comes back, MSI restarts at 4 MHz (the PLL does not restart by itself), and the core resumes at the instruction after WFI. This takes about 8 µs. The pending RTC interrupt is taken.
  8. Firmware restores what was lost. Clear SLEEPDEEP, re-enable the PLL and switch back to 80 MHz (otherwise your UART baud rate is 20× too slow), restart SysTick, then do the job and repeat.
void sleep_until_rtc(void) {
    SysTick->CTRL &= ~SysTick_CTRL_TICKINT_Msk;              // step 1: tick would wake us at once
    MODIFY_REG(PWR->CR1, PWR_CR1_LPMS, PWR_CR1_LPMS_STOP2);  // step 2: deep sleep = Stop 2
    SCB->SCR |= SCB_SCR_SLEEPDEEP_Msk;                       // step 3: request deep sleep
    __DSB();                                                 //         finish pending memory writes
    __WFI();                                                 // steps 4-7: asleep here, resumes here
    SCB->SCR &= ~SCB_SCR_SLEEPDEEP_Msk;                      // step 8: next WFI is light sleep again
    SystemClock_Config();                                    //         MSI 4 MHz → PLL 80 MHz
    SysTick->CTRL |= SysTick_CTRL_TICKINT_Msk;               //         tick back on
}

A design process you can say in an interview

  1. Budget first. Write down every phase (sleep, wake, active, radio) with current and time, and compute I_avg and life on paper, like Step 5.
  2. Pick the sleep mode from three questions: what must be retained (RAM? registers?), what must be able to wake me (which pins, which peripheral)? and how fast must I respond (wake-up latency)?
  3. Make the hardware quiet: every pin defined, every external part in its shutdown mode, no resistor leaking into a switched-off rail.
  4. Make the firmware correct: race-free sleep entry, clocks restored after wake, no periodic tick, no busy-waiting.
  5. Measure with the debugger detached, after a power cycle, using an auto-ranging current analyser, and compare with the budget. The biggest mismatch is your next bug.
"Battery life is usable capacity divided by average current, and average current is the sum of current × time for each phase divided by the period. So I first build that budget, find the biggest slice, and attack it. Usually that means: sleep in the deepest mode that keeps the state and wake-up sources I need, set every unused pin to analog, wake only on interrupts, finish the work quickly at the best clock for the voltage range, restore clocks after Stop, and then verify the whole thing with a Joulescope or PPK2 with the debugger disconnected."

01

CMOS power: dynamic αCV²f and static leakage

BEGINNER▶

1. What is it?

This card explains why a chip uses power at all. Every modern microcontroller is built from CMOS transistors (CMOS = Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor: each logic gate has a P-type transistor that pulls the output up to the supply and an N-type transistor that pulls it down to ground). Every wire inside the chip behaves like a tiny capacitor. Running code means charging and discharging billions of those tiny capacitors per second. The formula that describes this is the first thing interviewers ask about in a low-power round:

P_total = α·C·V²·f + I_sc·V + I_leak·V
SymbolMeaningTypical value / unit
P_totalTotal power drawn by the logicW (mW for an MCU running)
α (alpha)Activity factor: the fraction of the capacitance that actually toggles each clock cycle0.1–0.3 for logic; 1 for the clock tree
CTotal switched capacitance (all gates and wires)farads (nF for a whole core)
VSupply voltage of the logic (VCORE, not the battery voltage)1.0–1.2 V on STM32L4
fClock frequencyHz (80 MHz max on the L476)
I_scShort-circuit current during an edge, when both transistors are briefly on≈ 5–10 % of dynamic
I_leakLeakage through transistors that are supposed to be offµA in a sleeping MCU

2. How does it work, step by step?

        VDD                     One inverter driving the next gate:
         │
       ┌─┴─┐ PMOS  (on when input = 0)     input 1→0 : PMOS turns on,
 in ──o┤   │                                current flows from VDD and
       └─┬─┘                                charges C to VDD
         ├──────────── out ──┬──            (energy drawn from supply = C·V²)
       ┌─┴─┐ NMOS            │
 in ───┤   │ (on when in = 1)═╪═ C  (wire + next gate)
       └─┬─┘                 │              input 0→1 : NMOS turns on,
         │                   │              C discharges to ground
        GND                 GND             (the stored ½·C·V² becomes heat)
  1. Output goes 0 → 1. The PMOS conducts and the supply pushes charge Q = C·V onto the load capacitor. The supply delivers that charge at voltage V, so it gives energy Q·V = C·V². Half of it (½·C·V²) ends up stored in the capacitor; the other half is turned into heat in the PMOS.
  2. Output goes 1 → 0. The NMOS conducts and dumps the stored ½·C·V² into ground as heat. The supply gives nothing during this edge.
  3. One full up-and-down cycle therefore costs C·V² from the supply. If only a fraction α of all the capacitance toggles per clock, and there are f clocks per second, the power is α·C·V²·f. That is dynamic (switching) power.
  4. During each edge, for a fraction of a nanosecond, both transistors are partly on and a short-circuit current flows straight from VDD to GND. Slow edges make this worse, which is why a slowly drifting, floating input pin is so expensive (card 08).
  5. Between edges, the "off" transistor is not perfectly off. A sub-threshold leakage current flows all the time. It depends exponentially on the transistor threshold voltage and on temperature, and it does not depend on the clock. That is static (leakage) power, and it is what remains in Stop mode.

3. Worked example with real numbers

Take an effective switched capacitance α·C = 100 pF for the whole STM32L4 core (a convenient round number that reproduces the ≈ 10 mA datasheet figure at 80 MHz).

Range 1 (V = 1.2 V, f = 80 MHz):
  P = α·C·V²·f = 100e-12 × 1.2² × 80e6 = 100e-12 × 1.44 × 80e6 = 11.5 mW
  core current  = P / V = 11.5 mW / 1.2 V = 9.6 mA
  energy/cycle  = α·C·V² = 100e-12 × 1.44 = 144 pJ

Range 2 (V = 1.0 V, f = 26 MHz):
  P             = 100e-12 × 1.0² × 26e6 = 2.6 mW
  energy/cycle  = 100e-12 × 1.0         = 100 pJ        → 31 % less per cycle

A job of 1 000 000 cycles:
  at 1.2 V: 1e6 × 144 pJ = 144 µJ   (in 12.5 ms at 80 MHz)
  at 1.2 V but 40 MHz:   = 144 µJ   (in 25 ms: same energy, power halved)
  at 1.0 V, 26 MHz:      = 100 µJ   (in 38.5 ms: less energy because V dropped)

The three consequences interviewers look for fall straight out of these numbers:

  1. V is squared. Dropping VCORE from 1.2 V to 1.0 V cuts switching energy by 1 − (1.0/1.2)² = 1 − 0.694 = 31 %, at any frequency.
  2. Energy per operation is α·C·V², independent of f. Halving the clock halves the power but doubles the run time, so the energy for a fixed job is unchanged. Lowering f helps only if it lets you also lower V.
  3. Leakage is paid per unit of time, not per operation. The longer the chip is awake, the more leakage energy it burns. A common rule of thumb on low-leakage processes is roughly 2× for every 15–20 °C. From 25 °C to 85 °C is 60 °C, i.e. 3–4 doublings, so ≈ 8–16×: that is why a 1.1 µA Stop 2 figure at 25 °C becomes ≈ 10 µA at 85 °C.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you believe "half the clock = double the battery life". You set the CPU to 40 MHz, the job takes twice as long, everything that is on while awake (flash, regulator, sensor, leakage) runs twice as long, and the measured battery life gets slightly worse. Symptom: average current barely moves or rises.
  • …you stay in Range 1 (1.2 V) while running at 16 MHz. You pay 44 % more switching energy per cycle than needed (1.44/1.0). Symptom: active current higher than the datasheet's Range 2 figure for that frequency.
  • …you quote sleep current from the 25 °C column for a product that sits in a hot car or an outdoor cabinet. Symptom: field units die several times faster in summer than in the lab estimate.
Common wrong answer: "Halve the frequency and you double battery life." Only the dynamic power scales with f; the job still needs the same number of cycles, so the charge per job barely changes.

6. Say it like this

"CMOS power is α·C·V²·f for switching plus leakage. Each switching event costs C·V² regardless of the clock, so the strong lever is voltage, because it's squared; frequency alone changes power but not energy per job. Leakage is paid for every second the chip is powered and rises steeply with temperature, so you want to finish quickly at the lowest voltage that supports the speed, then cut clocks or power entirely."
02

Current, charge, energy, power: units that trip people up

BEGINNER▶

1. What is it?

Low-power work mixes four physical quantities: current, charge, power and energy. Mixing them up is the most common source of wrong battery-life estimates in interviews. The rule of thumb: a meter shows current, a battery stores charge (mAh) or energy (Wh), and battery life is "how much is stored" divided by "how fast you use it".

QuantityUnitPlain meaningWhy you care
Current IA (mA, µA, nA)How fast charge is flowing right nowWhat a meter shows. Datasheets quote MCU modes in current.
Charge Q = ∫I dtC = A·s; 1 mAh = 3.6 CHow much charge has flowed in totalWhat a battery stores. Battery life = usable charge ÷ average current.
Power P = V·IWHow fast energy is being usedWhat heats the chip, and what a harvester supplies.
Energy E = ∫P dtJ; 1 Wh = 3600 JTotal energy usedWhat matters when the voltage changes between battery and load (buck converters, harvesters).

2. How does it work, step by step? (computing an average current)

  1. Split one repeating period into phases where the current is roughly constant (sleep, wake-up, CPU active, radio).
  2. Multiply current × duration for each phase to get its charge. Use the shortcut: mA × ms = µC, µA × s = µC.
  3. Add the charges to get the charge per period.
  4. Divide by the period: I_avg = ΣQ / T. This is the single number that decides battery life.
  5. Divide the usable battery charge by I_avg to get the life in hours.
I_avg = Σ(I_k · t_k) / T Life[h] = C_usable[mAh] / I_avg[mA]

3. Worked examples

(a) A burst is "worth" a constant current:
    10 mA for 1 ms every 1 s  → 10 mA × 1 ms = 10 µC per second → 10 µA average
    exactly the same battery drain as a constant 10 µA load.

(b) Coin cell content:
    CR2032 ≈ 225 mAh = 225 × 3.6 C = 810 C
    at ≈ 3.0 V        → 810 C × 3 V ≈ 2430 J ≈ 0.675 Wh
    at 10 µA average  → 225 mAh / 0.010 mA = 22 500 h ≈ 2.6 years (before derating)

(c) A handy conversion: 1 µA flowing for one year
    = 0.001 mA × 8760 h = 8.76 mAh
    → a CR2032 (225 mAh) is "25.7 µA-years"; every extra 1 µA costs 3.9 % of it per year.

LDO vs buck: when to think in mAh and when in mWh

  1. LDO (linear regulator): the load current passes straight through. Ibattery = Iload + Iq (Iq = the regulator's own quiescent current). The excess voltage becomes heat, so an LDO saves no current. Think in mAh.
  2. Buck converter (step-down SMPS): converts power, so Ibattery ≈ (Vload × Iload) ÷ (Vbat × η), where η (eta) is efficiency. Think in energy (mWh).
Load: 1.8 V at 10 mA. Battery: 3.6 V.
  LDO : I_bat = 10 mA (+ Iq)                         → battery sees 10 mA
  Buck: I_bat = (1.8 V × 10 mA) / (3.6 V × 0.90)     → 18 mW / 3.24 V = 5.56 mA
  The buck nearly halves battery current at this load.

  • …you average the peak and sleep currents ("(10 mA + 1 µA) / 2 = 5 mA") instead of weighting by time. The estimate is off by 1000×. Always multiply by duration.
  • …you use mAh with a buck converter and a battery whose voltage falls (2× AA from 3.2 V to 2.0 V). As the battery voltage falls, the buck draws more current for the same load, so a mAh-only estimate is optimistic. Work in energy.
  • …you multiply mA by seconds and call it µC. 10 mA × 2 s = 20 mC (millicoulombs), not 20 µC: a 1000× error. Keep the unit pairs straight (mA·ms = µC, mA·s = mC, µA·s = µC).

"A battery stores charge, so battery life is usable mAh divided by the time-weighted average current. With an LDO I think in mAh because current passes straight through; with a buck converter I think in energy, because battery current then depends on the battery voltage and efficiency."

Cortex-M sleep: WFI, WFE, SLEEPDEEP, SLEEPONEXIT

BEGINNER▶

1. What is it?

On every Arm Cortex-M processor, the CPU core itself only knows two kinds of sleep: sleep (stop the core clock) and deep sleep (stop the core clock and send a signal to the chip vendor's power controller saying "you may now turn more things off"). What deep sleep actually means (Stop 0, Stop 2, Standby…) is decided by vendor registers, on STM32L4 by PWR_CR1.LPMS. The core enters either kind of sleep by executing one of two instructions:

  • WFI = Wait For Interrupt: "sleep here until an interrupt needs attention".
  • WFE = Wait For Event: "sleep here until an event happens, unless one already happened" (card 04).

Which sleep you get is chosen by a few bits in the SCR (System Control Register), part of the SCB (System Control Block) at address 0xE000_ED10, which is the same on every Cortex-M3/M4/M7/M33:

SCB->SCR @ 0xE000ED10
 bit  31 ............ 5 │    4      │  3  │     2     │      1      │  0
      reserved          │ SEVONPEND │ res │ SLEEPDEEP │ SLEEPONEXIT │ res

 SCR = 0x00 → WFI gives plain Sleep
 SCR = 0x04 → WFI gives deep sleep (Stop/Standby per PWR_CR1.LPMS)
 SCR = 0x06 → deep sleep, and go back to sleep automatically after each ISR
 SCR = 0x10 → any interrupt that becomes pending wakes WFE (even if disabled)

WFIinstructionSleep until an interrupt that would be taken if PRIMASK were 0 becomes pending (or a debug event or reset). 2. How does it work, step by step? (WFI)

  1. The core executes WFI.
  2. It checks: is an interrupt already pending that has high enough priority to run (ignoring PRIMASK)? If yes, WFI completes at once and the core keeps running. This is what makes the race-free idle loop below work.
  3. If no, the core stops its own clock. If SLEEPDEEP = 0 that is all (Sleep). If SLEEPDEEP = 1 it also raises its SLEEPDEEP output, and the ST power controller then performs the mode selected by LPMS.
  4. When an enabled interrupt becomes pending, the core wakes. If PRIMASK = 0, the ISR runs immediately and execution then continues after WFI. If PRIMASK = 1, execution continues after WFI without running the ISR; the ISR runs as soon as firmware clears PRIMASK.

3. Worked example: the lost wake-up race

Firmware wants to sleep only when there is no work. The naive version checks a flag and then sleeps:

WRONG
if (!work_pending) {     // (1) reads 0
                         // (2) IRQ fires HERE,
                         //     ISR sets work_pending = 1
    __WFI();             // (3) sleeps anyway!
}
RIGHT
__disable_irq();         // PRIMASK = 1
if (!work_pending) {     // IRQ now only PENDS
    __DSB();
    __WFI();             // pending IRQ → returns at once
}
__enable_irq();          // ISR runs here

Timeline of the wrong version: at t = 0 the main loop reads work_pending = 0. At t = 0.2 µs a UART byte arrives, the ISR runs and sets work_pending = 1, and returns. At t = 0.3 µs the main loop executes WFI. Nothing is pending any more (the ISR already ran), so the core sleeps with work queued. If the next interrupt is the RTC in 10 s, that UART byte waits 10 s. In the right version, the interrupt arriving at t = 0.2 µs cannot run (PRIMASK = 1), so it stays pending; WFI sees it and returns immediately; __enable_irq() lets the ISR run; the loop handles the work.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you check the flag with interrupts enabled (wrong version above). Symptom: occasional large latency spikes, or a device that "hangs" until a button press, impossible to reproduce under the debugger.
  • …you leave SLEEPDEEP = 1 after a Stop entry and later call plain __WFI() in an idle loop while waiting for DMA. Symptom: the device enters Stop instead of Sleep, DMA and the UART stop, transfers are lost, and the clock comes back on MSI.
  • …you mask interrupts with BASEPRI (as FreeRTOS does) instead of PRIMASK around WFI. Interrupts below the BASEPRI threshold then do not wake WFI at all. That is why FreeRTOS's tickless code uses __disable_irq() around the sleep.

6. Say it like this

"The Cortex-M core has sleep and deep sleep, selected by SCR.SLEEPDEEP; the vendor's PWR registers decide what deep sleep means. WFI wakes on a pending interrupt even with PRIMASK set, which gives the race-free idle pattern: disable interrupts, check for work, WFI, enable interrupts. That way an interrupt arriving between the check and the sleep stays pending and WFI returns immediately instead of losing the wake-up."

The event register: why WFE returns immediately

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

WFE (Wait For Event) does not look at interrupts directly. It looks at a single hidden flip-flop inside the core called the event register. The event register is one sticky bit, not a counter: something sets it, and only a WFE clears it. This one fact explains the famous symptom "WFE sometimes returns immediately without sleeping".

Things that set the event register (or wake a sleeping WFE):

  • An SEV instruction, executed by this core or by another core in a multi-core chip.
  • A pulse on the core's RXEV input (on STM32 this can come from an EXTI line configured as an event rather than an interrupt).
  • An exception being entered or returning (any ordinary ISR running).
  • With SCR.SEVONPEND = 1: any interrupt changing from not-pending to pending, even one disabled in the NVIC.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. The core executes WFE.
  2. If the event register is 1: clear it to 0 and continue with the next instruction. No sleep at all.
  3. If it is 0: sleep (or deep sleep if SLEEPDEEP = 1).
  4. When a wake-up event arrives, the core wakes and continues after WFE. The event that woke it is consumed (the register stays 0).
time ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────►
         ISR runs       main reaches loop     WFE #1        WFE #2          real event
         (entry/return) flag still 0          (bit=1 →      (bit=0 →        wakes core
event    0 ─────► 1 ────────────────────────► 0, no sleep)  SLEEPS) ......  → loop re-checks
register                                      returns at    ────────────
                                              once

3. Worked example: why a single WFE is never enough

volatile int flag = 0;          // set by some ISR

while (!flag) {
    __WFE();      // 1st call: event register already 1 (an earlier ISR ran) → clears it, returns at once
}                 // loop re-checks flag (still 0) → 2nd call: actually sleeps

/* Idiom to clear a stale event, then really sleep: */
__SEV();          // guarantee the bit is 1, whatever it was
__WFE();          // consume it → returns immediately, bit now 0
__WFE();          // sleeps until a NEW event

With 100 interrupts per second from an unrelated timer, the first WFE after each of them falls straight through. That costs only a few cycles because the loop re-checks flag and sleeps again. Without the loop, each spurious return would end the "wait" early.

Multi-core use: sleeping on a lock

/* Core A waits for a lock; core B releases it. */
void lock(volatile uint32_t *l) {
    while (__LDREXW(l) != 0 || __STREXW(1, l) != 0) {
        __WFE();                 // sleep instead of burning current while spinning
    }
    __DMB();
}
void unlock(volatile uint32_t *l) {
    __DMB();
    *l = 0;
    __DSB();                     // make the store visible first
    __SEV();                     // wake any core sleeping in WFE
}
CodeMeaning
__LDREXW / __STREXWExclusive load/store: atomically try to take the lock (STREX returns 0 on success).
__WFE()Lock busy: sleep until the other core signals, instead of spinning at full Run current.
__DSB(); __SEV();Publish the store, then set the event register on all cores so the waiter wakes and retries.

4. STM32 specifics

  • EXTI lines can be configured as events (EXTI_EMR1) instead of interrupts (EXTI_IMR1). An event-mode line wakes WFE with no ISR and no NVIC pending bit to clear.
  • The ST HAL's HAL_PWREx_EnterSTOP2Mode(PWR_STOPENTRY_WFE) does a SEV / WFE / WFE sequence internally for exactly the stale-event reason.
  • WFE vs WFI: WFE is the right choice for a spin-wait-with-sleep on a lock or flag shared with another core, or for waiting on an event-mode EXTI line. WFI is the right choice for an idle loop driven by interrupts.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you write one WFE followed by a busy loop (__WFE(); while(!flag){}). A stale event makes WFE return at once, and the CPU then spins at Run current. Symptom on a current analyser: no drop at all after the call, or a drop only sometimes (Scenario 4).
  • …you assume WFE returning means "my event happened". Any SEV from another core, or any ISR, can end the wait. Symptom: the code proceeds with data that is not ready.
  • …you rely on SEV; WFE; WFE without a loop. An event that arrives between the first and second WFE is consumed by the first and lost. Acceptable only inside a loop that re-checks the condition.

6. Say it like this

"WFE checks a one-bit sticky event register: if it's set, WFE clears it and returns immediately, otherwise it sleeps until an event such as SEV, an interrupt, or with SEVONPEND any interrupt becoming pending. Because earlier interrupts or SEVs leave the bit set, WFE can return spuriously, so I always put it inside a loop that re-checks the real condition. I use WFE for inter-core locks and event-mode wake-ups, and WFI with PRIMASK for interrupt-driven idle."

BEGINNER▶

1. What is it?

A power mode is a named combination of "which clocks run, which regulator is on, which blocks keep power". STM32L4/U5 chips offer a ladder of modes. Each step down the ladder reduces current by roughly 10–100× but gives something up: speed of wake-up, retained data, or the list of things that can wake you. Choosing a mode means trading those against each other.

2. How does it work, step by step? (what each step down turns off)

  1. Run → Sleep: only the CPU's own clock stops. Flash, SRAM, all peripherals and all oscillators keep running. Any interrupt wakes it in a few cycles. Saves the core's switching current (≈ 10 mA → ≈ 3 mA at 80 MHz).
  2. Sleep → Stop 0: every high-speed oscillator stops (PLL, HSE, HSI16, MSI). Without clocks, no digital logic switches, so switching current is gone. The main regulator stays on, which is why Stop 0 is still ≈ 113 µA but wakes fastest.
  3. Stop 0 → Stop 1: the main regulator is replaced by the tiny low-power regulator: ≈ 6.7 µA.
  4. Stop 1 → Stop 2: more of the digital domain is put into a reduced-leakage state, and fewer peripherals remain usable (only LPUART1, LPTIM1, I2C3 and a few others): ≈ 1.1 µA.
  5. Stop 2 → Standby: the core domain (CPU, SRAM1, peripheral registers) loses power completely. Only the backup domain, wake-up logic and optionally SRAM2 stay alive: ≈ 0.12 µA. Waking is a reset.
  6. Standby → Shutdown: even the brown-out detector and the internal low-speed oscillator are off: ≈ 0.03 µA. Only wake-up pins and an RTC on the external crystal can wake it.

Entry on STM32L4 is always the same three moves: set PWR_CR1.LPMS, set SCB->SCR.SLEEPDEEP, then WFI or WFE.

3. Worked example: is the deeper mode worth it?

Device sleeps 99.9 % of the time. Compare the sleep floor over one year:
  Stop 2 + RTC  : 1.4 µA  × 8760 h = 12.3 mAh/year
  Standby + RTC : 0.42 µA × 8760 h =  3.7 mAh/year
  Saving        : 8.6 mAh/year = 3.8 % of a CR2032 per year

But Standby wakes through reset. If each reboot + re-init costs 2 ms at 1 mA = 2 µC:
  wake every 1 s   → 2 µC/s  = 2 µA extra      → Standby LOSES (saves only ≈ 1 µA)
  wake every 60 s  → 2/60    = 0.033 µA extra  → Standby WINS
  break-even T     = 2 µC / (1.4 − 0.42) µA ≈ 2 s

Read the ladder as decades: mA → 100 µA → µA → 100 nA → 10 nA. Lower is only better if the cost of getting back up is paid rarely enough.

2. How does it work, step by step? (a Standby wake-up)

  1. Before Standby, firmware stores the little state it needs (a sample counter, a sequence number) in a backup register or in SRAM2 with PWR_CR3.RRS = 1.
  2. Standby entry cuts power to the core domain. CPU registers, the stack, SRAM1 and every peripheral register disappear.
  3. A wake-up event (RTC alarm or a WKUP pin edge) restarts the regulator. The chip goes through a reset: execution starts at Reset_Handler, exactly like a power-on.
  4. Startup code copies .data and zeroes .bss again (see the Memory & Startup topic), so every global variable is back to its initial value.
  5. Firmware must find out why it started: read PWR_SR1.SBF (StandBy Flag: 1 = we came from Standby) and PWR_SR1.WUFx (which wake-up pin), plus RCC_CSR reset flags. Then clear them via PWR_SCR.
  6. If it was a Standby wake-up, restore state from the backup register / SRAM2 instead of doing first-boot initialisation.

3. Worked example: a counter that survives Standby

int main(void) {
    RCC->APB1ENR1 |= RCC_APB1ENR1_PWREN;      // clock the PWR block so we can read it
    bool from_standby = (PWR->SR1 & PWR_SR1_SBF) != 0;
    uint32_t wkup     =  PWR->SR1 & 0x1Fu;      // WUF1..WUF5 bits
    PWR->SCR = PWR_SCR_CSBF | PWR_SCR_CWUF;     // clear SBF and all WUFx

    PWR->CR1 |= PWR_CR1_DBP;                    // unlock backup domain writes
    uint32_t n = from_standby ? RTC->BKP0R : 0; // restore or start at 0
    n++;
    RTC->BKP0R = n;                             // survives Standby (backup domain)

    do_measurement(n);
    enter_mode(3);                              // LPMS = 011 → Standby; never returns
}

  • In Standby/Shutdown, pins go to analog/high-Z (floating) unless you enable PWR_CR3.APC (Apply Pull-up/down Configuration) and program PWR_PUCRx / PWR_PDCRx. That is how you keep a load switch's enable low or a radio's reset line defined while the core domain is off.
  • The backup domain (RTC, LSE, backup registers, RCC_BDCR) survives every mode and can run from the VBAT pin when the main supply is removed.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you keep a global variable for "number of samples" and use Standby. It is reset to 0 on every wake-up. Symptom: the counter never increases; the device behaves as if it had just been switched on.
  • …you don't clear WUFx before entering Standby. Symptom: average current ≈ 1 mA, the device reboots every few milliseconds, reset-cause logging shows endless Standby wake-ups (Scenario 10).
  • …you forget APC and a radio's enable line floats. Symptom: the radio half-wakes and draws mA during "Standby".

"Stop modes keep SRAM and peripheral registers and resume after the WFI; only the fast clocks are lost. Wake-up sources shrink as you go deeper: in Stop 2 only LPUART1, LPTIM1, I2C3, RTC and EXTI, in Standby only the WKUP pins, RTC and IWDG."

Clock restoration after Stop (the most common low-power bug)

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

When an STM32 enters any Stop mode, it switches off every fast clock: the PLL (Phase-Locked Loop, the circuit that multiplies a clock up to 80 MHz), the HSE (High-Speed External crystal), HSI16 (16 MHz internal RC) and MSI (Multi-Speed Internal RC). When the chip wakes up, the hardware restarts only one internal oscillator and runs the CPU from it. It does not restart the PLL. Your code resumes after WFI believing it still runs at 80 MHz, but it now runs at 4 or 16 MHz. Every baud rate, timer period and delay computed for 80 MHz is now wrong. This is the single most common low-power bug.

  • STM32L4/U5: wakes on MSI (at the range in RCC_CR.MSISRANGE, 4 MHz by default) if RCC_CFGR.STOPWUCK = 0, or on HSI16 if STOPWUCK = 1.
  • STM32F4: always wakes on HSI (16 MHz).

2. How does it work, step by step? (the correct wake-up sequence)

  1. WFI returns. The core is running on MSI 4 MHz, Range 1 voltage, with the flash wait states you had set for 80 MHz (too many wait states is safe, just slow).
  2. Clear SLEEPDEEP so a later idle WFI is light sleep.
  3. Voltage first: make sure the regulator is in Range 1 (PWR_CR1.VOS = 01) and wait for PWR_SR2.VOSF = 0. The core needs 1.2 V before it can run at 80 MHz.
  4. Restart the source: turn on HSE (or use MSI as the PLL input) and wait for its ready flag. A crystal takes 1–2 ms; MSI takes µs.
  5. Restart the PLL and wait for PLLRDY (lock time ≈ tens of µs).
  6. Flash wait states up: FLASH_ACR.LATENCY = 4 for 80 MHz in Range 1, and read it back to make sure it took effect.
  7. Switch SYSCLK to the PLL (RCC_CFGR.SW = 11) and wait for SWS = 11.
  8. Resume SysTick (and anything else you paused). Only now are BRR, timer prescalers and delays correct again.

Going down in speed (before sleep, or when switching to a slower clock) the order is reversed: lower the clock first, then the wait states, then the voltage range. The rule behind both orders: at every instant, the voltage must support the frequency, and the flash must have enough wait states for the frequency.

3. Worked example: decoding the garbage on the UART

USART2 at 80 MHz:   BRR = 80 000 000 / 115 200 = 694.4 → 694 (0x2B6)
After Stop 2:       PCLK1 = 4 MHz (MSI range 6)
                    baud  = 4 000 000 / 694 = 5764 Bd
Ratio: 115 200 / 5764 = 20 = 80 MHz / 4 MHz  → the clock ratio, every time

5. Design choice: do you even need the PLL after waking?

  • Restoring costs time and energy: MSI start ≈ µs, PLL lock ≈ tens of µs, HSE crystal start-up 1–2 ms. For a 50 µs task that can double the awake time.
  • Often the right answer is to do the wake-up work on MSI (up to 48 MHz on the L4, no PLL needed) and only spin up the PLL for heavy jobs. Or choose STOPWUCK = 1 so you wake directly on HSI16 and derive the UART from it.
  • Peripherals that must work during Stop (LPUART1, LPTIM1, I2C3) need a kernel clock that survives: LSE (32.768 kHz; LPUART up to 9600 baud) or HSI16 requested on demand (the peripheral wakes HSI16 itself, which costs current while it runs).

6. What goes wrong if…

  • …you never restore the clock: UART garbage, timers 20× slow, HAL_Delay(10) lasting 200 ms, SPI sensors read at the wrong rate, ADC sampling time changed.
  • …you switch to 80 MHz before raising the wait states: the flash cannot deliver instructions fast enough; symptom is a HardFault or random wrong behaviour immediately after the clock switch.
  • …you raise the frequency before VOSF = 0: the core runs above the speed its voltage supports; symptom is intermittent crashes that depend on temperature.

7. Say it like this

"Stop modes switch off the PLL and the high-speed oscillators, and the STM32L4 wakes on MSI or HSI16 depending on STOPWUCK. So right after WFI returns I call SystemClock_Config: voltage range first, then the oscillator and PLL lock, then flash wait states, then switch SYSCLK. If I don't, a UART set for 80 MHz runs 20 times too slow. For short wake-ups I often skip the PLL entirely and do the work on MSI, because the lock time can be longer than the job."

GPIO leakage, floating inputs and unused pins

BEGINNER▶

1. What is it?

Every pin configured as a digital input connects to a small CMOS input buffer (a Schmitt trigger: an inverter with a little hysteresis) inside the chip. That buffer draws almost no current when its input is firmly at 0 V or firmly at VDD. But if nothing drives the pin and there is no pull resistor, the pin floats: its voltage drifts with leakage, nearby signals, humidity, even a finger near the board. When it drifts to the middle (around VDD/2), both transistors of the input buffer are partly on and current flows straight from VDD to ground through them. That is tens to hundreds of µA per pin, in a chip whose whole sleep budget is 1 µA.

  3. PUPDR (also 2 bits per pin): 00 = no pull, 01 = pull-up, 10 = pull-down. Use pulls only for pins that must stay digital.
  4. Once configured, the port's clock (RCC_AHB2ENR.GPIOxEN) can be turned off: the configuration is retained, and the port stops consuming clock power in Run/Sleep.

5. Look outside the pin too

  • STM32L4/U5 reset state is analog mode (except the debug pins). STM32F4 resets to floating input (MODER = 0): porting F4 init code that "initialises all pins as input" to an L4 re-introduces the leakage (Scenario 1).

  • …unused pins are floating inputs: sleep current is milliamps, noisy, and changes when you touch the board or breathe on it. The classic diagnostic is that the current depends on your hand.
  • …a pull-up fights an output: a steady extra 75 µA per pin, not noisy. Find it by computing V/R for every pull on the schematic.
  • …you set PA13/PA14 to analog in the debug build: the debugger disconnects the moment the code runs; recover by connecting under reset.

"A floating digital input drifts towards mid-rail, where both transistors of the input buffer conduct, so it can draw tens to hundreds of microamps per pin, and it changes with humidity or a hand near the board. Peripheral clock gating, voltage scaling, LDO vs SMPS, brown-out

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

This card covers four hardware knobs that set the awake current and the robustness of a low-power design:

  • Clock gating: turning off the clock of each peripheral you are not using.
  • Voltage scaling (DVFS = Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling): running the core at a lower internal voltage when full speed is not needed.
  • LDO vs SMPS: how the internal core voltage is produced, which decides how much battery current each mA of core current costs.
  • Brown-out: what happens when the battery voltage dips.

2. Clock gating, step by step

  1. RCC_AHBxENR / RCC_APBxENRy (RCC = Reset and Clock Control) have one "enable" bit per peripheral. 1 = the peripheral's bus clock runs in Run mode.
  2. A clocked-but-idle timer or UART still toggles its clock tree and register flops (α = 1 for the clock net), roughly a few µA/MHz each on the L4. At 80 MHz that is ≈ 100–200 µA per idle peripheral.
  3. The L4 also has RCC_xxxSMENR registers (Sleep and Stop Mode ENable): one bit per peripheral that says whether its clock keeps running while the CPU is in Sleep mode. They reset to all ones, so by default every enabled peripheral keeps running while the CPU sleeps.
  4. Analog blocks are not covered by clock gating at all: the ADC's internal regulator, VREFBUF, comparators, op-amps, the USB transceiver, the temperature sensor and VREFINT channels draw bias current until explicitly powered down.
/* Sleep while DMA receives on SPI2: keep only what is needed clocked in Sleep */
RCC->AHB1SMENR  = RCC_AHB1SMENR_DMA1SMEN | RCC_AHB1SMENR_SRAM1SMEN;  // DMA1 + RAM it writes
RCC->APB1SMENR1 = RCC_APB1SMENR1_SPI2SMEN;                           // SPI2 only
RCC->APB2SMENR  = 0;                                                 // nothing on APB2

/* ADC to its lowest-power state (ADC must be idle) */
ADC1->CR |= ADC_CR_ADDIS;  while (ADC1->CR & ADC_CR_ADEN) { }        // 1. disable, wait
ADC1->CR &= ~ADC_CR_ADVREGEN;                                        // 2. internal regulator off
ADC1->CR |=  ADC_CR_DEEPPWD;                                         // 3. deep power-down
RCC->AHB2ENR &= ~RCC_AHB2ENR_ADCEN;                                  // 4. then gate its clock

AHB1SMENR = DMA1 | SRAM1In Sleep, keep clocking DMA1 and the SRAM it writes into; everything else on AHB1 stops.

3. Voltage scaling, step by step

  • STM32L4: Range 1 VCORE ≈ 1.2 V, up to 80 MHz. Range 2 ≈ 1.0 V, up to 26 MHz. Low-power run uses the LP regulator, up to 2 MHz. Set with PWR_CR1.VOS; after raising it, wait for PWR_SR2.VOSF = 0.
  • STM32U5: Range 1–4 (160 / 110 / 55 / 25 MHz).
  • Flash wait states (extra clock cycles the CPU waits for each flash read) depend on both range and frequency. L476 Range 1: 0 WS ≤ 16 MHz, 1 WS ≤ 32, 2 WS ≤ 48, 3 WS ≤ 64, 4 WS ≤ 80 MHz. Range 2: 0 WS ≤ 6 MHz, 1 WS ≤ 12, 2 WS ≤ 18, 3 WS ≤ 26 MHz.
Same 26 MHz clock, two voltage ranges (switching energy ∝ V²):
  Range 1 (1.2 V): 1.44 units/cycle      Range 2 (1.0 V): 1.00 units/cycle
  → staying in Range 1 at 26 MHz costs 44 % more switching energy for nothing

4. LDO vs SMPS, with numbers

  1. An LDO passes the load current straight through: Iin = Iout + Iq. Its efficiency is Vcore/Vin ≈ 1.2/3.3 = 36 %: almost two thirds of the battery power is burnt as heat in the regulator.
  2. An SMPS (buck) converts power: Iin ≈ Iout·Vcore/(Vin·η). At 1.2 V core, 3.3 V input and η = 85 %: Iin = Iout × 1.2/(3.3 × 0.85) = 0.43 × Iout.
  3. That is the main reason the STM32U5 (internal SMPS) reaches ≈ 19.5 µA/MHz while the L4 (LDO) needs ≈ 100 µA/MHz. L4 "xxxP" parts accept an external SMPS on their VDD12 pins.
  4. At µA loads the SMPS's own switching losses and quiescent current dominate, so parts switch back to an LDO or the LP regulator in Stop. The U5 selects with PWR_CR3.REGSEL; the nRF52 with DCDCEN. An SMPS also adds ripple and EMI, which matters for ADC precision and RF.

5. Brown-out, with numbers

  • The L4 BOR (Brown-Out Reset) is always on except in Shutdown, with levels from BOR0 ≈ 1.7 V up to BOR4 ≈ 2.9 V (set in option bytes). The PVD (Programmable Voltage Detector) gives an early-warning interrupt.
  • A coin cell's internal resistance (10–20 Ω fresh, far more when cold or nearly empty) turns a 15 mA radio burst into a voltage dip of 15 mA × 20 Ω = 0.3 V. A 2.9 V cell then reads 2.6 V at the chip. With BOR4 (2.9 V) set, every radio burst resets the MCU.
  • A bulk capacitor supplies the burst instead. If the capacitor alone had to deliver 15 mA for 3 ms while drooping at most 0.2 V: C = I·t/ΔV = 15 mA × 3 ms / 0.2 V = 225 µF. In practice the cell supplies part of it, so 47–100 µF of low-leakage capacitance is common. Check the capacitor's own leakage: an electrolytic can leak more than the whole sleep budget.
  • Shutdown mode has no BOR: a sagging battery can leave the part in an undefined state, which is a reason to pick Standby for products with slowly decaying supplies.

6. What goes wrong if…

  • …generated init code enables every peripheral clock "just in case" and SMENR stays at its reset value: Sleep current at 80 MHz is 6–7 mA instead of 3 mA (Scenario 7).
  • …the ADC is enabled once at boot and never powered down: a steady extra current in every mode that keeps the regulator on; clock gating does not remove it.
  • …the BOR level is set without considering the loaded cell voltage: random resets during radio TX or flash writes on old or cold batteries.

7. Say it like this

"Clock gating stops switching power per peripheral: on the L4 the ENR bits gate in Run and the SMENR bits decide what keeps running in Sleep, and SMENR resets to all ones, so I trim it. Analog blocks like the ADC regulator need an explicit power-down. I run at the lowest voltage range that supports the clock because energy goes with V², and I prefer an SMPS at milliamp loads because an LDO wastes the voltage difference. Finally I size the BOR level and a bulk capacitor for the battery's voltage dip under radio bursts."

Duty cycling, race-to-sleep vs slow-and-steady

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

A duty-cycled device does a fixed job (say, read a sensor and filter the data: N CPU cycles) every period T, and sleeps the rest of the time. You choose the CPU clock for the job. Race-to-sleep (or race-to-idle) says: run as fast as possible, finish early, sleep sooner. Slow-and-steady says: run slowly at a lower power. Which is right depends on one thing: how much current is "on" while the chip is awake regardless of the clock.

2. How does it work, step by step? (the model)

  1. While awake, current has two parts: a part proportional to the clock (k·f: the switching current of the core) and a fixed part that is on whenever the chip is awake (Ifix: regulator, flash, analog blocks, the sensor's own current, oscillators, leakage).
  2. The job takes tact = N/f seconds.
  3. Charge for the job = current × time:
I_act(f) = I_fix + k·f t_act = N / f
Q_job = I_act·t_act = I_fix·N/f + k·N
  1. The k·N term is the switching charge for the job: it does not depend on f (card 01: energy per cycle is CV²).
  2. The I_fix·N/f term falls as f rises: a faster clock means the fixed current flows for less time.
  3. So with any fixed current at all, running faster wins, unless the higher clock also raises k (a higher voltage range), raises N (more flash wait states), or adds a fixed cost per wake-up (PLL lock, HSE start-up), or the work is I/O-bound (waiting for a slow sensor, so a faster CPU only waits faster).

4. What breaks race-to-sleep (with the reason)

  • Voltage range jump: above 26 MHz the L4 needs Range 1 (1.2 V), so switching energy per cycle rises by 44 %.
  • Flash wait states: 4 WS at 80 MHz versus 3 WS at 26 MHz in Range 2. The ART accelerator (ST's flash prefetch/cache) hides most of it, but branch-heavy code takes more cycles, so N grows.
  • PLL / HSE start-up per wake-up: ≈ 40 µs of PLL lock or 1–2 ms of crystal start-up is pure overhead. For a 50 µs job, that overhead dominates.
  • I/O-bound work: if the job is "start ADC, wait 1 ms for conversion", a faster CPU just waits at higher current. Sleep (or use DMA) during the wait instead.

  • …you slow the clock to "save power" with a sensor powered during the job: the sensor's current flows for longer and average current rises. Symptom: the change makes battery life worse.
  • …you always run at 80 MHz for a 30 µs task: the PLL lock time (≈ 40 µs) exceeds the job. Symptom: the Profiler shows the "wake-up / PLL" slice larger than the "CPU active" slice.
  • …you optimise without measuring: the optimum moves with the job size, Ifix and the part. The Profiler's frequency sweep shows the curve live.

6. Say it like this

"Energy per job is the fixed current times the active time plus the switching charge, and the switching part is set by voltage, not frequency. So I run at the fastest clock the lowest usable voltage range allows, finish, and enter the deepest sleep mode I can tolerate. Then I measure, because wait states and start-up costs move the optimum."

Average current and battery life calculation

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

The battery-life calculation is the core arithmetic of every low-power interview and every design review. It has only two formulas. The skill is in listing every phase honestly and in derating the battery realistically.

I_avg = (I_sleep·t_sleep + I_wake·t_wake + I_act·t_act + I_tx·t_tx) / T
Life[h] = C[mAh] × derating / (I_avg[mA] + I_selfdischarge[mA])
SymbolMeaning
TPeriod of the repeating pattern (for example 10 s between measurements)
I_x · t_xCharge of phase x (current × duration)
CNominal (label) capacity of the battery in mAh
deratingFraction of the label you can really use (0.6–0.8)
I_selfdischargeSelf-discharge expressed as an equivalent current: C × (%/year) / 8760 h

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. List the phases of one period: sleep, wake-up, CPU active, sensor, radio. Get each current from the datasheet (maximum column for worst case) or, better, from a measurement.
  2. Compute each charge (mA × ms = µC).
  3. Sum and divide by T → I_avg.
  4. Find the biggest slice; that is where optimisation pays.
  5. Derate the battery for pulse load, temperature and cut-off voltage.
  6. Add self-discharge as an equivalent current and divide.

3. Worked example

STM32L476 in Stop 2 with RTC (1.4 µA), waking every 10 s for 2 ms at 80 MHz (≈ 10 mA including the sensor), plus a BLE advertisement of 3 ms at 8 mA:

Sleep:   1.4 µA × 9.995 s = 14.0 µC
Active:  10 mA  × 2 ms    = 20.0 µC
Radio:   8 mA   × 3 ms    = 24.0 µC
Total    58.0 µC per 10 s  →  I_avg = 5.8 µA

Sleep is only 24 % of the budget: the radio (41 %) and the active window (34 %) dominate.

CR2032: 225 mAh × 0.80 = 180 mAh usable
Self-discharge: 225 mAh × 1 %/yr / 8760 h = 0.26 µA equivalent
Life = 180 mAh / (5.8 + 0.26) µA = 180 / 0.00606 mA = 29 700 h ≈ 3.4 years

Worst-case version: the same design at 60 °C with maximum datasheet currents might have Stop 2 at ≈ 5 µA (×3–4 leakage), active at 12 mA. Sleep becomes 50 µC, active 24 µC, radio 24 µC: 98 µC / 10 s = 9.8 µA → 180 / 0.0101 mA ≈ 17 800 h ≈ 2.0 years. Quote the range, not only the best case.

4. Derating: why the label lies

  • Rated capacity is measured at a gentle continuous load (CR2032: about 0.2 mA down to 2.0 V) at 20–25 °C. Pulse loads, cold, and a higher cut-off voltage (your BOR level) all reduce what you actually get. If your BOR is at 2.4 V, you never use the last part of the discharge curve.
  • Temperature: cold raises internal resistance and cuts capacity (alkaline cells are poor below 0 °C). Heat raises self-discharge and MCU leakage: Stop 2 at 85 °C can be roughly 10× the 25 °C figure.
  • Self-discharge (typical): Li-MnO₂ coin ≈ 1 %/yr, Li-SOCl₂ (ER14505) < 1 %/yr but suffers passivation (a voltage delay after long idle, so pulses may need a hybrid capacitor), alkaline ≈ 2–3 %/yr, Li-ion/LiPo ≈ 2–3 %/month plus the protection IC's current.
  • Typical margin: design for 60–80 % of rated capacity, use maximum (not typical) datasheet currents for the worst-case estimate, and quote battery life at your hottest operating temperature.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you forget self-discharge on a LiPo: 1000 mAh × 2 %/month = 20 mAh/month = 27 µA equivalent, more than most sleeping MCUs. A "5-year" LiPo design is impossible regardless of firmware.
  • …you use 25 °C typical currents: field life comes in at half the estimate in hot climates.
  • …you omit a phase (the RRC tail of a cellular modem, a sensor's conversion time, an LED blink): that omitted phase is usually the biggest one.

6. Say it like this

"I list each phase with current and duration, sum current times time over one period and divide by the period to get the average current. Then usable capacity, derated to 60–80 % for pulses, temperature and cut-off, divided by average current plus self-discharge as an equivalent current gives the life. For example 58 µC every 10 s is 5.8 µA, and 180 mAh over about 6 µA is about 3.4 years. I'd quote a worst case with max currents at the hottest temperature too."

Measuring current: shunts, dynamic range, and debuggers

INTERMEDIATE▶

1. What is it?

You cannot optimise what you cannot measure, and low-power current is unusually hard to measure. A sleeping node draws 300 nA; the same node transmitting on LTE draws 200 mA. That is almost a million to one range, with transitions taking microseconds. This card explains why ordinary tools fail and what to use instead, and why the debugger itself is the most common reason "Stop mode doesn't work".

2. How does it work, step by step? (why one shunt can't do it)

  1. Most meters measure current by passing it through a small resistor (a shunt) and measuring the voltage across it: V = I × R.
  2. To resolve 300 nA you need a big shunt: with 10 Ω, 300 nA gives 3 µV, still lost in amplifier offset and noise.
  3. But the same 10 Ω shunt at 200 mA drops V = 0.2 A × 10 Ω = 2 V. The device's supply falls by 2 V and it browns out. This drop is called the burden voltage.
  4. A good tool therefore switches shunts automatically within microseconds: a large shunt while the device sleeps, a small one when the current jumps.

3. Worked example: the multimeter trap

A handheld DMM on its µA range: burden ≈ 100 µV per µA (≈ 100 Ω shunt)
  Sleep 2 µA   → drop 0.2 mV        fine
  Wake 10 mA   → drop 10 mA × 100 Ω = 1.0 V    → a 3.0 V supply becomes 2.0 V
  → the MCU browns out and resets on its first wake-up; the reading shows a "spike"
    and the device never behaves as in the field.

Integration beats peaks: a Joulescope shows 58 µC over a 10 s window directly
  → I_avg = 58 µC / 10 s = 5.8 µA, no eyeballing of peak heights.

  1. Flash the firmware.
  2. Disconnect the probe (on a Nucleo board: open the ST-LINK jumpers and measure at the IDD jumper).
  3. Power-cycle the board so DBGMCU_CR is reset.
  4. Wait for it to settle (crystal start-up, sensor init, first radio activity).
  5. Measure with an auto-ranging analyser over many periods; read the integrated charge.
  6. Hold the MCU in reset once to measure the rest of the board alone (regulator Iq, sensors, LEDs, pull-ups).

  • …you measure with the debugger attached: a flat 1.5–2 mA with no drop at WFI (Scenario 2). You conclude "Stop mode is broken" and waste days.
  • …you use a DMM for the whole profile: the device resets on wake-up, or you read a meaningless slowly-averaged number.
  • …you look at peaks instead of charge: a 200 mA, 1 ms peak looks scary but is 0.2 mC; a 1 mA, 10 s tail looks harmless but is 10 mC, 50× worse.

7. Say it like this

"Low-power current spans almost six decades with microsecond edges, so a fixed shunt either can't resolve the sleep current or drops so much voltage at the peak that the device browns out. I always measure with the probe disconnected and after a power cycle, because DBGMCU_CR's DBG_STOP keeps the clocks running in Stop and is only cleared by a power-on reset."

Tickless idle in an RTOS (FreeRTOS and Zephyr)

ADVANCED▶

1. What is it?

An RTOS (Real-Time Operating System such as FreeRTOS or Zephyr) normally keeps time with a periodic tick interrupt, typically 1000 times per second (1 kHz) from the Cortex-M SysTick timer. Every tick wakes the CPU, runs the scheduler, and goes back to sleep, even when no task has anything to do. Tickless idle stops that periodic tick whenever only the idle task can run, and instead programs a single timer interrupt for the moment the next task actually needs to wake up.

2. How does it work, step by step?

Ticking (1 kHz):  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  ← 1000 wake-ups/s
                  ^ each tick: wake, stack, scheduler, unstack, sleep (≈ 10 µs at 10 mA)

Tickless:         |                                                     |      ← 1 wake-up when
                  sleep in Stop 2 on LPTIM, 1 timeout programmed        task's delay ends
  1. The idle task runs because every other task is blocked. The kernel computes how many ticks until the next task is due (xExpectedIdleTime).
  2. If that is at least configEXPECTED_IDLE_TIME_BEFORE_SLEEP ticks (≥ 2), the kernel calls the port's portSUPPRESS_TICKS_AND_SLEEP().
  3. The port masks interrupts, asks the kernel whether it is still OK to sleep (a task may have become ready in the meantime), stops SysTick, and programs a one-shot low-power timer for the expected idle time.
  4. It enters sleep or Stop mode with WFI.
  5. On wake-up (timer expired, or some other interrupt woke it early), it measures how long it actually slept and tells the kernel with vTaskStepTick(), so the kernel's tick count jumps forward.
  6. It restarts SysTick and unmasks interrupts; the waking ISR runs.
  • FreeRTOS: configUSE_TICKLESS_IDLE = 1 uses the built-in SysTick version: CPU Sleep only, because SysTick stops in deep sleep, and the sleep length is limited by SysTick's 24-bit counter (2²⁴ / 80 MHz = 0.21 s). = 2 means you provide vPortSuppressTicksAndSleep() yourself, typically with LPTIM1 or the RTC, which keep running in Stop 2.
  • Zephyr: CONFIG_PM=y plus devicetree power-states with min-residency-us and exit-latency-us per state. The default policy picks the deepest state whose residency fits the next timeout. Devices take part through the Device PM API (suspend/resume, runtime PM). The kernel is tickless (CONFIG_TICKLESS_KERNEL, on by default on most SoCs).

3. Worked example: what the tick costs

1 kHz tick, each tick ≈ 10 µs awake at 80 MHz (≈ 10 mA):
  10 mA × 10 µs = 0.1 µC per tick × 1000 ticks/s = 100 µC/s = 100 µA average
  and Stop mode is impossible (SysTick needs the CPU clock) → floor = Sleep ≈ 3 mA
Tickless on LPTIM1 + Stop 2, one task waking every 1 s:
  1.4 µA floor + one wake-up per second → a few µA. ≈ 1000× better than ticking in Sleep.

Tick conversion with LPTIM on LSE (32 768 Hz) and a 1000 Hz RTOS tick:
  LPTIM counts per tick = 32768 / 1000 = 32.768   (not an integer!)
  slept 1000 LPTIM counts → 1000 / 32.768 = 30.52 ticks → step 30, carry 0.52 tick (17 counts)
  Trick: configTICK_RATE_HZ = 1024 → 32768 / 1024 = exactly 32 counts per tick, no remainder.

4. Code, line by line

void vPortSuppressTicksAndSleep(TickType_t xExpected) {
    if (xExpected > MAX_LPTIM_TICKS) xExpected = MAX_LPTIM_TICKS;
    __disable_irq();
    if (eTaskConfirmSleepModeStatus() == eAbortSleep) {  // a task became ready
        __enable_irq(); return;
    }
    SysTick->CTRL &= ~SysTick_CTRL_ENABLE_Msk;
    lptim_start_oneshot(xExpected * LPTIM_TICKS_PER_RTOS_TICK);
    configPRE_SLEEP_PROCESSING(xExpected);              // gate clocks, pins
    enter_stop2_wfi();                                  // WFI with PRIMASK=1
    configPOST_SLEEP_PROCESSING(xExpected);             // SystemClock_Config()
    uint32_t slept = lptim_elapsed() / LPTIM_TICKS_PER_RTOS_TICK;
    lptim_stop();
    vTaskStepTick(slept);                               // correct the kernel's time
    SysTick->LOAD = SYSTICK_RELOAD; SysTick->VAL = 0;
    SysTick->CTRL |= SysTick_CTRL_ENABLE_Msk;
    __enable_irq();                                     // the waking ISR runs now
}

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you assume you slept the full xExpected: an early wake-up by a UART interrupt makes the kernel's time jump ahead; delays end too early.
  • …you always round down and never carry the remainder: the RTOS clock drifts slow by up to one tick per sleep. With 10 sleeps per second that is up to 10 ms/s = 1 %.
  • …you use configUSE_TICKLESS_IDLE = 1 and expect Stop 2: the built-in version only uses Sleep and wakes every ≤ 0.21 s at 80 MHz. Symptom: average ≈ 3 mA (Scenario 11).
  • …one task calls vTaskDelay(1) in a loop: the idle time is never ≥ 2 ticks, so tickless never engages.

6. Say it like this

"A 1 kHz tick wakes the CPU a thousand times a second and prevents Stop mode. Tickless idle stops SysTick when only the idle task can run, programs a low-power timer like LPTIM or the RTC for the next deadline, sleeps with interrupts masked after confirming no task became ready, then measures the actual sleep time and calls vTaskStepTick. The pitfalls are early wake-ups, remainder drift, restoring clocks after Stop, and tasks that poll every tick."

Radio power profiles: BLE, LTE-M / NB-IoT (PSM, eDRX)

ADVANCED▶

1. What is it?

In a wireless product the radio, not the MCU, usually dominates the battery budget. A BLE radio draws 5–8 mA while active; a cellular (LTE-M / NB-IoT) modem draws 100–500 mA peaks and several mA while "connected". Both standards define ways to keep the radio off most of the time: the advertising/connection interval for BLE, and PSM and eDRX for cellular IoT. The firmware's job is to request them correctly and then make sure the application does not keep waking the radio.

Terms: BLE = Bluetooth Low Energy. LTE-M (Cat-M1) and NB-IoT = cellular standards for low-power devices. UE = User Equipment (the device). RRC = Radio Resource Control, the "connected" state. TAU = Tracking Area Update, the periodic "I'm still here" message. PSM = Power Saving Mode. eDRX = extended Discontinuous Reception.

2. BLE: how the numbers work

  • nRF52832/52840 class with DC/DC on: TX at 0 dBm ≈ 5–7 mA, RX ≈ 5–6 mA, System ON idle with RTC ≈ 2–3 µA.
  • One advertising event (3 channels, ~31-byte payload) ≈ 2–4 ms of radio activity including ramp-up ≈ 10–25 µC.
  • Average = event charge ÷ interval: 15 µC every 1 s = 15 µA; every 100 ms = 150 µA; every 10 s = 1.5 µA.
  • In a connection, the connection interval and peripheral latency (how many intervals the peripheral may skip) play the same role. A 1 s interval with latency is far below a 100 ms one.
  • Coin-cell pulse budget: put a 47–100 µF low-leakage capacitor near the radio. Capacitor leakage (an electrolytic can leak µA) can exceed the sleep current.

3. LTE-M / NB-IoT: PSM and eDRX step by step

  1. Uplink: the device sends its data at 100–500 mA for tens of ms (longer in poor coverage, where repetitions are used).
  2. RRC inactivity tail: the network keeps the connection open for its inactivity timer (often 5–20 s, set by the operator) at mA-level current. RAI (Release Assistance Indication, Rel-14) tells the network "no more data", so the release happens at once.
  3. Idle, active timer T3324: the device listens for paging (reachable for downlink) for T3324 seconds.
  4. PSM: T3324 expires, the radio switches off but the device stays registered. Current ≈ 1–10 µA (nRF9160 ≈ 2.7 µA). It is unreachable for downlink.
  5. Wake: when the periodic TAU timer T3412-extended (up to ≈ 413 days) expires, or the application has uplink data, the device wakes without a full re-attach.
  6. eDRX (Rel-13) is the alternative when downlink reachability matters: in idle mode the paging cycle is extended (LTE-M up to 2621.44 s ≈ 43.7 min, NB-IoT up to 10485.76 s ≈ 2.9 h), with a Paging Time Window (PTW) during which the device listens.

  • …an MQTT or TCP keep-alive (or NTP, or a cloud SDK heartbeat) is shorter than the PSM cycle: every keep-alive reopens RRC, restarts the tail and T3324, so the modem never reaches PSM. Symptom: average current in mA, a regular burst every 60 s on the trace (Scenario 8).
  • …you never read the granted timers: the network may refuse PSM or grant different values. Symptom: the device behaves differently on another operator.
  • …BLE advertises every 20 ms "for fast connection" permanently: 15 µC × 50/s = 750 µA, a coin cell lasts two weeks. Use fast advertising for 30 s after a button press, then slow down.

6. Say it like this

"For BLE, average current is roughly event charge divided by the advertising or connection interval: about 15 µC per advertisement, so 1 s gives about 15 µA. RTC accuracy: LSE vs LSI, and the backup domain

ADVANCED▶

1. What is it?

The RTC (Real-Time Clock) keeps the time of day and wakes the device while everything else is off. It is only as accurate as the 32 kHz clock that drives it. STM32 parts offer two: the LSE (Low-Speed External), a 32.768 kHz quartz crystal, and the LSI (Low-Speed Internal), an on-chip RC oscillator. Choosing LSI to save a 10-cent crystal is a classic field failure.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. The 32 kHz clock feeds two prescalers: an asynchronous one (PREDIV_A, 7 bits) and a synchronous one (PREDIV_S, 15 bits).
  2. The 1 Hz calendar clock is ck_spre = f_RTCCLK / ((PREDIV_A + 1) × (PREDIV_S + 1)).
  3. For the LSE: 32 768 / (128 × 256) = 1.000 Hz exactly (PREDIV_A = 127, PREDIV_S = 255).
  4. For the LSI (32 kHz nominal on the L4): 32 000 / (128 × 250) = 1.000 Hz (PREDIV_A = 127, PREDIV_S = 249), if the LSI really runs at 32.000 kHz.
  5. Every second the calendar advances by one. Any error in f_RTCCLK becomes the same relative error in the time of day.

3. Worked example: ppm and percent into seconds per day

1 day = 86 400 s
1 ppm (part per million) = 86 400 × 1e-6 = 0.0864 s/day = 86.4 ms/day

LSE ±20 ppm at 25 °C   → 20 × 86.4 ms = 1.73 s/day ≈ 52 s/month
LSE temperature drift  : Δf/f ≈ −0.034 ppm/°C² × (T − 25 °C)²
   at −20 °C: −0.034 × (−45)² = −0.034 × 2025 = −69 ppm → −6 s/day extra
LSI ±1 %               → 0.01 × 86 400 = 864 s/day = 14.4 min/day
LSI measured 31.2 kHz  → (32.0 − 31.2)/32.0 = 2.5 % slow
   ck_spre = 31 200 / 32 000 = 0.975 Hz → loses 0.025 × 86 400 = 2160 s = 36 min/day

Load capacitors for the crystal: C_L = (C1·C2)/(C1+C2) + C_stray. With C1 = C2 = C, that is C/2 + C_stray. For a crystal specified at C_L = 12.5 pF and ≈ 3 pF of board stray capacitance: C/2 = 9.5 pF → C ≈ 19 pF (use 18 pF). Wrong load caps shift the frequency by tens of ppm. Check ST's oscillator guide (AN2867) for the oscillator's gain margin with your crystal; the L4's LSE drive strength (RCC_BDCR.LSEDRV) trades start-up robustness against current.

4. Code: select LSE and calibrate

          | (1u << RCC_BDCR_RTCSEL_Pos);     // 3. RTCSEL = 01: RTC clocked by LSE

// 6. Optional: smooth calibration RTC_CALR (CALP/CALM): range ≈ −487 to +488 ppm,
//    resolution ≈ 0.95 ppm, measured against a reference via the 512 Hz / 1 Hz output

PWR_CR1_DBPThe RTC and RCC_BDCR are write-protected after reset.

5. The backup domain

  • RTC, LSE, RCC_BDCR, 32 backup registers (128 B) and tamper logic, powered from VDD or VBAT. Survives Standby, Shutdown and main-supply loss when VBAT has a coin cell or supercap.
  • BDRST (in RCC_BDCR) resets it; DBP unlocks writes. Init code that sets BDRST on every boot destroys the time it was supposed to keep.

6. What goes wrong if…

  • …the RTC runs on LSI: timestamps drift by minutes per day and worse in the cold (Scenario 6). Wake-up schedules of a radio drift out of their network windows.
  • …the load caps are wrong: a steady 20–50 ppm error (seconds per day), or the crystal fails to start at cold.
  • …you wait for LSERDY without a timeout: a board with a cracked crystal hangs forever at boot.

7. Say it like this

"For timekeeping I use the 32.768 kHz LSE crystal: about ±20 ppm, which is under 2 seconds per day, plus a parabolic temperature drift. The LSI is an RC oscillator with a several-percent tolerance, and 1 % is already 14.4 minutes per day, so it's only good for the watchdog or rough timeouts. If the BOM forces LSI, I calibrate it against a reference and correct with RTC_CALR or in software, and I keep the RTC in the backup domain so it survives Standby."

Energy harvesting basics

ADVANCED▶

1. What is it?

Energy harvesting powers a device from its environment (light, heat, vibration, radio waves) instead of, or in addition to, a battery. The harvested power is tiny, intermittent and unpredictable, so the design has three parts: a transducer (solar cell, thermoelectric generator…), a harvester PMIC (Power Management IC) that extracts and stores the energy, and energy-aware firmware that adapts to what is available.

SourceTypical harvested powerNotes
Outdoor PV≈ 10–20 mW/cm² (full sun, 15–20 % cell)Zero at night; size the storage for the longest dark spell
Indoor PV≈ 10–100 µW/cm² (200–1000 lux, amorphous cell)Office lighting gives ≈ 100× less than sun
Thermoelectric (TEG)≈ 10s of µW/cm² per few K ΔTNeeds a boost converter that starts from ~20–50 mV (LTC3108 class)

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Cold start: with the storage empty, the PMIC must start from the transducer's tiny voltage (some hundreds of mV for PV, tens of mV for a TEG) using a special start-up circuit.
  2. MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking): a solar cell delivers maximum power at one particular voltage. Many PMICs use the "fractional open-circuit voltage" method: periodically disconnect, measure VOC, then regulate the cell at ≈ 75–80 % of VOC. Examples: TI BQ25570, e-peas AEM10941, ADI ADP5091.
  3. Storage: energy is put into a supercapacitor, a lithium-ion capacitor (LIC), a thin-film or a small Li battery. It covers peaks (a radio burst) and outages (night, weekends).
  4. Output: a buck or LDO stage supplies the MCU, often with a "power good" signal once the storage is charged enough.
  5. Firmware reads the storage voltage and chooses how often to measure and transmit.
average load ≤ average harvest × efficiency (over the worst-case period)

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you size for average light only: the device dies every weekend when the office lights are off.
  • …the supercap leaks more than the MCU draws: the storage never charges in dim light. Symptom: works on the bench under a desk lamp, never starts in the field.
  • …brown-out is not handled: with harvesting, losing power is normal operation. Use the PVD/BOR so the system dies and restarts cleanly, and make flash writes atomic (journal plus CRC) so a power loss mid-write does not corrupt data.

6. Say it like this

"I start from the energy budget: harvested power at worst-case conditions times efficiency must cover the average load, and storage must cover the longest outage, such as a weekend with the lights off. The chain is transducer, a PMIC with cold start and MPPT, storage with leakage well below the load, then a regulator. The firmware reads the storage voltage and scales the duty cycle, and treats brown-out as normal with clean resets and atomic flash writes."

Advanced Stop-mode tricks: SLEEPONEXIT, batch acquisition, LPBAM, SRAM retention

ADVANCED▶

1. What is it?

Once the basics work (deep sleep, analog pins, clocks restored), the remaining gains come from making the CPU wake less often and do less when it does. This card collects the techniques that interviewers at silicon vendors and wearables companies like to probe: SLEEPONEXIT, batch acquisition with DMA, LPBAM on the STM32U5, flash power-down, partial SRAM retention, and debounced wake-ups.

2. SLEEPONEXIT: firmware that lives in interrupts

  1. main() configures everything, sets SCB->SCR.SLEEPONEXIT = 1, and executes WFI once.
  2. An interrupt wakes the core; its ISR runs.
  3. When that ISR (the last active one) returns, the core goes straight back to sleep without returning to main(): no unstacking and no re-stacking of the 8-word exception frame, about 2 × 12 cycles saved per interrupt.
int main(void) {
    init_all();
    SCB->SCR |= SCB_SCR_SLEEPONEXIT_Msk;   // after each ISR: sleep again
    __DSB();
    __WFI();                               // executed once; never "returns" in normal operation
    for (;;) { }                           // only reached if an ISR clears SLEEPONEXIT
}
void RTC_WKUP_IRQHandler(void) {
    RTC->ISR &= ~RTC_ISR_WUTF;  EXTI->PR1 = EXTI_PR1_PIF20;
    sample_and_send();                     // all work happens here
}

3. Batch acquisition and LPBAM: let the hardware collect data

  • Batch acquisition (L4): stay in Sleep with only DMA and one peripheral clocked (trimmed SMENR bits). A UART or I2C stream lands in RAM without waking the CPU; the core wakes only on DMA half/full transfer. Example: 1 byte every 1 ms with an interrupt per byte means 1000 wake-ups/s; with a 256-byte DMA buffer and half-transfer interrupts, 1000/128 ≈ 8 wake-ups/s.
  • LPBAM (Low-Power Background Autonomous Mode, STM32U5): the low-power DMA (LPDMA) runs linked-list chains in Stop 2 together with autonomous peripherals (LPUART1, SPI3, I2C3, ADC4, LPTIM, DAC). Example: sample ADC4 on an LPTIM trigger every 10 ms into a buffer, and wake the CPU only when 100 samples are ready or a threshold is crossed. The CPU wakes once per second instead of 100 times.

4. Flash power-down, SRAM retention, debouncing

  • Flash power-down in Sleep (FLASH_ACR.SLEEP_PD, and RUN_PD in LP Run) plus a short ISR executing from SRAM saves the flash read current, at the cost of a flash wake-up delay on return.
  • SRAM retention selection (U5): power down SRAM pages you don't need in Stop. 8.95 µA (full SRAM) vs 4.0 µA (16 KB retained) in Stop 2: 4.95 µA saved = 4.95 × 8.76 = 43 mAh per year, a fifth of a CR2032. Place the retained state in the retained page with the linker script (a dedicated section).
  • Wake-up debouncing: a bouncing button produces several edges within ≈ 5–20 ms, each one a wake-up. Take the first edge, disable that EXTI line, start an LPTIM one-shot of 20 ms (655 counts at 32 768 Hz), sleep, then sample the pin and re-enable the line. No busy-wait, and Stop 2 continues during the debounce window.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • …you use SLEEPONEXIT and also expect main() to run a loop: the main loop never runs again. Work must be in ISRs, or an ISR must clear SLEEPONEXIT to hand work back.
  • …you keep a per-byte UART interrupt at 1 kHz: every byte costs a full wake-up and stacking; the CPU spends more time entering and leaving ISRs than sleeping.
  • …you power down SRAM pages but the stack or a retained variable lives there: after wake-up the data is garbage and the device faults. Check the map file.
  • …you debounce with HAL_Delay(20) inside the EXTI ISR: the CPU runs 20 ms at full current per press, and SysTick must be running.

6. Say it like this

"After the basics, I reduce the number of wake-ups: SLEEPONEXIT for purely interrupt-driven designs, DMA batching so peripherals fill a buffer while the CPU sleeps, and on the U5 LPBAM, where LPDMA and autonomous peripherals sample in Stop 2 and only wake the CPU on a full buffer or a threshold. I retain only the SRAM I need, and I debounce wake-up buttons with an LPTIM one-shot rather than a delay."
