// 42 — EMBEDDED SYSTEM DESIGN ROUND

The 18-Step Design Framework

The embedded system design round (Apple, Google, Meta Reality Labs, Amazon Devices, Tesla — about 2 years of experience) gives you a one-line prompt such as "Design the firmware for a fitness band" and 45 minutes. There is no single right answer. The interviewer is watching how you think: do you ask before you build, do you put numbers on things, do you know why you chose each part, and do you see what can fail.

Every step below is taught the same way: what it is, the words you need, how to do it step by step, a worked example with real numbers (one running example — a BLE fitness band — is carried through all 18 steps so you see the numbers connect), what goes wrong if you skip it, what the interviewer scores, and a "Say it like this" line you can use out loud. Open each card; the progress bar counts the ones you have read.

THE 45-MINUTE CLOCK

The bar shows where your minutes should go. Two habits matter most: (1) spend the first 5 minutes asking questions and writing the answers on the board — the numbers you collect there drive every later decision; (2) reach a complete but rough end-to-end design by minute 25, then let the interviewer pick which part to go deep on. A candidate who spends 30 minutes perfecting the sensor driver and never mentions power, update or failure handling usually fails, even if the driver is perfect.

THE RUNNING EXAMPLE USED IN EVERY STEP

Prompt: "Design the embedded system for a wrist-worn fitness band that counts steps and measures heart rate, and syncs with a phone." As each step adds a piece, the numbers are reused: accelerometer 50 Hz, optical heart-rate sensor 25 Hz, 100 mAh battery, 7-day battery-life target, BLE to the phone, $10 electronics bill of materials. If you can explain this one design end to end, you can transfer the same moves to any prompt.

// 42.2 — TEN FULLY WORKED DESIGNS

Worked Designs

Ten classic prompts, each solved the way a strong candidate would solve it on the whiteboard: the requirements they asked for, the assumptions they stated, the estimates with the arithmetic shown, the block diagram, the task table with priorities and periods, the data flow, the power budget (computed live from the table), the failure modes, the trade-offs, and the follow-up questions with answers. Every design starts with a "Terms used here" box, so no acronym is left undefined.

// 42.3 — WHITEBOARD PRACTICE

Design Simulator

Three tools. Design Builder: pick a prompt, make a choice at each step, and the tool computes power, battery life, flash/RAM, CPU load, link bandwidth and BOM cost from your choices, then scores each step and explains why. Budget Calculators: the three budgets every design needs (power, memory, timing with real rate-monotonic response-time analysis). 45-Minute Mock: a timer that walks you through the phases with the follow-ups an interviewer is likely to ask at each one.

// 42.4 — DESIGN REVIEWS

Spot the Flawed Choice

In a real design round the interviewer often pushes on a choice you made and waits to see whether you notice the problem. Each review below shows a design decision a candidate (or a junior colleague) made, with the evidence: a design note, numbers, code, or a trace. Pick the real problem. Your first pick is scored; then every option explains why it is right or wrong, followed by the root cause, the fix and the follow-up questions.

// 42.5 — FOLLOW-UP QUESTIONS

Interviewer Follow-Up Bank

The questions interviewers ask after your first design sketch, with full model answers: the reasoning, a worked example and the pitfall. Coding questions that come up in design rounds (ring buffer, state machine, watchdog supervisor, config store, A/B boot selection, fixed point) have complete C solutions.
