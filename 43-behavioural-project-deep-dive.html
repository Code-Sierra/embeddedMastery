// 43 — BEHAVIOURAL & PROJECT DEEP-DIVE

How behavioural and deep-dive rounds are graded

The technical rounds test whether you can do the work. The behavioural round and the project deep-dive test whether you did do real work, how you think when things go wrong, and whether a team would want you next to them at 2 a.m. during a field failure. This tab explains what the interviewer is writing down, the answer formats that make their job easy, and how to present internship work so it reads like two years of engineering. Open every card; each one is written to be read slowly once and then reviewed.

Honesty note before we start. Apple and most tier-1 companies do not publish their interview rubrics, and loops vary a lot between teams. What follows is the common pattern that hiring managers at Apple-style hardware companies and at automotive/semiconductor tier-1s (Bosch, Continental, NXP, TI, Infineon, Qualcomm) describe and train interviewers on. Treat it as a strong model, not a leaked rulebook.

1. WHAT IS IT, IN PLAIN WORDS?

A behavioural question asks you to describe something you actually did in the past ("Tell me about a time you…"). The idea behind it is simple: the best predictor of how you will behave on the new team is how you behaved on your last one. Hypothetical answers ("I would…") are weak evidence; a specific past event with specific actions is strong evidence.

A project deep-dive is a longer version of the same idea aimed at technical depth. The interviewer picks one project from your résumé (or asks you to choose) and spends 30 to 45 minutes on it. They start broad ("walk me through the system") and keep asking "why?" until they reach the edge of what you know. They are not trying to embarrass you. They want to find out two things: was this really your work, and how deep does your understanding go.

2. WHERE THEY APPEAR IN A LOOP

  1. Recruiter / hiring-manager screen (30 min). Usually "tell me about yourself", "why this team", and one project at a shallow level. The manager is checking whether your experience is relevant enough to bring you on-site.
  2. Technical phone screen (45–60 min). Mostly C and embedded fundamentals, but often opens with 5–10 minutes on a project. A sloppy project summary here colours the whole interview.
  3. On-site or virtual loop (4–6 interviews of 45–60 min). At Apple, the loop is normally run by the hiring team itself, so the people interviewing you are the engineers you would work with, often including a hardware engineer and a manager. Almost every interviewer reserves some time for behavioural questions, and one interviewer typically owns the deep-dive.
  4. Debrief. Each interviewer writes feedback with evidence ("candidate said X, which shows Y") and a hire / no-hire leaning. The panel compares notes. A single "they could not explain their own project" note is very hard to overcome.

3. WHY THIS MATTERS EVEN MORE FOR AN INTERN

When you have two years of experience, your title and company carry some signal. As an intern doing a Project Based Internship (PBI), you have almost no title signal, so your stories are your experience. The good news: you have unusually rich material. You debugged a real field failure on a 100 V buck converter, wrote a protocol specification that firmware was checked against, designed a CAN test plan, and are porting an application layer to a new platform. Many candidates with two years of experience cannot tell a story as concrete as your buck RCA. The work in this page is about telling it well.

4. WHAT GOES WRONG IF YOU DO NOT PREPARE

  • You ramble. Without a structure you spend three minutes on background and twenty seconds on what you did. The interviewer writes "unclear what the candidate personally contributed".
  • You say "we" for everything. The interviewer cannot separate your work from the team's and has to assume the smaller share.
  • You cannot give numbers. "The voltage spiked a lot" sounds like you were not in the lab. "We measured 150 V at the input capacitor on a 58 V pack, against a 100 V absolute maximum" sounds like an engineer.
  • You get caught overclaiming. At level 4 or 5 of a deep-dive your answer runs out, and it becomes obvious the decision was made by someone else. That is worse than having said so at the start.
Say it like this: "I understand the deep-dive is about how I actually worked, so I will be clear about which parts I owned, which parts my lead decided, and where I am still unsure. I am happy to go as deep as you like into the buck-converter failure, because that is the one I drove end to end."

1. WHAT IS A COMPETENCY?

A competency is a behaviour the company has decided predicts success in the role. Interviewers are usually assigned one or two to probe. They listen for evidence: a concrete thing you did that demonstrates the behaviour. Adjectives about yourself ("I am detail-oriented") count for nothing; an action that shows detail ("I noticed the schematic symbol said SS14 but the BOM description said a 100 V, 2 A part, and flagged it before the replication tests") counts for a lot.

3. HOW AN INTERVIEWER CONVERTS YOUR ANSWER INTO A SCORE

  1. They ask the question and start a mental clock.
  2. As you talk, they write down verbatim fragments: numbers you said, the verbs you used ("I measured", "we decided"), and the outcome.
  3. They ask follow-ups until they can answer three things: What exactly did this person do? How hard was it? Would they do it again here?
  4. After the interview they map the notes to the competencies and write a leaning: strong hire, hire, lean no, no hire.

So the practical rule is: make their note-taking easy. Say the number, say "I", say the result, say the lesson. Every item they can copy down is a point of evidence.

Say it like this: "The competency I think this story shows best is ownership: no one had been assigned the root cause after the field swaps, so I took it, planned the replication tests, and drove the redesign proposal."

1. WHAT ARE THEY?

They are fill-in-the-blanks structures that stop you from rambling and make sure every answer contains what the interviewer needs.

  • STAR = Situation (where and when, in one or two sentences), Task (what you had to achieve), Action (what you did, step by step), Result (what happened, ideally with a number).
  • CARL = Context (Situation + Task merged), Action, Result, Learning (what you would do differently or now do by default). CARL is better for failure and "what did you learn" questions, and for engineers in general, because interviewers at Apple-style companies almost always follow up with "what would you change?" anyway.

Use CARL as your default and add a one-line learning to STAR answers too.

2. HOW TO BUDGET THE TIME

A spoken answer should take about 2 minutes. People speak at roughly 130–150 words per minute, so a 2-minute answer is about 260–300 words. Split it like this:

C 15%ACTION 60%RESULT 15%L 10%

Worked numbers: 280 words × 15% ≈ 40 words of context (two sentences); 280 × 60% ≈ 170 words of action (four or five concrete steps); 280 × 15% ≈ 40 words of result; 280 × 10% ≈ 30 words of learning.

The most common mistake is inverting this: 60% context, 15% action. The interviewer is scoring the action.

3. WORKED EXAMPLE — THE BUCK FIELD FAILURE IN CARL

CONTEXT (≈40 words)
"Our VIM telematics board, which runs a Quectel EC200U modem from a 16S battery pack
(about 58 V), was failing in the field: at the moment of power-on the 100 V LM5164 buck
burnt at its VIN and GND pins and shorted. I took on the root-cause analysis."

ACTION (≈170 words)
"First I listed the possible stresses: input overvoltage from hot-plug, input short,
reverse polarity, and output short. I designed one replication test for each and ran
them: 100 hot-plugs, a short at the connector before connecting the battery, reverse
connection plus hot-plug, and an output short plus hot-plug.
None destroyed the part, so I switched from 'reproduce it' to 'measure the margin'.
On the scope the hot-plug produced spikes up to 150 V near the input capacitor, lasting
10 to 30 microseconds. The LM5164's absolute maximum VIN is 100 V and its recommended
maximum is also 100 V, so on a 58 V pack we had no headroom for transients. The TVS
fitted clamps at 121 V at 12.4 A, which is above the IC's absolute maximum, so it could
not protect it. And the input capacitors were three 2.2 µF X7T ceramics, which lose most
of their capacitance at 58 V DC bias, leaving a lightly damped LC with the harness and
the ferrite bead. I removed the ferrite and the spikes dropped, which supported that model."

RESULT (≈40 words)
"I proposed a new input protection chain: series Schottky, PTC, a TVS, a pi filter,
then the buck, now an LM5013. [Status: e.g. 'the redesign is in review / built and
tested on N boards with peak input of X V'.]"

LEARNING (≈30 words)
"I learned that a failure you cannot reproduce can still be root-caused if you measure
the margins, and that a protection part only protects if its clamp voltage sits below
the absolute maximum of what it protects."

4. WHAT GOES WRONG IF…

  • …you skip the Result: the interviewer assumes nothing happened. If the result is not final yet, say the current status honestly and what the next measurement is.
  • …you skip the Learning: they will ask for it anyway, and a learning invented on the spot sounds generic ("communication is important").
  • …you put your numbers only in the Result: numbers in the Action ("150 V against a 100 V abs-max") prove you were hands-on. Numbers only in the Result can sound like a slide you read.
Say it like this: start with one sentence of context, then say "What I did was…" and walk through your actions in order, then "The result was…" with a number or status, then "What I took away was…". That verbal signposting tells the interviewer exactly where to write.

1. WHAT IS THE QUESTION REALLY ASKING?

It is not asking for your life story. It is asking: "In 90 seconds, give me a reason to be excited about the next 44 minutes, and tell me which of your projects to ask about." You get to choose the topic of the rest of the interview. Point them at your strongest story.

2. THE STRUCTURE (PRESENT → PAST → WHY HERE → HOOK)

Present 15sPast: 2 highlights 45sWhy here 20sHook 10s
  1. Present (15 s, ~35 words). Who you are now, in engineering terms. Not "I am a student"; instead, what you build.
  2. Past highlights (45 s, ~110 words). Two projects, each with one number and one sentence of what you personally did. Choose one debugging story and one design/system story so you show both depth and breadth.
  3. Why here (20 s, ~45 words). One honest connection between your work and this team's product. Specific beats flattering.
  4. Hook (10 s, ~20 words). Offer the deep-dive: "I'd be happy to go deep on the buck failure."

Word count of the fixed text above: about 190 words, which is roughly 80–90 seconds at a calm pace. Time yourself out loud; if you go past 100 seconds, cut the second highlight to one sentence.

4. WHAT GOES WRONG IF…

  • …you recite your résumé in date order: the interviewer has the résumé already. You have wasted the one free choice you get.
  • …you list eight projects: none of them lands. Two with numbers beats eight without.
  • …you say "I'm passionate about embedded": everyone says it. Show it with one detail instead.
Say it like this: practise the script until you can deliver it without notes, then deliberately change a few words each time so it does not sound memorised. A memorised pitch sounds like a recording; a practised one sounds confident.

1. WHAT HAPPENS, MINUTE BY MINUTE

0–5 overview5–30 drill-down30–40 reflectQs
  1. 0–5 min: the overview. You explain the system. Draw a block diagram (power source, protection, regulator, MCU/modem, loads; or cloud, gateway, station, slots). An interviewer who can see the system asks better questions, and you control where they look.
  2. 5–30 min: the drill-down. They pick one block and go down, level by level, until you reach the edge of your knowledge. This is normal. Everyone hits the edge. The score depends on how deep the edge is and how you behave at it.
  3. 30–40 min: reflection. "What would you change? What did you learn? How would this scale to 10,000 units?"
  4. 40–45 min: your questions. Covered in card 10.

2. THE FIVE LEVELS OF "WHY"

L1
What and why. "What does this system do, and why was it designed this way?" You should answer this without hesitation; if you stumble here, the interviewer doubts the whole project.
L2
Alternatives. "What else did you consider? Why not that?" They test whether you made a decision or just inherited one. If you inherited it, say so and then explain what you would have weighed.
L3
Numbers. "What was the voltage / bitrate / latency / margin? How did you calculate it?" This is where hands-on engineers separate from people who read the report.
L4
What broke. "What went wrong? What edge case did you miss? How did you know it was fixed?" They test honesty and debugging method.
L5
What you would change. "Knowing what you know now, what would you redesign? How would it scale? What would you test first in production?" They test judgement and growth.

3. WORKED EXAMPLE OF A DESCENT (BUCK RCA)

L1  Q: What failed and why does the board have a buck at all?
    A: The pack is 16S, about 58 V. The modem and MCU need 3.3 V and 1.8 V. Dropping 58 V to
       a few volts with a linear regulator would burn most of the power as heat, so a
       switching buck converts it efficiently first, then low-noise LDOs make the final rails.

L2  Q: Why a 100 V part? Why not a 60 V one?
    A: A 60 V part has almost no margin over a 58 V pack. The 100 V class was the obvious
       step up. The real lesson is that 100 V is ALSO not enough once you add hot-plug
       transients and a possible 23S (~85 V) pack.

L3  Q: How big were the transients and how long?
    A: Up to 150 V near the input capacitor during hot-plug, 10–30 µs wide. The LM5164
       absolute maximum VIN is 100 V. The TVS clamps at 121 V at 12.4 A.

L4  Q: Your replication tests never killed a part. Isn't your root cause a guess?
    A: It is a margin-based root cause, not a reproduced one, and I say so. The evidence is
       that measured stress (150 V) exceeds the rating (100 V) by 50%, and the burn location
       (VIN/GND pins) matches overvoltage breakdown of the input. Destruction is statistical:
       it depends on harness length, pack impedance, connector bounce, and part-to-part
       variation. Next step: [field transient capture / more units / higher-voltage test].

L5  Q: What would you design differently from scratch?
    A: Choose the protection first: pick a TVS whose clamp at the expected surge current is
       below the regulator's abs-max, add a damped bulk capacitor so the LC is not high-Q,
       and pick a regulator with real headroom over the worst-case pack voltage.

4. WHAT GOES WRONG AT EACH LEVEL

  • L1: you start with history ("so in 2024 the company…") instead of the system. Fix: diagram first.
  • L2: "we just used what was there". Fix: even if you inherited it, explain the trade-off you would weigh.
  • L3: no numbers, or numbers with no units. Fix: memorise the "numbers to know" table for each project in the My Projects tab.
  • L4: claiming nothing went wrong. Fix: every real project has a failure; pick it in advance.
  • L5: "I wouldn't change anything". Fix: always have two concrete changes ready.
Say it like this: when they push past your edge, name it: "That is the boundary of what I measured. Here is how I would reason about it, and here is the measurement I would take to confirm." Then reason out loud.

1. WHAT "SENIOR-ISH" ACTUALLY MEANS TO AN INTERVIEWER

Interviewers do not judge seniority by your title. They judge it by the size of the problem you can hold in your head and how you talk about decisions. A junior engineer describes tasks ("I wrote a script"). An engineer with two years of experience describes problems, trade-offs and outcomes ("certificates had to be per-device and could not be rebuilt into every firmware image by hand, so I wrote a tool that swaps them inside the .pac package; the open question is whether the certificate fetch belongs in the technician's GUI or on a server").

3. OWNERSHIP LANGUAGE THAT IS HONEST AND STILL STRONG

  • "I owned…" for things you drove start to finish (buck RCA, payload spec, relay test plan, cert-replacement script).
  • "I proposed… and it was reviewed by…" for designs where someone senior approved (input protection redesign, DC backup).
  • "I contributed…" for team efforts (Simulink application layer, tablet port), followed by the specific piece.
  • "That decision was made before I joined; here is how I would evaluate it…" for inherited choices. This sentence turns a weakness into a judgement test you can pass.

4. WHAT GOES WRONG IF YOU INFLATE

Suppose you say "I designed the station firmware architecture". The interviewer asks why the application layer runs on the mother board rather than the relay board, what the step rate is, how the FOTA state machine recovers from a power cut. If those were not your decisions, you will give vague answers at level 2 or 3, and the interviewer now doubts every claim you made, including the true ones. Precise, modest claims that hold up at level 5 are scored far higher than big claims that collapse at level 2.

Say it like this: "I want to be precise about scope: I owned the root cause analysis and the replication tests; the redesign was a proposal I made that the hardware team reviewed. Happy to go into either."

1. WHY THIS IS SCORED

Every deep-dive is designed to reach a question you cannot answer. What the interviewer is watching at that moment is intellectual honesty (do you bluff?) and reasoning (can you get somewhere useful from first principles?). In embedded work, a bluffing engineer is dangerous: they will tell the team the transient is "probably fine" instead of measuring it.

2. THE FOUR-STEP RESPONSE TO A QUESTION YOU CANNOT ANSWER

  1. Say it plainly. "I don't know that number off the top of my head." (Not "um, I think maybe it's around…")
  2. Say what you do know that is related. "I know the LM5164's absolute maximum VIN is 100 V and that our TVS clamps at 121 V."
  3. Reason out loud to an estimate. "For an undamped LC hit by a voltage step, the capacitor voltage overshoots to about twice the step, so a 58 V pack could ring towards 116 V; real damping makes it less, and the 150 V we saw means something else, like connector bounce or the inductance of a long harness, added energy."
  4. Say how you would find out. "I would check the capacitor vendor's DC-bias curve and measure the harness inductance with an LCR meter."

3. WHEN YOU REALISE YOU JUST SAID SOMETHING WRONG

Correct yourself immediately and explicitly: "Actually, let me correct that: I said the TVS would protect the IC, but its 121 V clamp is above the 100 V absolute maximum, so it would not." Interviewers consistently rate a self-correction higher than an answer that was right first time, because it shows you are checking your own reasoning in real time, which is exactly what they want during a design review.

4. WHAT GOES WRONG IF…

  • …you bluff: the interviewer usually knows the answer. You lose the point for the question and the credibility for every other answer.
  • …you freeze: silence over 10 seconds feels long. Say "Let me think about that for a moment" and think out loud.
  • …you over-apologise: one "I don't know" is fine. Five "sorry"s reads as low confidence. State it, reason, move on.
Say it like this: "I haven't measured that, so I don't want to give you a made-up number. Here's how I'd estimate it… and here's the measurement that would settle it."

1. WHAT IT IS

Apple is famous for product secrecy, and most tier-1 suppliers sign strict non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) with their customers. An NDA is a contract that forbids sharing specified information. Interviewers watch how you talk about your current employer's and customer's information, because how you treat Vecmocon's and Battery Smart's information is how you will treat theirs.

3. HOW TO SAY IT WITHOUT SOUNDING EVASIVE

Name the boundary once, then give as much technical depth as you can around it. "I'll keep the customer's API details out of it, but structurally the provisioning tool asks the customer's API for the certificate for a given device ID, then writes it into the firmware package." That shows you know where the line is and that you understand the system.

4. WHAT GOES WRONG IF…

…you show a real failure-rate spreadsheet on a screen-share, or read out a device ID to sound concrete. An Apple interviewer will note it as a judgement concern even if every technical answer was perfect.

Say it like this: "Some of the field numbers are the customer's, so I'll describe them in relative terms: the failures happened at power-on, and replacement units from the same batch worked."

1. WHY EMBEDDED INTERVIEWERS CARE SO MUCH

Embedded software sits on top of hardware that someone else designed. Most hard embedded bugs live at the boundary: a firmware engineer sees a reset, a hardware engineer sees a brown-out, and the bug is found only when they look at the same scope capture together. Apple in particular organises around tight hardware/software integration, so interviewers listen for whether you treat hardware engineers as partners or as the people to blame.

2. "INFLUENCE WITHOUT AUTHORITY" DEFINED

It means changing a decision owned by people who do not report to you, and to whom you do not report, using evidence, framing and timing instead of rank. As an intern, almost everything you changed was by influence, which is a strength in these stories.

3. THE STEP-BY-STEP PATTERN THAT WORKS (and which your stories already follow)

  1. Understand their constraints first. The hardware designer cares about cost, board area, and qualification time. A proposal that ignores those is rejected.
  2. Bring data, not opinions. "The TVS clamps at 121 V and the IC's abs-max is 100 V" cannot be argued with. "I think the protection is weak" can.
  3. Make it easy to say yes. Offer a concrete alternative (the new protection chain) rather than just a problem.
  4. Give credit and share ownership. "We went through the chain together" invites the hardware engineer to own the fix.
  5. Follow up. Track it until it is decided, then report the outcome.

4. YOUR EVIDENCE

  • Buck RCA: a firmware-side intern showing the hardware protection chain could not protect the part, and proposing the redesign.
  • DC backup: proposing to remove the AC UPS and add a 12 V 35 Ah lithium battery after the 12 V 30 A SMPS. Prepare the trade-off argument (see the My Projects tab) because this is pure influence.
  • Payload spec verification: telling the owners of a document that it disagreed with the firmware, which needs tact: you are telling someone their document is wrong.
Say it like this: "I didn't own the hardware design, so I didn't argue opinions. I brought the scope captures and the datasheet limits to the designer, showed the TVS clamp was above the regulator's abs-max, and proposed a chain we could both review."

1. WHY YOUR QUESTIONS ARE PART OF THE SCORE

The last five minutes are still interview time. A thoughtful question shows you are evaluating fit like an engineer, not just hoping for an offer. "No questions" reads as low interest. A question you could have answered from the careers page reads as low effort.

2. QUESTIONS GROUPED BY WHAT THEY REVEAL

QuestionWhy it is a good question
"What does the boundary between firmware and hardware look like on this team? When there's a power or reset issue, who drives the root cause?"Shows you know that is where hard bugs live, and tells you whether you would be in the lab or only in the IDE.
"What does a new engineer typically own in their first six months?"Shows you are thinking about ownership; reveals how much trust juniors get.
"How do you validate firmware before it reaches the field, and what does the team do when a field issue comes back?"Connects to your validation and field-debugging experience; reveals engineering maturity.

3. WHAT TO AVOID

  • Salary, leave and perks in a technical round (save them for the recruiter).
  • Asking what unreleased products the team builds. At Apple this is a confidentiality red flag; they will not say, and asking shows you do not understand the culture.
  • "How did I do?" It puts the interviewer in an awkward position and they are usually not allowed to say.
Say it like this: "Most of my hardest bugs have been at the hardware boundary, like a buck failing at power-on. How does this team split ownership when a bug could be either power or firmware?"

    One page to review the night before

    Competency coverage across your projects, which story to use for which question, the verbs that make ownership audible, the red flags that sink candidates, and a day-before checklist.

    60-SECOND REVISION
    1. Answer format: CARL. Context in two sentences, Action is 60% of the time, Result with a number or honest status, Learning in one sentence. About two minutes, 260–300 words.
    2. Say "I". "We" only for genuinely shared work, and then say which part was yours.
    3. Numbers in the Action, not just the Result: 58 V pack, 100 V abs-max, 150 V spike, 121 V clamp, 10–30 µs, 15 test items, 0x66666, 0xFF broadcast, 12 V 35 Ah, 12 V 30 A.
    4. Deep-dive ladder: What/why → alternatives → numbers → what broke → what you would change. Prepare two answers for level 5 for every project.
    5. At the edge of your knowledge: say so, say what you do know, reason to an estimate, say how you would measure it.
    6. Wrong mid-answer? Correct yourself out loud immediately; it scores higher than never being wrong.
    7. Confidentiality: architecture and physics yes; device IDs, endpoints, customer numbers no. Name the boundary once, then go deep around it.
    8. Flagship: buck RCA. Second: payload spec verification. Third: relay-board CAN test plan.

    RED FLAGS THAT SINK CANDIDATES
    • Blaming another team ("hardware messed up"). Say what the system lacked, not who failed.
    • "We" for everything, so your contribution is invisible.
    • No failure story, or a fake one ("I work too hard").
    • Bluffing a number or a mechanism at level 3 or 4.
    • Overclaiming scope ("I designed the station firmware") that collapses on the second follow-up.
    • Leaking customer data, device IDs, endpoints, failure rates.
    • Rambling context: 90 seconds before you say what you did.
    • No questions for the interviewer, or only about perks.
    • Badmouthing your internship, your lead, or the customer.
    • Generic "why Apple" ("I love the iPhone") with no connection to your work.
