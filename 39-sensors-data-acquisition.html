01

The transfer function: range, sensitivity, resolution, offset, gain

BEGINNER▶

A sensor turns something physical (tilt, temperature, current) into a number. The transfer function is the rule for that conversion. Almost every line of a sensor datasheet describes one way the real rule differs from the ideal straight line.

1. WHAT IS IT?

Ideally the output is a straight line: output = sensitivity × input. Real parts add a few errors on top. Here is the full model; each symbol is defined below it.

y = S·(1 + εg)·x + y0 + NL(x) + n(t) + drift(T, t)
x — input
The physical quantity, e.g. acceleration in g (1 g = 9.80665 m/s², the acceleration of gravity).
y — output
What you read: a voltage for an analog sensor, or a signed integer ("counts" or "LSB") for a digital sensor.
S — sensitivity
How much the output changes per unit of input. Digital sensors usually quote the reciprocal, "mg/LSB" = how many milli-g one count is worth.
LSB — least significant bit
One count, the smallest step the digital output can take.
εg — gain error
The slope is a little wrong, e.g. +1 % means every reading is 1 % too big.
y0 — offset (also "bias", "zero-g level", "zero-rate level")
What the sensor outputs when the input is exactly zero. Ideally 0.
NL(x) — nonlinearity
The curve bends away from a straight line.
n(t) — noise
Random jitter that changes from sample to sample.
drift(T, t)
Offset and gain slowly change with temperature T and with time t.
FS — full scale (range)
The input span the sensor can represent, e.g. ±2 g. Inputs beyond it clip.

2. HOW A DIGITAL ACCELEROMETER MAKES ONE NUMBER

  1. The MEMS structure moves under acceleration; its capacitance changes.
  2. The sensor's internal ADC (analog-to-digital converter) turns that into a signed 16-bit integer, range −32768 … +32767.
  3. The full-scale setting you wrote into a control register decides how many g those 65 536 codes cover: ±2 g means 4 g / 65 536 codes = 0.061 mg per code.
  4. The value is written into two 8-bit output registers (low byte, high byte).
  5. Your driver reads both bytes, joins them into an int16_t, and multiplies by the sensitivity to get mg.

3. WORKED EXAMPLE (ST LSM6DSO, ±2 g)

  1. Datasheet sensitivity at ±2 g: 0.061 mg/LSB (check: 4000 mg / 65 536 = 0.0610).
  2. You read the bytes OUTZ_L_A = 0x5A, OUTZ_H_A = 0x40 → raw = 0x405A = 16 474 counts.
  3. 16 474 × 0.061 mg = 1004.9 mg. The board is lying flat, so the truth is 1000 mg.
  4. The 4.9 mg difference is offset plus gain error. The datasheet says offset is typically ±20 mg and sensitivity tolerance ±1 % (= ±10 mg at 1 g), so 4.9 mg is normal. Calibration (card 19) removes it.
  5. Range trade-off: at ±16 g the same code would be worth 0.488 mg, so 1 g is only 2049 counts. You lose fine resolution, but a 10 g impact no longer clips.

4. RESOLUTION IS NOT BITS

Resolution is the smallest change you can actually detect. It is the larger of (a) one LSB and (b) the noise. The LSM6DSO at ±2 g has an LSB of 0.061 mg but about 0.6 mg rms noise (card 02). So a single sample cannot resolve 0.061 mg; its useful resolution is about 0.6 mg, roughly 10 LSB. Averaging lowers the noise (card 20), which is why you sometimes see "resolution" improve with filtering.

02

Accuracy vs precision, and noise density → rms noise

BEGINNER▶

"Accurate" and "precise" are different things. Accuracy is about being right on average; precision is about giving the same answer every time. They have different causes and different fixes, and interviewers ask you to separate them.

1. DEFINITIONS

Accuracy (trueness)
How close the average reading is to the true value. Damaged by offset, gain error, nonlinearity and drift. Fixed by calibration.
Precision (repeatability)
How close repeated readings are to each other. Damaged by noise. Improved by averaging/filtering.
rms (root mean square) noise, σ
The standard deviation of the noise: the typical size of the random jitter. About 99.7 % of samples fall within ±3σ, so peak-to-peak noise is roughly 6σ.
Noise density (ND)
Noise per square root of bandwidth, units like µg/√Hz. It says "for every hertz of bandwidth you let through, you get this much noise". You need it because the rms noise depends on your filter setting.
Bandwidth (BW), cut-off fc
The frequency above which a filter starts to suppress the signal (the −3 dB point, where amplitude has dropped to 70.7 %).
ENBW — equivalent noise bandwidth
The width of an ideal brick-wall filter that would let through the same noise power as your real filter. A real filter's gentle roll-off lets extra noise through, so ENBW > fc.

2. FROM NOISE DENSITY TO THE NOISE YOU WILL SEE

  1. Read the noise density from the datasheet, e.g. 70 µg/√Hz (LSM6DSO-class accelerometer, high-performance mode).
  2. Find the bandwidth of the filter that sits in front of your reading: the sensor's internal digital low-pass filter, plus any filter in your firmware.
  3. Convert to ENBW. For a single-pole (first-order) low-pass, ENBW = (π/2)·fc ≈ 1.57·fc. For a steep filter it approaches fc.
  4. σ = ND × √ENBW.
  5. Convert to the unit you care about (degrees of tilt, °C, mA).
  1. ND = 70 µg/√Hz, single-pole filter at fc = 50 Hz.
  2. ENBW = 1.57 × 50 = 78.5 Hz; √78.5 = 8.86.
  3. σ = 70 × 8.86 = 620 µg = 0.62 mg rms; peak-to-peak ≈ 6σ ≈ 3.7 mg.
  4. Tilt near level: 1° of tilt changes the horizontal axis by sin(1°) × 1000 mg = 17.45 mg. So 0.62 mg rms ≈ 0.036° rms of tilt noise.
  5. Gyro, same idea: 3.8 mdps/√Hz × 8.86 = 34 mdps rms (mdps = milli-degrees per second).
  6. To halve the noise you must cut ENBW by 4 (noise scales with √bandwidth). Going from 50 Hz to 12.5 Hz bandwidth gives 0.31 mg, but the sensor now reacts about 4× more slowly.

3. CODE: MEASURE NOISE ON YOUR BOARD

Datasheet noise is measured on an ideal board. Measure your own with the device lying still:

The naive "sum of x² minus square of sum" formula loses all precision in float when the mean (1000 mg) is huge compared with σ (0.6 mg). Welford's method avoids that.

  • You average to fix an offset: averaging 1000 samples of a sensor with a 20 mg offset gives a very precise 20 mg error. Averaging improves precision, never accuracy.
  • You calibrate with a single noisy sample: the noise at that instant (±1.9 mg at 3σ) becomes a permanent offset in every future reading. Always average many samples at calibration points.
  • You compare noise at different filter settings without converting through ENBW: "the new part is twice as noisy" is often just a wider bandwidth setting.
Accuracy is how close the average is to the truth and is limited by offset, gain and drift, so I fix it with calibration. Precision is how repeatable readings are and is limited by noise, so I improve it by filtering, at the cost of bandwidth and latency. To predict noise I take the noise density and multiply by the square root of the equivalent noise bandwidth, which is about 1.57 times the cut-off for a single-pole filter. For example 70 µg/√Hz at 50 Hz gives about 0.6 mg rms.
03

ODR, sensor bandwidth and aliasing

BEGINNER▶

A digital sensor samples the world at a fixed rate and filters it first. The two settings, sampling rate and filter bandwidth, are related but not the same, and mixing them up causes aliasing and timing bugs.

1. DEFINITIONS

ODR — output data rate
How many new samples per second the sensor writes into its output registers. LSM6DSO choices: 12.5, 26, 52, 104, 208, 416, 833, 1666, 3332, 6664 Hz.
Sample period Ts
1/ODR. At 104 Hz, Ts = 9.615 ms.
Nyquist frequency
ODR/2. Any signal content above it cannot be represented correctly.
Aliasing
When content above Nyquist "folds" down and appears as a fake lower frequency. A 100 Hz vibration sampled at 104 Hz looks like a 4 Hz wobble (|100 − 104| = 4 Hz).
Anti-aliasing filter (digital LPF inside the sensor)
A low-pass filter that removes content above Nyquist before the data is decimated to the ODR. On ST parts this is "LPF1/LPF2"; the bandwidth is typically ODR/2 to ODR/20, selectable.

2. WHAT THE SENSOR DOES INTERNALLY

  1. The MEMS element is converted at a high internal rate (kHz).
  2. A digital low-pass filter removes high-frequency content (and reduces noise, card 02).
  3. The result is decimated (every Nth sample kept) to the ODR you chose.
  4. The new sample is latched into the output registers, a data-ready flag is set, and an interrupt pin can pulse.
  5. The timing comes from the sensor's own internal oscillator, which is typically only ±1–2 % accurate. It is not synchronised to your MCU clock.
  1. You configure 104 Hz. The oscillator is 1.4 % slow, so the real ODR is 104 × 0.986 = 102.5 Hz, i.e. Ts = 9.75 ms instead of 9.615 ms.
  2. If firmware integrates the gyro assuming 9.615 ms, every angle is 1.4 % too small. After rotating 360°, you report 355°: a 5° error from timing alone.
  3. A washing machine vibrates the device at 98 Hz. If the sensor filter bandwidth is wider than 52 Hz (Nyquist), that 98 Hz aliases to |98 − 102.5| = 4.5 Hz, which looks like slow genuine motion to a step counter or tilt filter.
  4. Fix: choose a filter bandwidth around ODR/4 (≈ 26 Hz) so 98 Hz is strongly attenuated, and measure the real ODR (count samples over a known MCU time) for integration.
✖ WRONG
✔ RIGHT
  • Polling faster than ODR: you read the same sample twice. A derivative computed from it shows zero, then a double-sized step. Symptom: jagged, "staircase" derived signals.
  • Polling slower than ODR without a FIFO: samples are overwritten before you read them. Symptom: step counts too low; integrated angles wrong.
  • Bandwidth wider than Nyquist: vibration aliases into the band of interest. Symptom: phantom low-frequency motion near motors, fans, speakers.
ODR is how often the sensor produces a sample; bandwidth is the cut-off of its internal low-pass filter, which must be below ODR/2 to prevent aliasing. The ODR comes from the sensor's own oscillator, typically ±1–2 %, so I never integrate with a hard-coded dt. I either timestamp each data-ready edge or measure the true rate. I pick the ODR from the signal bandwidth I need, roughly 5–10× the highest frequency of interest, and set the filter around ODR/4.
04

Nonlinearity, hysteresis, temperature drift, cross-axis, aging

BEGINNER▶

Offset and gain are the easy errors: a straight line in the wrong place. The errors in this card are harder because some bend the line, some depend on history, and some change with temperature or time. Knowing which calibration removes which error is a classic interview question.

1. DEFINITIONS

Nonlinearity (%FS)
The largest gap between the real curve and a reference straight line, as a percentage of full scale. Read which line: "BSL" (best-fit straight line) gives numbers about half as big as "end-point" (the line through the two ends).
Hysteresis
The output for the same input depends on whether you approached it from below or above (pressure rising vs falling, magnetic materials, mechanical stress relaxing).
TCO — temperature coefficient of offset
How much the offset moves per °C, e.g. ±0.1 mg/°C (accelerometer), ±0.01 dps/°C (gyro).
TCS — temperature coefficient of sensitivity
How much the gain changes per °C, in %/°C or ppm/°C (ppm = parts per million; 100 ppm = 0.01 %).
Cross-axis sensitivity
An input on X leaks into the Y output, typically ±1 %, because the sensing axes on the die are not perfectly perpendicular.
Aging / long-term drift
Slow offset change over months or years.
Solder-stress shift
Offset change caused by the stress of reflow soldering and PCB bending. It can be tens of mg for MEMS parts, larger than the datasheet number.

3. WORKED EXAMPLES

  1. Gyro TCO: 0.01 dps/°C. A watch warms from 15 °C (outdoors) to 35 °C (on the wrist): ΔT = 20 °C → bias changes by 0.2 dps. Integrated over one minute: 0.2 × 60 = 12° of heading error. A one-time factory bias calibration does not fix this; you need temperature compensation or continuous bias tracking.
  2. Accelerometer TCO: 0.1 mg/°C over a 60 °C range = 6 mg = 6/17.45 ≈ 0.34° of tilt. Irrelevant for screen rotation, important for a precision inclinometer.
  3. Pressure sensor nonlinearity: 0.25 %FS end-point on a 0–100 kPa part = 0.25 kPa maximum error at mid-scale even after a perfect 2-point calibration at 0 and 100 kPa.
  4. Cross-axis 1 %: a 1 g input on Z appears as 10 mg on X: 0.57° of fake tilt.

  • You calibrate the bare sensor, then solder it: reflow shifts the offset by tens of mg, so your calibration is wrong on every unit. Calibrate after final assembly.
  • You calibrate at 25 °C only and the product works at −10 to 45 °C: gyro bias wanders by several tenths of a dps. Heading drifts more in winter.
  • You expect calibration to remove hysteresis: it cannot, because one input maps to two outputs. Approach calibration points from one direction and budget the hysteresis as error.
A two-point calibration removes offset and gain only. Nonlinearity needs multi-point or polynomial correction, temperature drift needs calibration at several temperatures or live compensation using the on-die temperature sensor, and cross-axis error needs a 3×3 matrix. Hysteresis and noise can't be calibrated out at all. For MEMS parts I calibrate after reflow, because solder stress moves the offset.
05

Reading a sensor datasheet like an engineer

BEGINNER▶

A datasheet is a contract: it tells you what the manufacturer guarantees and under which conditions. Reading it well means knowing which numbers are guaranteed, which are only typical, and which hidden register behaviours will bite you.

1. THE VOCABULARY

Min / Max
Guaranteed limits, usually tested in production or guaranteed by design. Design worst-case on these.
Typ. (typical)
The average of characterisation samples. Not a guarantee; half the parts can be worse.
Test conditions
The supply voltage, temperature, mode and filter setting the number applies to. A noise figure in "high-performance mode" does not apply to low-power mode.
Register map
The list of internal 8-bit registers you read and write over I²C/SPI, with the meaning of every bit.
Application note (AN)
A separate document explaining how to use the features (FIFO, interrupts, self-test). Often more important than the datasheet for firmware.

2. A READING ORDER THAT WORKS

  1. Absolute maximum ratings: supply, I/O voltage, shock (MEMS: ~10 000 g). Things that destroy the part.
  2. Mechanical/electrical characteristics table: FS, sensitivity, offset, noise, drift. Note min/max vs typ and the conditions column.
  3. Timing: boot/turn-on time, ODR accuracy, I²C/SPI timing, maximum SPI clock (LSM6DSO: 10 MHz).
  4. Power modes and current: what you pay for each ODR and mode.
  5. Register map: reset values, which bits are reserved ("must be 0"), auto-increment, BDU, interrupt latching.
  6. Application note: FIFO modes, self-test limits, interrupt configuration sequences.
  7. Errata: known silicon bugs.

3. WORKED EXAMPLE: A TYPICAL IMU TABLE

Then I do a quick error budget, for example bias drift versus angle random walk over the mission time.
06

Voltage dividers into an ADC (battery, rails, ratiometric sensors)

BEGINNER▶

An ADC can only measure voltages between 0 V and its reference (e.g. 3.3 V). To measure a 4.2 V battery or a 12 V rail you scale it down with two resistors. It sounds trivial, but the divider's impedance, current draw and tolerance all affect the reading.

1. DEFINITIONS

Divider ratio
Vout/Vin = R2/(R1 + R2), where R1 is the top resistor (to Vin) and R2 the bottom one (to ground).
Thevenin resistance Rth
The resistance the ADC pin "sees" looking back into the divider: R1 ∥ R2 = R1·R2/(R1 + R2). It limits how fast the ADC's capacitor can charge.
SAR ADC sampling capacitor (Cs)
A SAR (successive-approximation) ADC briefly connects a small internal capacitor (about 4 pF on an STM32F4) to the pin and charges it to the input voltage. That's the "sample" phase.
Sample time
How long that capacitor stays connected, configurable in ADC cycles. It must be long enough for the capacitor to charge to within ½ LSB.
Ratiometric measurement
The sensor is powered from the same voltage that the ADC uses as its reference, so supply variations cancel out.
Quiescent current Iq
Current the divider wastes all the time: Vin/(R1 + R2).

2. DESIGN STEP BY STEP

  1. Pick the ratio so the maximum input lands just under Vref: Vin,max × ratio ≤ ~0.95 Vref.
  2. Pick the absolute values: high enough for low Iq, low enough for the ADC to sample accurately. If high, add a capacitor at the pin.
  3. Check the sampling: either Rth is small enough for the sample time, or the pin capacitor is big enough that sharing charge with Cs changes the voltage by less than ½ LSB: Cext ≥ Cs·2N+1.
  4. Budget the tolerance and decide whether to calibrate.
  5. Convert in firmware: Vin = code × Vref/2N / ratio.

Measure a Li-ion battery (max 4.2 V) with a 12-bit ADC, Vref = 3.3 V.

  1. Ratio needed ≈ 3.15/4.2 = 0.75 (leaves 4 % headroom below 3.3 V). R2/(R1+R2) = 0.75 means R1 = R2/3. Choose R1 = 330 kΩ, R2 = 1 MΩ → ratio = 1/1.33 = 0.752; 4.2 V → 3.16 V. OK.
  2. Iq = 4.2 V / 1.33 MΩ = 3.2 µA → 2.3 mAh per month. Compare 100 k + 300 k: 10.5 µA → 7.6 mAh/month.
  3. Rth = 330 k ∥ 1 M = 248 kΩ. Charging 4 pF through 248 kΩ to 12-bit accuracy needs about 9 time constants: 9 × 248 k × 4 pF = 9 µs (ln 213 ≈ 9). That forces the longest sample-time setting on every conversion, and any pin leakage current flowing through 248 kΩ adds error too (100 nA → 25 mV). So add a capacitor.
  4. Cext ≥ 4 pF × 213 = 33 nF → use 100 nF. Now the divider plus cap is a low-pass with τ = 248 k × 100 n = 25 ms: fine for a battery (it changes over minutes), useless for anything fast.
  5. Code 3900 → Vpin = 3900 × 3.3/4096 = 3.142 V → Vbat = 3.142/0.752 = 4.18 V.
  6. Tolerance: two 1 % resistors → ratio error up to about ±1.5 % = ±63 mV at 4.2 V. That's a 10 % state-of-charge error on a flat Li-ion curve. Use 0.1 % resistors or calibrate once in production.

Units handling and fixed-point representation

BEGINNER▶

A sensor value without its unit is a bug waiting to happen. Firmware converts raw counts into physical units, stores them either as floats or as scaled integers ("fixed point"), and passes them between modules. Every conversion is a chance to multiply by the wrong factor.

1. DEFINITIONS AND COMMON UNITS

SI units
The international system: m/s², rad/s, T (tesla), Pa, K.
Fixed point
Storing a fractional value as an integer with an agreed scale. Example: temperature in centi-degrees, so 23.45 °C is stored as the integer 2345.
Q format (Qm.n)
A binary fixed-point scale: n fraction bits. Q15 stores −1 … +0.99997 in int16 with value = integer / 32768.
FPU
Floating-point unit. A Cortex-M4F has one for single-precision float only (about 1 cycle for add/multiply). double falls back to slow software routines.

2. A UNITS DISCIPLINE THAT WORKS

  1. Convert counts to units once, in the driver, using the scale for the current configuration.
  2. Pick one internal unit per quantity and write it into the variable name: accel_mg, rate_dps, temp_cC.
  3. Convert again only at the edges (display, radio protocol, log file), in one well-tested function.
  4. Wrap values in structs that carry the unit (and a timestamp and validity flag, card 27) so the compiler catches mixing.
  1. Fixed-point scaling: raw accel = 12 345 counts, 0.061 mg/LSB. In integers: mg = (12 345 × 61 + 500)/1000 = (753 045 + 500)/1000 = 753 mg. The +500 rounds to the nearest integer. The intermediate 753 045 fits easily in int32; the worst case 32 767 × 61 = 1 998 787 also fits.

NTC thermistors: Beta, Steinhart–Hart, divider design, self-heating

INTERMEDIATE▶

An NTC thermistor is a resistor whose resistance drops as it gets hotter. It's the cheapest temperature sensor there is (cents), very sensitive, and very nonlinear, so the work is all in the circuit and the maths that turns an ADC code back into degrees.

1. WHAT IS IT?

NTC — negative temperature coefficient
Resistance goes down when temperature goes up: about −4 % per °C near 25 °C for a typical part.
R25 (R0)
Nominal resistance at 25 °C, e.g. 10 kΩ, with a tolerance like ±1 %.
β (Beta, "B value")
One number that describes how steeply R changes, e.g. 3950 K. Datasheets say which two temperatures it was fitted between: "B25/50" or "B25/85".
Steinhart–Hart (SH)
A more accurate 3-coefficient equation (A, B, C) fitted to three temperature points.
Dissipation constant δ
How many milliwatts heat the thermistor 1 °C above its surroundings, e.g. 1.5 mW/°C. Depends a lot on the PCB and airflow.
Rf (fixed resistor)
The other half of the voltage divider that turns resistance into voltage.
Beta:  1/T = 1/T0 + (1/β)·ln(R/R0)   T in kelvin, T0 = 298.15 K
Steinhart–Hart:  1/T = A + B·ln R + C·(ln R)3

2. FROM TEMPERATURE TO DEGREES IN FIRMWARE: STEP BY STEP

  1. The NTC sits at the bottom of a divider, Rf at the top, both fed from the ADC's reference (Vref).
  2. The pin voltage is V = Vref · Rntc/(Rntc + Rf).
  3. The ADC returns code = 4096 · V/Vref = 4096 · Rntc/(Rntc + Rf). Vref has cancelled: this is ratiometric measurement.
  4. Solve for resistance: Rntc = Rf · code/(4096 − code).
  5. Convert R to T with β, Steinhart–Hart, or a lookup table (LUT) built from the manufacturer's R–T table.
  6. Plausibility: code near 0 means the NTC is shorted; near 4095 means it's open (disconnected).

    [NTC]  ← R falls as T rises

    GND          hot → small R → low code      cold → big R → high code

3. WORKED EXAMPLE (10 kΩ NTC, β = 3950, Rf = 10 kΩ, 12-bit)

  1. ADC reads code = 1500.
  2. Rntc = 10 000 × 1500/(4096 − 1500) = 15 000 000/2596 = 5778 Ω.
  3. ln(R/R0) = ln(5778/10 000) = ln(0.5778) = −0.5485.
  4. 1/T = 1/298.15 + (−0.5485/3950) = 0.0033540 − 0.0001389 = 0.0032151 K−1.
  5. T = 311.03 K → 37.9 °C.
  6. Sanity check: code 2048 (half scale) means Rntc = Rf = 10 kΩ, i.e. exactly 25 °C.

4. CHOOSING Rf: RESOLUTION ACROSS THE RANGE

The divider is most sensitive (most ADC counts per °C) where Rntc = Rf. Far from that point the curve flattens and each count covers many degrees. To balance a range Tmin…Tmax, use Rf ≈ √(R(Tmin) · R(Tmax)).

12-bit, β = 3950 NTC, range −30 … +100 °C: R(−30) = 200 kΩ, R(100) = 698 Ω → √(200k × 698) = 11.8 kΩ, so 10 kΩ is a good choice.

5. SELF-HEATING

Current through the NTC heats it: P = I² · Rntc. The thermistor then reads ΔT = P/δ above the real temperature. P is largest when Rntc = Rf: Pmax = Vref²/(4Rf).

  1. Rf = 1 kΩ, 3.3 V, NTC at 100 °C (698 Ω): I = 3.3/1698 = 1.94 mA; P = (1.94 mA)² × 698 = 2.64 mW.
  2. δ = 1.5 mW/°C → ΔT = 2.64/1.5 = 1.76 °C too hot.
  3. Rf = 10 kΩ at 25 °C: Pmax = 3.3²/(4 × 10k) = 0.27 mW → 0.18 °C. Powering the divider from a GPIO only during the conversion (duty cycle 1 %) cuts the average heating 100×.

6. BETA vs STEINHART–HART vs LUT

  • β is a straight line in (ln R, 1/T) through two points. Exact at, say, 25 and 85 °C, drifting away outside them: commonly 0.5–2 °C at the range ends, and more for parts whose curve bends strongly. The NTC Designer's default example (a B25/85 = 3588 part) is off by about 5 °C at −40 °C and 2 °C at +125 °C.
  • Steinhart–Hart adds the cubic term and is typically within a few hundredths of a degree over a 100 °C span.
  • LUT from the manufacturer's R–T table with linear interpolation: as accurate as the table, no logf() (about 100+ cycles even with an FPU), works on an 8-bit MCU. Index it by ADC code directly so you skip the R computation too.

RTDs (PT100/PT1000) and Callendar–Van Dusen

INTERMEDIATE▶

An RTD is a precision platinum resistor whose resistance rises almost linearly with temperature. It's more accurate, more stable and more linear than an NTC, but its signal is small, so the circuit must be careful about current, lead wires and the reference.

1. WHAT IS IT?

RTD — resistance temperature detector
A metal (usually platinum) resistor with a well-defined, standardised R–T curve.
PT100 / PT1000
Platinum, 100 Ω or 1000 Ω at 0 °C (R0).
α = 0.00385 /°C
Average resistance change per °C between 0 and 100 °C, relative to R0: a PT100 changes 0.385 Ω/°C.
Callendar–Van Dusen (CVD)
The standard polynomial (IEC 60751) giving R from T.
Class A / class B
Tolerance classes: A = ±(0.15 + 0.002|T|) °C, B = ±(0.30 + 0.005|T|) °C.
2-, 3-, 4-wire
How many wires connect the RTD. More wires let the circuit cancel the resistance of the wires themselves.
R(T) = R0[1 + A·T + B·T² + C·(T − 100)·T³],  A = 3.9083e−3, B = −5.775e−7, C = −4.183e−12 (C = 0 for T ≥ 0)

2. HOW A MEASUREMENT WORKS (MAX31865-STYLE)

  1. A bias voltage drives a small current through a precision reference resistor Rref and the RTD in series.
  2. The ADC measures the RTD voltage relative to the Rref voltage, so the result is the ratio Rrtd/Rref. The exact current doesn't matter (ratiometric again).
  3. The MAX31865 returns a 15-bit ratio: code = 32768 · Rrtd/Rref, with Rref = 430 Ω for PT100 or 4.3 kΩ for PT1000 (Maxim's recommended values).
  4. Firmware computes Rrtd = code · Rref/32768, then inverts CVD to get T.
  5. For T ≥ 0 the inversion is the quadratic formula; below 0 °C (where C ≠ 0) use a couple of Newton iterations or a fitted polynomial.
  1. PT100 at 100 °C: R = 100 × (1 + 0.39083 − 0.005775) = 138.51 Ω.
  2. MAX31865 code = 32768 × 138.51/430 = 10 555. (In the RTD register it sits in bits 15..1; bit 0 is the fault flag, so shift right by 1.)
  3. Back: R = 10 555 × 430/32768 = 138.51 Ω.
  4. Invert for T ≥ 0: disc = A² − 4B(1 − R/R0) = 1.52748e−5 − 4(−5.775e−7)(1 − 1.3851) = 1.52748e−5 − 8.896e−7 = 1.43852e−5; √disc = 3.79278e−3.
  5. T = (−3.9083e−3 + 3.79278e−3)/(2 × −5.775e−7) = (−1.1552e−4)/(−1.155e−6) = 100.0 °C.
  6. Lead resistance: 2-wire with 0.25 Ω per wire adds 0.5 Ω → 0.5/0.385 = +1.3 °C error. A 3-wire connection cancels it when the leads match; 4-wire (Kelvin: two wires carry current, two sense voltage) eliminates it.
  7. Self-heating: 1 mA in 100 Ω = 0.1 mW; a PT1000 at 0.3 mA = 0.09 mW but with 3× more signal (1.16 mV/°C vs 0.385 mV/°C).
  2-wire:  ADC sees Rrtd + 2·Rlead (error)
  3-wire:  third wire lets circuit subtract one lead → cancels if leads equal
  4-wire:  I+ ──Rlead──┐             ┌──Rlead── V+   (no current in sense wires
                       RTD                              → no voltage drop)
           I- ──Rlead──┘             └──Rlead── V-
  • Using T = (R/R0 − 1)/α (the linear approximation) at 300 °C: about 3 °C error, because B ≠ 0.
  • Using the T ≥ 0 formula below zero: small errors near 0 °C, growing to tenths of a degree at −100 °C. Branch on R < R0.
  • 2-wire with long cables: a fixed offset of degrees that varies with cable length and temperature.
  • Excitation current left on continuously at high current: self-heating reads high, worst in still air.
An RTD is a platinum resistor following the Callendar–Van Dusen curve, 100 Ω at 0 °C and about 0.385 Ω per degree for a PT100. The signal is small, so I measure it ratiometrically against a precision reference resistor, as the MAX31865 does with 430 Ω, and use 3- or 4-wire connections so lead resistance doesn't add a degree or more. Above zero I invert CVD with the quadratic formula; below zero I iterate because of the C term. I keep excitation current low to avoid self-heating.

Thermocouples and cold-junction compensation

INTERMEDIATE▶

A thermocouple is two different metals welded together. It makes a tiny voltage that depends on the temperature difference between the hot tip and the place where it connects to your board. It survives over 1000 °C, but you must measure the connection temperature too, or the reading is wrong.

1. WHAT IS IT?

Seebeck effect
A temperature gradient along a metal wire produces a voltage. Two different metals produce different voltages; the difference is what you measure.
Hot junction
The welded tip at the temperature you want.
Cold junction (CJ)
Where the thermocouple wires meet copper: your connector or terminal block. It is also a junction and it also produces a voltage.
CJC — cold-junction compensation
Measuring the CJ temperature with another sensor and correcting for it.
Type K
Chromel–Alumel, about 41 µV/°C near room temperature, usable roughly −200 … +1250 °C.
NIST tables / polynomials
The standard voltage-vs-temperature reference for each thermocouple type.
   hot junction (T_hot)          cold junction (T_cj) at the connector
        ●═══ chromel ════════════╗ copper ──────┐
         ╲                       ║              ADC   V_meas = V(T_hot) − V(T_cj)
          ═══ alumel ════════════╝ copper ──────┘
                               [thermistor] measures T_cj

2. CJC STEP BY STEP

  1. Measure the thermocouple voltage Vmeas (microvolts, so you need a high-gain, low-offset amplifier).
  2. Measure the connector temperature Tcj with a thermistor, RTD or the converter's die sensor placed right at the terminals.
  3. Convert Tcj into the voltage a thermocouple would produce at that temperature: V(Tcj) from the NIST table.
  4. Add voltages: V(Thot) = Vmeas + V(Tcj).
  5. Convert that total back into temperature with the inverse NIST table or polynomial.
  1. Type K, hot tip at 200 °C, connector at 25 °C. NIST: V(200 °C) = 8.138 mV, V(25 °C) = 1.000 mV.
  2. The amplifier sees Vmeas = 8.138 − 1.000 = 7.138 mV.
  3. No CJC: 7.138 mV → about 175 °C. You are 25 °C low, exactly the connector temperature.
  4. Correct CJC: 7.138 + 1.000 = 8.138 mV → 200 °C.
  5. The shortcut "T(Vmeas) + Tcj" adds temperatures instead of voltages. For type K between 0 and 200 °C the curve is nearly straight, so the error is small (a fraction of a degree). It grows wherever the slope at Thot differs from the slope near Tcj: below 0 °C, and for more curved types. It's wrong in principle, and interviewers listen for "add voltages, not temperatures".

3. INTEGRATED CONVERTER: MAX31855 (MAXIM, TYPE K)

It does amplification, CJC and digitising, and you read 32 bits over SPI (read-only, no command byte):

BitsMeaning
D31..D1814-bit signed thermocouple temperature, 0.25 °C/LSB
D16Fault (any of the three below)
D15..D412-bit signed internal (cold-junction) temperature, 0.0625 °C/LSB
D2 / D1 / D0SCV short to VCC / SCG short to GND / OC open circuit

Note the trick: shifting the field to the top of an int16_t first and then arithmetic-shifting right sign-extends it correctly. (Right-shifting a negative signed value is implementation-defined in C but arithmetic on every ARM compiler.) The MAX31855 applies a linear approximation internally; for best accuracy away from room temperature, back out the voltage and re-linearise with NIST polynomials.

  • CJ sensor far from the terminals (or a warm regulator next to one terminal): the two copper junctions are at different temperatures, and no CJC can see that. Symptom: error changes when the board warms up.
  • Ordinary copper wire used to extend the thermocouple: you've moved the cold junction to wherever the splice is. Use matching extension wire.
  • No open-circuit detection: a broken thermocouple floats and reads random or room-temperature values. Fault bits exist; use them.
  • Mains pickup on long leads: 40 µV/°C means 1 mV of hum = 25 °C. Filter, twist, shield, and use a converter with 50/60 Hz rejection.
A thermocouple's voltage depends on the difference between the hot tip and the cold junction at the connector, about 41 µV per degree for type K. For cold-junction compensation I measure the connector temperature, convert it to the equivalent thermocouple voltage, add it to the measured voltage, and only then convert back to temperature. Adding temperatures is the common shortcut and is wrong because the curve isn't linear. I also make sure the terminals are isothermal, use the open-circuit detection, and filter mains pickup.

Digital temperature sensors and thermal placement

INTERMEDIATE▶

A digital temperature IC does the analog work for you and hands back a calibrated number over I²C. The catch is that it measures the temperature of its own silicon die, so in a product the accuracy problem moves from electronics to thermal design.

1. WHAT IS IT?

Die temperature
The temperature of the sensor chip itself. Heat from the PCB, nearby parts and the sensor's own conversions all affect it.
One-shot mode
The sensor sleeps, does one conversion when asked, then sleeps again. Lower power, less self-heating.
Conversion time
How long one measurement takes. Reading faster than this returns the old value.
Example parts
TI TMP117: ±0.1 °C from −20 to +50 °C, 16-bit, 7.8125 m°C/LSB. TI TMP102: ±0.5 °C, 12-bit, 0.0625 °C/LSB. MCU internal sensor: a few °C, even after calibration.

2. READING A TMP117 STEP BY STEP

  1. At boot, read the device ID register (0x0F) and check it.
  2. Configure the conversion mode, averaging and cycle time in the configuration register (0x01). Example: one-shot with 8× averaging takes about 125 ms.
  3. Wait for data-ready (a flag in the configuration register, or the ALERT pin configured as data-ready).
  4. Read the 16-bit temperature register (0x00) in one transaction, MSB first on this part.
  5. Convert: T = (int16_t)raw × 0.0078125 °C.
  1. Raw = 0x0C80 = 3200 → 3200 × 0.0078125 = 25.00 °C.
  2. Raw = 0xFF00 → as int16 = −256 → −2.00 °C. As unsigned it would be 65 280 × 0.0078125 = 510 °C: the classic sign bug.
  3. Placement: a 3.3 V LDO dissipating 0.3 W, 5 mm away on the same copper plane, can lift the sensor by 1–3 °C. The ±0.1 °C sensor now reads ambient ±2 °C.
✖ WRONG
✔ RIGHT
  • Sensor next to a heat source or on a big copper pour: reads several °C high. Symptom: reading rises when the radio transmits or the CPU is busy.
  • Continuous conversion with no standby: the sensor's own current warms the die slightly; worse in sealed enclosures.
  • Using the MCU's internal sensor as an ambient thermometer: it measures the CPU die, which is hotter than ambient by an amount that depends on load.
A digital temperature sensor like the TMP117 gives a calibrated value good to ±0.1 °C, but it measures its own die, so accuracy in a product depends on thermal design: keep it away from regulators and big copper, use slots or thin traces to isolate it, and use one-shot mode to avoid self-heating. In firmware I read the register as a signed 16-bit value, only after data-ready, and convert with the datasheet LSB.

Current sensing: shunt + amplifier vs Hall effect

INTERMEDIATE▶

To measure current you either put a tiny resistor in its path and measure the voltage across it (a shunt), or measure the magnetic field the current creates (Hall effect). Shunts are cheap and precise; Hall sensors give isolation and no power loss.

1. DEFINITIONS

Shunt resistor
A low-value precision resistor (milliohms). V = I · R.
Current-sense amplifier (CSA)
A differential amplifier with fixed gain that amplifies the small shunt voltage, even when both shunt terminals sit at a high voltage. TI INA240: gain 20/50/100/200 V/V, common mode −4 … 80 V, designed to reject PWM edges.
Common-mode voltage
The average voltage of both amplifier inputs relative to ground. On a high-side shunt it is about the supply voltage.
Low-side / high-side
Shunt between load and ground / between supply and load.
Kelvin connection
Separate sense traces from the inner pads of the shunt, so the voltage across copper and solder isn't measured.
Hall-effect sensor
A sensor that outputs a voltage proportional to the magnetic field from the current-carrying conductor (Allegro ACS712: 185 mV/A for the ±5 A version, zero at VCC/2).

2. DESIGNING A SHUNT CHANNEL STEP BY STEP

  1. Choose the maximum current Imax and an acceptable power loss → Rshunt ≤ Ploss/Imax².
  2. Choose the amplifier gain so Imax · R · G uses most of the ADC range.
  3. For bidirectional current, bias the output to mid-scale (a reference pin).
  4. Check the low-current floor: amplifier offset Vos/Rshunt = the current error at zero.
  5. Lay out Kelvin connections and filter to the bandwidth you need.
  6. Calibrate zero (at a known zero current) and gain (at a known current) in production if accuracy matters.
  1. ±10 A motor current, accept 0.2 W loss: R = 0.2/10² = 2 mΩ. At 10 A: V = 20 mV.
  2. INA240A2 (G = 50): 20 mV × 50 = 1.0 V. Reference at 1.65 V (mid-supply) → output 0.65 … 2.65 V for −10 … +10 A. Fits a 3.3 V ADC.
  3. Sensitivity: 50 × 2 mΩ = 100 mV/A → 12-bit LSB 0.806 mV = 8 mA/LSB.
  4. Offset floor: 25 µV (max) / 2 mΩ = 12.5 mA. Measuring 50 mA idle current with ±12.5 mA error is ±25 %; 10 A is ±0.13 %.
  5. Shunt tempco 50 ppm/°C over a 50 °C rise = 0.25 % gain change.
  6. Hall alternative: ACS712-05B at 5 V: 3 A → 2.5 + 3 × 0.185 = 3.055 V (needs a divider for a 3.3 V ADC). Its offset drift and noise are tens of mA, but it's isolated.

  VBAT ─[Rsh]─┬─ LOAD ─ GND        VBAT ─ LOAD ─┬─[Rsh]─ GND
         └─CSA┘ (CM = VBAT)                     └─amp (CM ≈ 0 V)
  sees short-to-GND faults         load ground lifted by I·R; can't see
                                   a load-to-GND short
  • No Kelvin connection: 1 mΩ of extra copper and solder on a 2 mΩ shunt gives +50 % error that also drifts with temperature (copper: +0.39 %/°C).
  • Generic op-amp on a PWM-switched high-side shunt: the common mode swings 0 → 48 V in nanoseconds; the output rings for microseconds. Use a CSA designed for PWM rejection, or sample mid-PWM-period.
  • Hall sensor next to another high-current trace or a magnet: offset errors. Symptom: "zero" current reads 200 mA when the neighbouring phase is on.
For precision at low voltage I use a milliohm shunt with a current-sense amplifier: the shunt value trades signal against I²R loss, the amplifier offset divided by the shunt sets the zero-current error, and Kelvin connections stop copper resistance being measured. High-side sensing needs an amplifier with a common-mode range to the supply, and for motor phases one with PWM rejection like the INA240. When I need isolation or very high currents I use a Hall sensor and accept more offset drift and noise, calibrating zero at a known no-load moment.

Pressure, humidity and ambient light sensors

INTERMEDIATE▶

These three show up in every phone and watch. Each has one quirk you must know: barometers need factory compensation maths, humidity depends on temperature, and light sensors must average out mains flicker.

1. BAROMETRIC PRESSURE

Piezoresistive MEMS barometer
A thin silicon membrane over a vacuum cavity, with resistors that change value as it bends (a Wheatstone bridge). Bosch BME280/BMP390, ST LPS22HB.
Compensation (trimming) coefficients
Per-unit constants measured at the factory and stored inside the sensor. The raw reading is useless without them.
t_fine
In Bosch's BME280 integer compensation code, an intermediate value computed from temperature and then used in the pressure and humidity formulas.
  1. At init, read the coefficient block once (BME280: dig_T1…dig_P9) and store it.
  2. Each cycle, burst-read raw pressure and temperature together (so they belong to the same measurement).
  3. Compensate temperature first: this produces t_fine.
  4. Compensate pressure using t_fine; never the other way round.
  5. Convert to altitude if needed: h ≈ 44330 · (1 − (p/p0)1/5.255) m.
  1. Near sea level 1 hPa ≈ 8.4 m of altitude.
  2. One building floor (3 m) ≈ 0.36 hPa. The BMP390's relative accuracy ±0.03 hPa ≈ ±25 cm, so floor counting works.
  3. Absolute altitude needs the local sea-level pressure p0: weather moves p0 by ±20 hPa ≈ ±170 m.

2. HUMIDITY

RH — relative humidity
How much water vapour the air holds as a percentage of the maximum it could hold at that temperature.
Capacitive polymer sensor
A polymer layer absorbs water and changes a capacitance (Sensirion SHT4x: ±1.8 %RH typ.).
Dew point Td
The temperature at which that air would condense. Independent of where you measure it, which makes it a better quantity to compare.
  1. Air at 25 °C, 60 %RH. Magnus: γ = ln(0.60) + 17.62 × 25/(243.12 + 25) = −0.511 + 1.643 = 1.132.
  2. Td = 243.12 × 1.132/(17.62 − 1.132) = 275.2/16.49 = 16.7 °C.
  3. If the sensor sits 1 °C warmer than the air (self-heating from nearby electronics), it reads about 56 %RH: a 4 %RH error from 1 °C. RH and T must be measured at the same spot.

3. AMBIENT LIGHT

Lux
Light intensity weighted by the human eye's sensitivity (the photopic curve, peak at 555 nm).
Integration time
How long the photodiode collects charge for one reading.
Gain
Amplification. High gain for dim light, low gain so bright sunlight doesn't saturate.
  1. Read the counts at the current gain and integration time.
  2. If near saturation, reduce gain or time; if tiny, increase them (auto-ranging with hysteresis).
  3. Convert: lux = counts × k/(gain × tint), with k calibrated for the cover glass.
  4. Correct for infrared with a second (IR) channel on multi-channel parts.

Mains lighting flickers at twice the mains frequency: 100 Hz (50 Hz grid) or 120 Hz (60 Hz grid). A 100 ms integration window contains exactly 10 periods of 100 Hz and 12 periods of 120 Hz, so the flicker averages out on both grids. A 15 ms window would catch a different part of the flicker cycle each time and read noisy.

  • Barometer: pressure compensated before temperature (stale t_fine): errors of several hPa that change with temperature.
  • Humidity sensor heater used to remove condensation: T and RH are invalid during and shortly after the pulse; discard those samples.
  • Light sensor behind dark glass without per-product calibration: lux readings 5–20× too low; auto-brightness never brightens.
For pressure sensors I read the factory compensation coefficients once and apply the vendor's formulas, temperature first because the pressure formula depends on it. One hPa is about 8 m near sea level, so relative altitude like floor counting works but absolute altitude needs a reference pressure. Humidity is relative to temperature, so the temperature must be measured at the same point, and 1 °C of self-heating is about 4 %RH of error. Light sensors auto-range gain and integration time, and I use 100 ms integration to cancel 100 and 120 Hz flicker.

IMU fundamentals: accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer

INTERMEDIATE▶

An IMU (inertial measurement unit) combines an accelerometer (measures "push", including gravity), a gyroscope (measures how fast you rotate) and often a magnetometer (measures Earth's magnetic field). Each one alone gives a wrong orientation in a predictable way, which is why they are fused (cards 22–23).

1. ACCELEROMETER: IT MEASURES SPECIFIC FORCE, NOT ACCELERATION

Specific force f
f = a − g: the acceleration you actually have minus gravity. In practice: all forces acting on the device except gravity, divided by its mass.
Tilt
The angle between the device axes and the vertical (gravity direction). Roll and pitch.
  1. At rest on a table, the table pushes up on the device: the accelerometer reads +1 g upward, even though a = 0.
  2. In free fall, nothing pushes: it reads 0 g on all axes.
  3. So when the device is still, f points straight up and its direction gives the tilt.
  4. When the device accelerates sideways, that sideways push adds to f and tilts the vector. The sensor cannot tell "tilted" from "accelerating"; that is Einstein's equivalence principle, not a sensor flaw.
roll = atan2(fy, fz)    pitch = atan2(−fx, √(fy² + fz²))  (signs depend on axis convention)
  1. Device still, rolled 30°: fy = sin 30° = 0.500 g, fz = cos 30° = 0.866 g → atan2(0.5, 0.866) = 30.0°. ✔
  2. Same device flat in a car braking at 0.3 g: fx = 0.3 g, fz = 1 g → pitch reads atan2(−0.3, 1.0) = −16.7° even though the device is level. ✖ (Scenario 01)
  3. Near level, 1° of tilt = sin 1° × 1000 mg = 17.5 mg on the horizontal axis. Near 90°, the vertical axis changes only cos-like: why you always use atan2 of two axes, never asin of one.

2. GYROSCOPE: ANGULAR RATE

Angular rate ω
Rotation speed in °/s (dps) or rad/s. Angle = ∫ω dt.
Bias
Output at zero rotation. Integrated, it becomes a steady drift.
ARW — angle random walk
The random angle error from integrating white noise; grows with √time. °/√h = noise density in °/s/√Hz × 60.
Bias instability
How much the bias wanders slowly; the minimum of the Allan deviation plot (°/h).
  1. Uncorrected bias 0.5 dps → after 60 s: 0.5 × 60 = 30° drift, growing linearly.
  2. Noise only, 3.8 mdps/√Hz → ARW = 0.23 °/√h → after 1 minute: 0.23 × √(1/60) = 0.03°.
  3. Lesson: for consumer MEMS, bias dominates drift, not noise. Remove it.

3. MAGNETOMETER: HEADING

Earth's field
25–65 µT, pointing north and steeply downward in most places (the inclination or dip angle, e.g. about 60° in Europe).
Hard iron
Magnetised parts on the board (speaker magnets, steel screws) add a constant offset that rotates with the device.
Soft iron
Ferrous material that bends the field, distorting it differently in different directions.
Declination
The angle between magnetic north and true north at your location.
  1. Calibrate hard/soft iron (card 21).
  2. Use roll/pitch (from accel or the fusion filter) to rotate the mag vector into the horizontal plane (tilt compensation).
  3. Heading = atan2(−my,h, mx,h) (convention-dependent), then add declination for true north.
           accel                 gyro                   mag
  measures gravity + push    rate of rotation      field direction
  ✔ absolute tilt, no drift  ✔ smooth, fast, all   ✔ absolute heading
  ✖ fooled by acceleration   ✖ bias → drift         ✖ fooled by iron, magnets
  ✖ blind to heading (yaw)                         ✖ needs tilt to be known
          └──────────── fuse them: each covers the others' weakness ──────────┘
  • Accel-only tilt in a moving vehicle or on a wrist: tilt swings by tens of degrees during acceleration.
  • Gyro-only heading: drifts tens of degrees per minute unless bias is removed, and keeps drifting slowly even after.
  • Heading without tilt compensation: tilting the device 10° with 60° field inclination changes the heading by many degrees.
  • Expecting the accelerometer to correct yaw: rotating about the vertical axis doesn't change the gravity vector, so yaw is unobservable from accel. You need a magnetometer (or GNSS, or vision).
An accelerometer measures specific force, so at rest it reads 1 g up and gives tilt from atan2 of two axes, but it can't distinguish tilt from linear acceleration. A gyro measures rate, which is smooth but integrates any bias into drift: half a degree per second of bias is 30 degrees a minute. A magnetometer gives absolute heading once it's calibrated for hard and soft iron and tilt-compensated. Accel can't see yaw, so heading needs the mag. That's why we fuse them.

MEMS internals (briefly): why gyros cost power and drift

INTERMEDIATE▶

MEMS (micro-electro-mechanical systems) sensors are tiny mechanical structures etched in silicon, next to an analog/digital chip that reads them. Knowing roughly how they move explains their noise, their power use, their self-test and why mechanical stress shifts their offset.

1. ACCELEROMETER

  1. A small silicon proof mass hangs on silicon springs.
  2. Acceleration pushes the mass; it moves until the spring force balances: x = m·a/k = a/ω0², where ω0 = √(k/m) is the resonant frequency.
  3. Comb-shaped electrodes form two capacitors that change in opposite directions as the mass moves (differential sensing). The changes are attofarads (10−18 F).
  4. A low-noise charge amplifier and ADC on the companion ASIC (application-specific IC) turn that into counts.
  5. Self-test: the ASIC applies a voltage that pulls the mass electrostatically. If the output shifts by the specified amount, the mechanics and the signal chain both work.

Resonance at 5 kHz: ω0 = 2π × 5000 = 31 416 rad/s. 1 g = 9.81 m/s² → x = 9.81/31 416² = 9.9 nm, about 1/10 000 of a hair's width. That is why packaging stress of a few nanometres of bending shows up as tens of mg of offset.

2. GYROSCOPE (CORIOLIS VIBRATORY)

  1. A proof mass is driven into continuous oscillation at its resonance (tens of kHz) by an electrostatic drive loop.
  2. When the device rotates at rate Ω, the moving mass feels the Coriolis force F = −2m·Ω×v, perpendicular to its velocity.
  3. That force makes the mass also move along a second (sense) axis, with amplitude proportional to Ω.
  4. The ASIC demodulates the sense signal at the drive frequency to get the rate.

Consequences: the drive loop runs all the time → a gyro draws ~0.4–0.5 mA versus µA for an accelerometer in low-power mode, and needs tens of ms to start (the oscillation must build up). Imperfect drive/sense separation (quadrature) and temperature shifting the resonance cause bias and bias drift.

3. MAGNETOMETERS

Hall (cheap, noisy), AMR (anisotropic magnetoresistance: needs periodic set/reset current pulses to re-align its film), TMR/GMR (tunnel/giant magnetoresistance: sensitive, low power), fluxgate (precision, larger).

  • MEMS part next to a mounting screw or board edge: board flex during assembly or temperature changes stress the package; the offset shifts after the product is closed up.
  • Gyro powered off and on every sample to save power: start-up time (tens of ms) and a settling transient make the first samples wrong. Duty-cycle in blocks, discard the first samples.
A MEMS accelerometer is a proof mass on springs whose nanometre-scale motion is read as a differential capacitance, so mechanical stress from soldering or mounting shifts the offset. A MEMS gyro keeps a mass vibrating at resonance and senses the Coriolis force rotation causes; the always-running drive loop is why gyros draw hundreds of µA and start slowly, and drive-sense imperfections are why they have bias that moves with temperature.

Digital sensors over I²C/SPI: register maps, burst reads, auto-increment, BDU, data-ready

INTERMEDIATE▶

A digital sensor looks to firmware like a small array of 8-bit registers. You write some to configure it and read others to get data. Most digital-sensor bugs come from how those reads happen: one byte at a time instead of in one burst, at the wrong moment, or without the bits that keep a sample consistent.

1. DEFINITIONS

Register map
The list of register addresses and the meaning of each bit. Includes ID (WHO_AM_I), control, status, output and FIFO registers.
Burst (multi-byte) read
One bus transaction that reads several consecutive registers.
Auto-increment
The sensor's internal register pointer moves to the next address after each byte, which makes burst reads work. LSM6DSO: CTRL3_C.IF_INC (bit 2, on by default). LIS3DH: per transaction, set bit 7 of the register address on I²C (0x80 | 0x28) or bit 6 (MS) on SPI.
BDU — block data update
When enabled, the sensor will not overwrite the output registers of a sample until you have read both its low and high byte. LSM6DSO: CTRL3_C bit 6. LIS3DH: CTRL_REG4 bit 7. Off by default on both.
Data-ready (DRDY)
A status bit (LSM6DSO STATUS_REG 0x1E: XLDA bit 0, GDA bit 1) or an interrupt pin that signals a new sample is available.
Latched vs pulsed interrupt
Latched: the pin stays active until you read the source register. Pulsed: it pulses briefly (≈ 75 µs on some ST parts) by itself.

2. WHAT THE BUS DOES DURING A 6-BYTE ACCELEROMETER READ

  1. I²C: START → device address + W → register address 0x28 → repeated START → device address + R → 6 data bytes (master ACKs each, NACKs the last) → STOP.
  2. SPI (ST convention): CS low → first byte 0x80 | 0x28 = 0xA8 (bit 7 = read) → clock out 6 bytes → CS high. ST parts support SPI mode 3 (and 0); check yours.
  3. Inside the sensor, auto-increment walks 0x28 → 0x29 → … → 0x2D: X_L, X_H, Y_L, Y_H, Z_L, Z_H.
  4. With BDU on, the sensor holds the output registers of this sample until all bytes of it are read, then allows the next update.
  5. Firmware assembles three signed 16-bit values (little-endian).
  I2C:  S │ 0x6A W │A│ 0x28 │A│ Sr │ 0x6A R │A│ XL │A│ XH │A│ YL │A│ YH │A│ ZL │A│ ZH │N│ P
         (0x6A = LSM6DSO 7-bit address with SA0 = 0; 0x6B with SA0 = 1)

3. WORKED EXAMPLE: THE TORN SAMPLE (WHY BDU EXISTS)

  1. Without BDU the Z value is changing from 0x00FF (255) to 0x0100 (256): a real change of 1 count.
  2. You read Z_L first: it still holds sample n → 0xFF.
  3. The sensor updates the registers between your two byte reads (the update rate is independent of your bus timing).
  4. You read Z_H: now from sample n+1 → 0x01.
  5. You assemble 0x01FF = 511. The true value was 255 or 256: a 256-count spike (≈ 15.6 mg at ±2 g). It happens only when a value crosses a byte boundary and only when the update lands mid-read, so it looks random and rare.
  6. With BDU = 1 and a single burst read, this cannot happen.

A burst keeps all axes from the same sample, and BDU stops the sensor overwriting a register pair halfway through my read, which otherwise causes rare 256-count spikes. I also know the part's quirks, like the LIS3DH needing the address MSB set for auto-increment, and whether its interrupts latch.

FIFO, watermark interrupts and ODR vs bus bandwidth

INTERMEDIATE▶

A sensor FIFO is a small queue inside the sensor that stores samples until you collect them. It lets the MCU sleep and fetch many samples at once, but only if you size the batch so the queue never overflows and the bus can carry the data.

1. DEFINITIONS

FIFO — first in, first out
A queue: samples come out in the order they went in.
Depth
How many samples fit. ADXL345: 32 samples. LSM6DSO: 3 KB (≈ 512 words of 7 bytes, tag + 6 data bytes).
Watermark (WTM)
A fill level you choose; when reached, the sensor raises an interrupt.
Overrun / overflow
The FIFO is full and a new sample arrives. In stream mode the oldest sample is lost. ADXL345 flags it in INT_SOURCE bit 0; LSM6DSO in FIFO_STATUS2.FIFO_OVR_IA.
FIFO modes
Bypass (off), FIFO (stop when full; for capturing a burst), Stream (overwrite oldest), Stream-to-FIFO (stream until a trigger, then freeze to keep the history around an event).

2. SIZING THE WATERMARK STEP BY STEP

  1. Fill time to the watermark: twm = Nwm/ODR. This sets how often the MCU wakes.
  2. Headroom after the watermark: thead = (depth − Nwm)/ODR. The MCU must start reading within this time.
  3. Read time: bytes × bits per byte / bus speed (+ overhead). Must be well under thead + twm.
  4. Worst-case response = interrupt latency + higher-priority tasks + read time. Must be < thead.

ADXL345, 32-sample FIFO, ODR 800 Hz, I²C 400 kHz.

  1. Full FIFO fills in 32/800 = 40 ms. A task that polls every 50 ms always overflows (Scenario 03).
  2. Watermark 16 → wake every 20 ms; headroom (32 − 16)/800 = 20 ms.
  3. Reading one ADXL345 FIFO entry = 6-byte burst ≈ (3 + 6) × 9 + overhead ≈ 90 SCL clocks = 225 µs at 400 kHz. 16 entries ≈ 3.6 ms. Fine. At 100 kHz it would be 14.4 ms, eating most of the headroom.

I always enable and count the overrun flag, and for kHz ODRs I use SPI.

Timestamping and synchronising multiple sensors

INTERMEDIATE▶

Every sample needs to know when it was measured. Fusion filters integrate over time and compare sensors with each other, so a wrong or jittery timestamp is as bad as a wrong value.

1. DEFINITIONS

Sample time vs read time
When the sensor measured the value vs when your code fetched it. They can differ by milliseconds (FIFO: by seconds).
Jitter
Random variation in timing from one sample to the next.
Input capture
A timer feature that latches the timer count in hardware at a pin edge, so the timestamp is exact regardless of interrupt latency.
Clock drift
The sensor's oscillator runs at a slightly different rate from the MCU's (±1–2 %), so their time bases slowly separate.

2. GETTING GOOD TIMESTAMPS STEP BY STEP

  1. Route DRDY (or the FIFO watermark) to a pin that is also a timer input-capture channel. Best: hardware timestamp. Next best: read a free-running µs timer as the first instruction of the ISR.
  2. For single samples: tsample = tedge (minus the sensor's fixed internal filter delay if you want to be exact).
  3. For FIFO batches: the watermark edge corresponds to the newest sample. Reconstruct backwards: ti = twm − (N − 1 − i)·Ts.
  4. Estimate the real Ts from consecutive watermark timestamps: Ts = Δtwm/ΔN, smoothed with an EMA. Never use the nominal 1/ODR.
  5. Feed the filter the real Δt between samples.
  6. For several sensors at different rates, either interpolate them onto a common timebase, or run an event-driven filter: predict with each gyro sample at its time, apply each accel/mag/baro update at its own time.
  1. FIFO watermark of 52 samples at nominal 104 Hz. Watermark IRQs captured at 1 000 000 µs and 1 507 300 µs.
  2. Measured Ts = 507 300/52 = 9755.8 µs (real ODR 102.5 Hz, 1.4 % slow; nominal would be 9615.4 µs).
  3. Sample i = 0 (oldest) of the second batch: t = 1 507 300 − 51 × 9755.8 = 1 009 754 µs; sample 51 (newest) = 1 507 300 µs.
  4. If you had stamped all 52 samples with the read time, the fusion filter would see 51 samples at Δt = 0 and one with Δt = 507 ms: a huge jolt each batch.
  5. Jitter example: an RTOS task polling "every 10 ms" actually runs at 8–14 ms intervals. Integrating 200 dps with a fixed dt = 10 ms gives per-step angle errors of up to 200 × 0.004 = 0.8° of jitter (Scenario 07).
✖ WRONG
✔ RIGHT
  • Read-time stamps on FIFO data: bursts of zero-Δt samples; filters jump or divide by zero.
  • Nominal ODR used for dt: integrated angles are off by the oscillator error (1–2 %).
  • Pairing "latest gyro" with "latest mag" that are 40 ms apart during fast rotation: heading jitter of (rate × 40 ms), e.g. 200 dps → 8°.
I timestamp the sample, not the read: ideally a timer input capture on the data-ready or watermark pin. For FIFO batches the watermark time belongs to the newest sample and I reconstruct older ones backward using the measured sample period, estimated from successive watermark timestamps, because the sensor clock can be a percent or two off. The filter then integrates with the real Δt, and sensors at different rates are either interpolated to a common timebase or fused event by event at their own timestamps.

Sensor driver architecture: init, WHO_AM_I, config, read, self-test, errors

INTERMEDIATE▶

A sensor driver is the code that owns one sensor: it brings it up, configures it, reads it and handles everything that can go wrong. A good one is portable (the same code on bare metal, an RTOS or a PC unit test) and never hangs or returns a made-up value.

1. THE LAYERS

  application / fusion       wants: accel_mg[3], gyro_dps[3], t_us, status
        │
  sensor driver (lsm6dso.c)  register sequences, scaling, plausibility, retries
        │  calls rd()/wr() through function pointers
  bus port (i2c_port.c)      HAL calls, DMA, timeouts, bus recovery
        │
  hardware                   I2C/SPI peripheral, INT pin
Bus abstraction
Function pointers rd(ctx, reg, buf, len) / wr(…) supplied by the platform, so the driver never calls a specific HAL.
ctx (context)
An opaque pointer the platform uses to find its I²C handle, address or chip-select pin.
Bus recovery
If an I²C slave was reset mid-byte it can hold SDA low forever. Toggle SCL up to 9 times as GPIO until SDA is released, then send STOP.

  1. Wait the datasheet boot time after power-up.
  2. Read WHO_AM_I (LSM6DSO: 0x0F should read 0x6C). Mismatch → wrong part, wrong address (SA0 strap), dead bus. Return an error; don't continue.
  3. Software reset (CTRL3_C.SW_RESET) and poll the bit until it self-clears, with a timeout.
  4. Write BDU + IF_INC, full scale, ODR, filters, FIFO and interrupt routing. Store the scale factors for the FS you wrote.
  5. Read back critical registers and compare.
  6. Optionally run self-test (production or boot).
  7. Enable the DRDY/watermark interrupt last, so it can't fire before the driver is ready.

Calibration: two-point, multi-point LUT, temperature compensation, storing it in NVM

INTERMEDIATE▶

Calibration means measuring how one particular unit is wrong, against a known reference, and storing correction numbers so the firmware can undo it. The maths is simple; the hard parts are choosing the points, averaging out noise, and making sure the numbers survive for the life of the product.

1. DEFINITIONS

Reference
A known true input: a calibrated pressure source, a temperature bath, gravity itself (exactly 1 g).
Two-point calibration
Measure at two references and fit a straight line: corrects offset and gain.
Multi-point / LUT calibration
Measure at N points and store a table; interpolate between neighbours at runtime. Also corrects nonlinearity.
Linear interpolation
Assume a straight line between two neighbouring table points.
NVM — non-volatile memory
Memory that keeps data without power: flash, EEPROM, OTP (one-time programmable).
CRC-32
A 32-bit checksum that detects corrupted data with very high probability.

2. TWO-POINT STEP BY STEP

  1. Apply reference y1; average many raw readings → r1.
  2. Apply reference y2; average → r2.
  3. gain = (y2 − y1)/(r2 − r1); offset = y1 − gain·r1.
  4. At runtime: y = gain·r + offset.
  1. Pressure sensor: at 10 kPa the average raw is 11 820 counts, at 90 kPa it is 53 540 counts.
  2. gain = (90 − 10)/(53 540 − 11 820) = 80/41 720 = 0.0019175 kPa/count.
  3. offset = 10 − 0.0019175 × 11 820 = 10 − 22.665 = −12.665 kPa.
  4. Raw 32 000 → 0.0019175 × 32 000 − 12.665 = 61.36 − 12.665 = 48.70 kPa.
  5. Noise check: σ = 8 counts per reading; averaging 64 readings → 1 count → 0.002 kPa uncertainty per point. Without averaging, one 3σ sample (24 counts = 0.046 kPa) would be baked in permanently.

3. MULTI-POINT WITH LINEAR INTERPOLATION

  1. Store the table sorted by raw value (it must be monotonic).
  2. Find the segment i with ri ≤ r < ri+1 (binary search for big tables).
  3. y = yi + (r − ri)·(yi+1 − yi)/(ri+1 − ri).
  4. Outside the table: clamp or extrapolate from the end segment, and set an out-of-range flag.

Table points (raw → kPa): (21 000 → 30), (31 500 → 50). Raw 26 000: fraction = (26 000 − 21 000)/(31 500 − 21 000) = 5000/10 500 = 0.476 → y = 30 + 0.476 × 20 = 39.52 kPa.

4. TEMPERATURE COMPENSATION

Measure offset (and gain) at, say, −10, 25 and 60 °C in a chamber. Fit offset(T) = c0 + c1(T − 25) + c2(T − 25)². Store c0..2 per unit. At runtime read the on-die temperature sensor and subtract offset(T). Gyro bias versus temperature is the most important case in IMUs.

5. STORING IT SAFELY

  1. Define a record: magic number, layout version, payload length, sequence counter, payload, CRC-32.
  2. Keep two copies (slot A, slot B). Write the new record into the older slot, then the newest valid copy wins on boot. A power cut mid-write leaves the other slot intact.
  3. Store it in a sector that firmware updates never erase.
  4. Treat the layout like an ABI (application binary interface): only append fields, bump the version, and write code that reads every older version.
  • A field inserted in the middle of the struct without a version bump: after a firmware update, old bytes are read as the wrong fields. CRC still passes because the bytes themselves are intact (Scenario 06).
  • Calibration in the application's flash sector: a full-image FOTA erase wipes it.
  • One copy, power loss during the write: no valid calibration left; units in the field fall back to defaults.
  • Calibration points too close together (e.g. 45 and 55 kPa for a 0–100 kPa range): small errors in the points become large slope errors at the ends.
Two-point calibration fits gain and offset from two averaged reference measurements and corrects offset and gain only; multi-point stores a monotonic table and interpolates linearly between neighbours, which also removes nonlinearity, and temperature compensation fits offset and gain against the on-die temperature. I store calibration as a versioned record with magic, length, sequence number and CRC, in two slots in a sector firmware updates never erase, and only ever append fields, so a power cut or an update can't destroy or misinterpret it.

Filtering: moving average, EMA, median, outlier rejection

INTERMEDIATE▶

Filters reduce noise by combining several samples. Each kind removes a different sort of disturbance and each adds delay. Choosing one means knowing what noise you have and how much lag you can tolerate.

1. DEFINITIONS

Moving average (MA, boxcar) of N
Output = mean of the last N samples.
EMA — exponential moving average (1-pole IIR)
y ← y + α·(x − y). Every new sample nudges the output a fraction α of the way toward it. IIR = infinite impulse response: old samples never quite disappear.
Time constant τ
Time for the EMA to cover 63 % of a step. α = Δt/(τ + Δt).
Median filter
Output = middle value of the last N (odd) samples. Ignores single spikes completely.
MAD — median absolute deviation
Median of |x − median|. A spread estimate that outliers can't inflate. For Gaussian data σ ≈ 1.4826·MAD.
Group delay
How late the filter output is. MA: (N − 1)/2 samples.

2. WORKED EXAMPLES

IMU calibration: 6-position accelerometer, gyro bias at rest, magnetometer hard/soft iron

ADVANCED▶

IMUs come with offsets and gain errors large enough to ruin tilt and heading. The good news is that nature gives you free references: gravity is exactly 1 g, a still device rotates at exactly 0 dps, and Earth's field has a constant magnitude. Each calibration below exploits one of those facts.

1. ACCELEROMETER: 6-POSITION CALIBRATION

ai+, ai−
The averaged reading of axis i when that axis points straight up (true +1 g) and straight down (true −1 g).
Offset bi, scale si
Corrections such that acal = (araw − b)/s.
  1. Place the device on a flat surface with +X up. Wait for stillness, average 1 s of data. Repeat with −X up, +Y, −Y, +Z, −Z (6 poses).
  2. For each axis: bi = (ai+ + ai−)/2,  si = (ai+ − ai−)/2 g.
  3. Store b and s per unit (card 20).
  4. Runtime: acal,i = (araw,i − bi)/si.
  1. Z up: 1018 mg. Z down: −986 mg.
  2. bz = (1018 + (−986))/2 = +16 mg; sz = (1018 − (−986))/2000 = 2004/2000 = 1.002.
  3. Check: (1018 − 16)/1.002 = 1000.0 mg ✔; (−986 − 16)/1.002 = −1000.0 mg ✔.
  4. The flat surface doesn't need to be perfectly level: a 1° tilt changes the up reading by only 1 − cos 1° = 0.015 %, because cos is flat near 0°. That's why up/down poses work without a fixture.

Beyond 6 parameters: the full model acal = M(araw − b) has 9 values in M (scale + cross-axis misalignment) and 3 in b. Collect 12+ random still poses and solve by least squares with the constraint |acal| = 1 g in each pose.

2. GYRO BIAS AT REST

  1. Detect stillness: accelerometer variance over 1 s below a threshold (e.g. σ < 5 mg) and |ω| below e.g. 3 dps.
  2. Average ω for 1–2 s: that's the bias estimate. Averaging N samples reduces noise by √N.
  3. Subtract it from every sample. Keep updating it slowly (an EMA) during every still period, because bias moves with temperature.
  4. Store a bias-vs-temperature table to start well at the next boot.

Noise σ = 0.05 dps per sample at 104 Hz. 2 s of stillness = 208 samples → bias uncertainty 0.05/√208 = 0.0035 dps → residual drift 0.2°/min, versus 30°/min for an uncorrected 0.5 dps bias.

3. MAGNETOMETER: HARD AND SOFT IRON

Hard iron
Fixed magnetisation on the board: shifts every reading by a constant vector. The data sphere is moved off-centre.
Soft iron
Ferrous material that distorts the field depending on direction: turns the sphere into a tilted ellipsoid.
Ellipsoid fit
Fitting the equation xᵀAx + bᵀx + c = 0 to the data, then mapping the ellipsoid back onto a sphere.
  1. Rotate the device through as many orientations as possible (figure-8 motions) while logging raw mag data.
  2. Simple min/max method: offseti = (maxi + mini)/2; radiusi = (maxi − mini)/2; r̄ = mean of the radii; scalei = r̄/radiusi. Handles hard iron and axis-aligned soft iron.
  3. Full fit: least squares for A, b, c. Centre = −½A−1b. Correction matrix W ∝ A½. mcal = W(mraw − centre).
  4. Verify: |mcal| should be nearly constant (spread of a few %).
  5. At runtime, if |m| or the inclination angle departs from the calibrated values, you are in a disturbed field: reduce trust in the mag rather than steering into it.

Raw X spans −12 … +68 µT, Y spans −55 … +31 µT, Z spans −48 … +40 µT. Offsets: X = 28, Y = −12, Z = −4 µT. Radii: 40, 43, 44 µT → r̄ = 42.3 µT → scales 1.058, 0.984, 0.962. Complementary filter for tilt: derivation, α, and the bias trap

ADVANCED▶

The accelerometer gives a tilt that is correct on average but noisy and fooled by movement. The gyro gives a tilt change that is smooth and fast but drifts. A complementary filter takes the slow part of the first and the fast part of the second and adds them. It's three lines of code and the first answer to "how do you fuse an IMU?".

1. DEFINITIONS

θacc
Tilt computed from the accelerometer with atan2 (card 14).
ω
Gyro rate about the same axis.
Low-pass / high-pass filter
Lets slow changes through / lets fast changes through.
Complementary
The two filters add up to exactly 1, so a true, noise-free angle passes through unchanged.
α (alpha)
The weight given to the gyro path each step (close to 1, e.g. 0.98).
τ (tau), crossover
The time constant splitting "slow" from "fast". Changes slower than 1/(2πτ) Hz come from the accel; faster ones from the gyro.
θk = α·(θk−1 + ωk·Δt) + (1 − α)·θacc,k,    α = τ/(τ + Δt)
equivalently: θ̂(s) = θacc(s)·1/(τs + 1) + (ω/s)·τs/(τs + 1)

2. WHAT HAPPENS EACH SAMPLE

  1. Predict with the gyro: θpred = θk−1 + ω·Δt. This follows fast motion.
  2. Measure with the accel: θacc = atan2(fx, fz).
  3. Blend: move θpred a small fraction (1 − α) toward θacc. This slowly pulls out gyro drift.
  4. Gate (optional): if | |f| − 1 g | is large, the device is accelerating; skip step 3 (α = 1).
  1. Δt = 10 ms, choose τ = 0.5 s → α = 0.5/0.51 = 0.980. Crossover = 1/(2π × 0.5) = 0.32 Hz.
  2. One step: θk−1 = 10.00°, ω = 20 dps, θacc = 12.0°. Predict: 10.00 + 20 × 0.01 = 10.20°. Blend: 0.98 × 10.20 + 0.02 × 12.0 = 9.996 + 0.240 = 10.236°.
  3. The bias trap: a constant gyro bias b gives a steady-state error of b·τ. With b = 2 dps and τ = 0.5 s: 1.0°. If you raise τ to 5 s to reject more linear acceleration, the error becomes 10°. Derivation: at steady state θ doesn't change, so α·b·Δt = (1 − α)·error → error = b·Δt·α/(1 − α) = b·τ.
  4. So remove gyro bias first (card 22), or use a filter that estimates it (Kalman with a bias state, card 24; Mahony's integral term).

Kalman filter: 1-D, angle + bias, and the attitude EKF / Madgwick / Mahony

ADVANCED▶

A Kalman filter is a complementary filter that works out its own blending weight from how noisy each source is, and can estimate hidden quantities (like gyro bias) that no sensor measures directly. It keeps two things: a best guess and how uncertain that guess is.

1. DEFINITIONS

State x
What we estimate: here the angle θ (and in the 2-state version, gyro bias b).
Covariance P
How uncertain the estimate is (a variance: degrees²). Grows when we predict, shrinks when we measure.
Q — process noise
How much uncertainty the prediction step adds per step (or per second): how much we distrust the gyro/model.
R — measurement noise
The variance of the measurement: how much we distrust the accelerometer angle.
K — Kalman gain
0…1: how far to move toward the measurement. Computed, not chosen.
Innovation
z − predicted measurement: the surprise.

2. SCALAR KALMAN STEP BY STEP

  1. Predict state: x⁻ = x + ω·Δt.
  2. Predict uncertainty: P⁻ = P + Q. (We're less sure after extrapolating.)
  3. Gain: K = P⁻/(P⁻ + R). If P⁻ ≫ R, K → 1 (trust the measurement); if P⁻ ≪ R, K → 0 (trust the prediction).
  4. Update state: x = x⁻ + K·(z − x⁻).
  5. Update uncertainty: P = (1 − K)·P⁻.
  1. Q = 0.01 deg² per step, R = 4 deg² (accel angle σ = 2°). Start P = 100 (we know nothing).
  2. Step 1: P⁻ = 100.01 → K = 100.01/104.01 = 0.96: jump almost to the accel reading.
  3. Step 2: P = 0.04 × 100.01 = 3.85 → P⁻ = 3.86 → K = 3.86/7.86 = 0.49.
  4. It converges to steady state: P⁻ = (Q + √(Q² + 4QR))/2 = (0.01 + √(0.0001 + 0.16))/2 = 0.205 → K∞ = 0.205/4.205 = 0.049.
  5. A constant K of 0.049 is just a complementary filter with α = 0.951. So in steady state, scalar Kalman = complementary filter. The value of Kalman is: the gain comes from the noise numbers, it adapts at start-up and when measurements are missing or inflated, and it can carry extra states.

3. ANGLE + GYRO BIAS (2-STATE)

x = [θ, b]ᵀ:   θk = θk−1 + (ω − b)Δt,   bk = bk−1;   z = θacc = [1 0]·x + v

F = [[1, −Δt], [0, 1]], H = [1, 0]. When the predicted angle keeps drifting away from the accel angle in the same direction, the filter attributes that consistent innovation to b and learns it. That removes the b·τ error of the complementary filter. You can watch the bias estimate converge in the Tilt Fusion Lab.

4. FULL 3-D ATTITUDE

  • EKF / error-state EKF (extended Kalman filter: linearised around the current estimate): state = quaternion + gyro bias. Predict with gyro; update with accel (gravity direction) and mag (heading). Adaptive R: inflate the accel R when |f| ≠ 1 g and the mag R when |m| or the inclination is off. Standard in phones, watches and drones.
  • Madgwick: gradient-descent correction of the quaternion toward the accel/mag reference with one gain β. Cheap, no covariance matrices.
  • Mahony: a complementary filter on rotations. The error is the cross product of measured and predicted gravity (and mag) directions, fed back into the gyro rate by a PI controller: Kp sets the crossover, the integral Ki learns gyro bias.
  • R too small: the filter follows accelerometer noise and linear acceleration (like α too low).
  • Q too small: the filter becomes overconfident and lags real motion; P collapses and it barely corrects.
  • Qb = 0: the bias estimate freezes after start-up and can't track temperature drift.
  • Using the wrong units in Q (per step vs per second): tuning breaks when the sample rate changes.
A Kalman filter alternates predict and update: predict the angle with the gyro and grow the uncertainty by Q, then compute the gain from the predicted uncertainty and the measurement noise R, and move toward the accelerometer angle by that gain. With constant noise it converges to a fixed gain, so it becomes a complementary filter, but the gain is derived rather than guessed, and it can carry extra states. The classic tilt version adds gyro bias as a second state, which the filter learns from consistent innovations, removing the steady error a complementary filter has. Full 3-D attitude uses a quaternion EKF, or the lighter Madgwick and Mahony filters.

Quaternions vs Euler angles, and gimbal lock

ADVANCED▶

To describe orientation in 3-D you need a representation. Euler angles (yaw, pitch, roll) are easy to read but break at certain orientations. Quaternions are four numbers that never break, which is why every serious fusion filter uses them internally.

1. DEFINITIONS

Euler angles
Three successive rotations about named axes, e.g. ZYX: yaw ψ about Z, then pitch θ about the new Y, then roll φ about the new X. The order matters.
Gimbal lock
At pitch = ±90° the yaw and roll axes line up, so two angles describe the same motion and one degree of freedom is lost.
DCM — direction cosine matrix
A 3×3 rotation matrix. No singularity, 9 numbers, needs re-orthonormalising.
Quaternion q
[w, x, y, z] = [cos(φ/2), u·sin(φ/2)] for a rotation by angle φ about unit axis u. Unit length. q and −q are the same rotation.
⊗ (quaternion product)
Composition of rotations, 16 multiplies.

2. INTEGRATING A GYRO INTO A QUATERNION STEP BY STEP

  1. Take the bias-corrected body rates ω = (ωx, ωy, ωz) in rad/s and the real Δt.
  2. The rotation in this step has angle |ω|Δt about axis ω/|ω|.
  3. Build Δq = [cos(|ω|Δt/2), (ω/|ω|)·sin(|ω|Δt/2)] (for tiny |ω| use [1, ωΔt/2] to avoid dividing by zero).
  4. q ← q ⊗ Δq (body-frame rates multiply on the right).
  5. Normalise q to unit length (rounding errors accumulate otherwise).
  6. Convert to Euler only for display.
  1. Rotate about Z at 90 dps for 1 s at 100 Hz. Per step: |ω|Δt = 1.5708 rad/s × 0.01 = 0.015708 rad.
  2. Δq = [cos(0.007854), 0, 0, sin(0.007854)] = [0.999969, 0, 0, 0.007854].
  3. After 100 steps: q = [cos 45°, 0, 0, sin 45°] = [0.7071, 0, 0, 0.7071]: a 90° yaw. ✔
  4. Gimbal lock: at pitch = 90°, the Euler rate equation contains ψ̇ = (ωy sin φ + ωz cos φ)/cos θ; cos 90° = 0, so yaw rate goes to infinity. The quaternion integration above has no division and works at every orientation.

Power-aware sampling: batching, wake-on-motion, step counting

ADVANCED▶

On a watch or wearable, sensing must run all day on a tiny battery. The sensor's own current is usually small; what drains the battery is waking the main processor too often and running expensive sensors (the gyro) when nothing is happening.

1. DEFINITIONS

Batching
Letting the sensor collect many samples in its FIFO and handing them over in one go.
Duty cycling
Turning a sensor on only part of the time.
Wake-on-motion (activity interrupt)
The accelerometer itself compares motion to a threshold and raises an interrupt only when something happens.
Sensor hub / always-on processor
A tiny low-power core (or the sensor's embedded logic: pedometer, tilt, significant-motion) that processes sensor data while the main processor sleeps.

2. WORKED POWER EXAMPLE

  1. MCU wake to handle one interrupt: 100 µs at 5 mA (plus wake-up energy) ≈ 0.5 µC per wake.
  2. Data-ready interrupt per sample at 50 Hz: 50 × 0.5 µC = 25 µA average.
  3. FIFO batching, watermark 100 samples: one wake per 2 s plus a longer read (say 1 ms at 5 mA = 5 µC) → 2.5 µA average. 10× less, same data.
  4. Gyro on continuously ≈ 450 µA; accel in low-power 50 Hz ≈ 20 µA. Gyro only during workouts or when motion is detected saves hundreds of µA, which dwarfs everything else.

3. WAKE-ON-MOTION STEP BY STEP

  1. Configure the accel at low ODR (12.5–25 Hz) in low-power mode.
  2. Set an activity threshold (e.g. 60–100 mg above the reference) and a duration (a few samples) so a single bump doesn't wake the system.
  3. The MCU sleeps. On the interrupt it wakes, raises ODR, maybe enables the gyro.
  4. An inactivity timer (e.g. 30 s below threshold) puts everything back to sleep.

4. STEP COUNTING STEP BY STEP

  1. Compute the magnitude |a| = √(ax² + ay² + az²), so wrist orientation doesn't matter.
  2. Band-pass ≈ 0.5–3 Hz: removes the 1 g DC (gravity) and high-frequency vibration. Walking is about 1.5–2.5 steps/s.
  3. Detect peaks above an adaptive threshold (e.g. a fraction of the recent peak-to-peak amplitude).
  4. Enforce a minimum interval of ~250–300 ms between steps (no human walks at 4+ steps/s for long).
  5. Regularity gate: start counting only after N (e.g. 6) consecutive steps with similar intervals, then add them retroactively. This rejects random arm movements and typing.
  • Per-sample interrupts at 50 Hz all day: the "sleeping" watch draws tens of µA more than designed; battery life misses spec by a day.
  • Fixed peak threshold: slow walkers aren't counted, while driving on a bumpy road counts steps.
  • No minimum interval: each step has two peaks (heel strike and push-off) and is counted twice.
  • Wake threshold too low: a vibrating desk or a car wakes the system constantly ("wake storm").
Power in sensing is dominated by processor wake-ups and by the gyro, not by the accelerometer. So I batch samples in the sensor FIFO with a watermark, which can cut wake-ups a hundredfold, keep only a low-power accelerometer running with a wake-on-motion threshold and an inactivity timer, and enable the gyro only when needed, or move the work to a sensor hub or the sensor's embedded functions. A basic step counter takes the acceleration magnitude, band-passes it around walking cadence, detects peaks above an adaptive threshold with a minimum interval, and only starts counting after several regular steps.

Data logging: ring buffers, flash logging, compression

ADVANCED▶

Logging sensor data means moving it from an interrupt into RAM, from RAM into flash, and keeping it compact and intact even if power fails halfway. Each hop has its own classic structure: a ring buffer, an append-only log, and delta encoding.

1. DEFINITIONS

Ring (circular) buffer
A fixed array with a write index (head) and a read index (tail) that wrap around.
SPSC
Single producer, single consumer: one context writes, one reads. Then no lock is needed if each index has exactly one writer.
NOR flash page / sector
Page = unit you can program (256 B on SPI NOR). Sector = smallest unit you can erase (4 KB on SPI NOR; 16–128 KB on STM32F4 internal flash). Programming only turns 1s into 0s; erase resets to 0xFF.
Endurance
How many erase cycles a sector survives (10k–100k).
Delta / zig-zag / varint
Store differences between samples; map signed to unsigned (0, −1, 1, −2 → 0, 1, 2, 3); use 7 bits per byte with a continuation bit so small numbers take one byte.

2. RING BUFFER STEP BY STEP

  1. Size N a power of two, so index wrap is i & (N − 1).
  2. Producer (ISR): if full, count a drop and return; else write the item, then a memory barrier, then advance head.
  3. Consumer (task): if head == tail, empty; else read the item, then advance tail.
  4. Use free-running 32-bit indices: count = head − tail works across wrap-around thanks to unsigned arithmetic.

3. FLASH LOG STEP BY STEP

  1. Accumulate records in a RAM page buffer; write whole pages.
  2. Each record: [length | sequence number | type | payload | CRC]. Append only; never rewrite.
  3. Pre-erase the next sector before you need it, so a write never waits for an erase (a 4 KB erase takes tens of ms).
  4. On boot, scan for the last record with a valid CRC: everything before it is good, a torn record at the end is discarded.
  5. Rotate through sectors (wear levelling) and budget the endurance.
  1. Log 6-axis IMU at 50 Hz, 12 B/sample = 600 B/s = 51.8 MB/day. Too much for an 8 MB SPI flash.
  2. Delta + zig-zag + varint: still-ish wrist motion gives deltas mostly within ±63 → 1 byte instead of 2 → about 2× smaller. Timestamps at a steady rate: delta-of-delta ≈ 0 → nearly free.
  3. Endurance: 8 MB = 2048 sectors of 4 KB; at 26 MB/day you erase 6350 sectors/day → each sector ~3 times/day → 100k cycles last ~90 years. At 64 KB of total log space it would be ~400 cycles/day → 250 days. Do the maths.
  4. Encoding example: samples 1000, 1003, 1001 → deltas 1000, +3, −2 → zig-zag 2000, 6, 3 → varint bytes D0 0F, 06, 03 (4 bytes instead of 6).
  • No barrier / both sides writing the same index: the consumer reads an item before it's written; occasional corrupted samples.
  • Overwriting a log record in place: NOR can't turn 0s into 1s without erasing; the "update" silently produces garbage.
  • No CRC per record: after a brown-out mid-write you can't tell where valid data ends.
  • Erasing synchronously in the sampling path: tens of ms of blocking; the ring buffer overflows and samples are dropped.
I move samples from the ISR to a logger task through a power-of-two single-producer single-consumer ring buffer, where each side owns one index, a barrier publishes data before the head moves, and drops are counted. In flash I write an append-only log of records with length, sequence number and CRC, writing whole pages, pre-erasing sectors ahead, and on boot I scan for the last valid record so a power cut loses at most one record. To save space I delta-encode, zig-zag and varint-pack the samples, which typically halves IMU data, and I check endurance against the write rate.

  5. In functional-safety terms (ISO 26262 / IEC 61508), detection plus reaction time must fit inside the fault-tolerant time interval.
  1. Accelerometer noise σ = 3 LSB. Probability that 20 consecutive samples are identical by chance is astronomically small. So 20 identical samples = stuck (e.g. the sensor reset into power-down and the registers keep the last value).
  2. Battery NTC at 100 ms sampling, physical max slope 1 °C/s → allowed change per sample 0.1 °C. Plus noise margin 0.5 °C → reject jumps > 0.6 °C, but accept after 5 consecutive consistent readings (real fast event or a reconnect).
  • Clamping instead of flagging: an open NTC is clamped to 125 °C and the charger stops forever, or clamped to −40 °C and the heater runs forever.
  • No freshness check: the driver stopped (latched interrupt never cleared), the last value stays in a global, and everything looks normal.
  • No debounce: a single EMI glitch triggers a safety shutdown in the field.
I check every sample for range limits like open or short codes and clipping, stuck-at values, using the fact that real sensors always show some noise, rate of change against what's physically possible, physics cross-checks like 1 g at rest, freshness against the expected sample period, and self-test at boot. Faults are debounced, the value travels with a status and timestamp instead of a magic number, and there's a defined reaction: mark invalid, substitute or degrade, and log a diagnostic, within the fault-tolerant time interval if it's safety-related.

  2. Accelerometer: specific force in the world is (ax, az + g). Rotating it into the device frame gives fx = cos θ·ax + sin θ·(az + g), fz = −sin θ·ax + cos θ·(az + g). Gaussian noise σ (mg) is added to each axis.

  4. Accel-only: θacc = atan2(fx, fz). Gyro-only: θg += ω·Δt, starting from the true angle.
  5. Complementary: θc = α(θc + ωΔt) + (1 − α)θacc; time constant τ = αΔt/(1 − α).

  7. RMS error = √(mean of (estimate − truth)²) over the last 10 s of samples.

(2) "Static" with bias 3 dps: complementary sits at a constant offset of b·τ; switch on "Kalman estimates bias" and watch the green bias line converge.
