01What problem does CAN solve?

A modern car contains somewhere between 30 and 100 small computers. Each one is called an ECU (Electronic Control Unit): the engine ECU, the ABS (anti-lock braking) ECU, the airbag ECU, the instrument cluster, the door modules, and so on. These computers constantly need information from each other. The cluster needs the engine speed to move the rev counter needle. The ABS needs the engine torque. The airbag ECU needs the vehicle speed.

The naive solution: one wire per conversation

Imagine connecting every ECU directly to every other ECU. With n ECUs, the number of point-to-point links is n × (n − 1) / 2.

  • 10 ECUs → 10 × 9 / 2 = 45 links.
  • 50 ECUs → 50 × 49 / 2 = 1 225 links. Each link needs at least two wires plus connector pins. That is kilograms of copper, hundreds of connector pins that can corrode, and a harness that is impossible to build.

The CAN solution: one shared pair of wires

CAN stands for Controller Area Network. Bosch invented it in 1986 for exactly this problem. Every ECU connects to the same two twisted wires, called the bus. Any ECU can talk; every ECU hears everything. Adding an ECU means adding one short branch, not dozens of new wires.

A shared bus immediately creates three hard questions, and CAN's whole design is the answer to them:

  1. Who gets to talk when two ECUs want to send at the same instant? CAN answers with arbitration: a clever bit-by-bit contest in which the most important message wins and loses nothing (section 5 walks through it with real numbers).
  2. How do we stay reliable in an electrically noisy car (ignition sparks, motors, 12 V load dumps)? CAN answers with a differential physical layer (two wires carrying opposite signals) and five independent error checks on every message.
  3. What if one ECU breaks and starts sending garbage? CAN answers with fault confinement: each ECU counts its own errors and switches itself off the bus if it is clearly the broken one.

Where you meet CAN

Cars and trucks (powertrain, chassis, body), buses and tractors (SAE J1939), industrial machines and robots (CANopen), medical devices, e-bikes, battery packs (the BMS — battery management system — talks to the charger over CAN), elevators, ships and satellites. Classic CAN runs at up to 1 Mbit/s with 8 data bytes per message; its newer version CAN FD (Flexible Data rate) carries 64 bytes and runs the data part at 2–8 Mbit/s.

CAN is a two-wire shared bus that lets dozens of ECUs exchange short messages in real time. It solves the three problems a shared bus creates: who may talk (priority-based arbitration that loses no time), noise (differential signalling plus five error checks), and broken nodes (each node counts its errors and removes itself if it is the faulty one).

02The physical picture: what is inside one ECU and on the wire

Before any protocol detail, picture the hardware. Every CAN node (node = any device on the bus) has the same three-block chain:

  1. The MCU and your firmware. MCU = microcontroller unit, the chip that runs your C code. Your code never wiggles the CAN wires itself. It writes an identifier and up to 8 data bytes into registers (memory-mapped hardware variables) and sets a "please send" bit.
  2. The CAN controller is a digital hardware block, usually built into the MCU (on STM32F4 it is called bxCAN, "basic extended CAN"; on STM32G4/H7 it is FDCAN). It turns your bytes into a correctly formatted stream of bits, adds the checksum, takes part in arbitration, checks every bit, and counts errors. It talks to the outside world through two logic pins: TXD (transmit data, output) and RXD (receive data, input), at normal 3.3 V or 5 V logic levels.
  3. The transceiver is a small separate analog chip (for example NXP TJA1051 or TI TCAN1042, about 8 pins). It converts the logic level on TXD into the two bus voltages on CANH (CAN High) and CANL (CAN Low), and converts the bus state back into a logic level on RXD. It also protects the MCU from the harsh car environment (it survives a bus wire shorted to the 12 V or even 24 V battery).
  4. The bus is one twisted pair of wires running past all ECUs. Each ECU connects with a short branch called a stub. A 120 Ω termination resistor sits across CANH–CANL at each of the two physical ends of the cable.

One crucial detail: the controller reads RXD while it transmits on TXD. RXD always reflects what is really on the bus, including the node's own bits. Every clever CAN trick — knowing you lost arbitration, knowing someone acknowledged you, detecting a bit error — is the controller comparing "what I sent" with "what I read back".

Each CAN node is MCU → CAN controller → transceiver → twisted pair. The controller is digital and handles the protocol; the transceiver is analog and converts TXD/RXD logic levels into the differential CANH/CANL voltages. The controller listens to its own bits on RXD while sending, which is how it detects arbitration loss, ACKs and errors.

03Dominant and recessive: the one idea everything else depends on

Ordinary logic signals have two states that are both actively driven: the pin pushes to 3.3 V for a 1 and pulls to 0 V for a 0. CAN is different. Its two bus states are not symmetric:

Dominant0Actively drives: pushes CANH up and pulls CANL down≈ 3.5 V≈ 1.5 V≈ 2 V

Now ask: what happens if ECU 1 drives dominant while ECU 2 lets go (recessive) at the same instant? ECU 2 is not pushing anything, so it cannot resist. The bus shows dominant. The only way the bus is recessive is if every node is recessive.

1 (recessive)1 (recessive)1Nobody drives; resistors pull the wires together

That table is exactly the truth table of a logical AND gate: the bus is 1 only if all inputs are 1. Engineers call this a wired-AND bus. Remember: 0 always wins.

Think of a rope held loosely by many people. "Recessive" means you are holding it loosely; "dominant" means you are pulling it down. If even one person pulls it down, the rope goes down, no matter how many people are holding it loosely. Nobody can "pull it up" — it only rises when everyone lets go.

Why two wires instead of one?

Noise in a car (for example a spark from the ignition) couples into nearby wires. Because CANH and CANL are twisted tightly together, the noise adds almost the same voltage to both wires. The receiver looks only at the difference CANH − CANL, so the noise cancels out. Example: a 1 V spike raises CANH from 3.5 to 4.5 V and CANL from 1.5 to 2.5 V. The difference is still 2.0 V, so the receiver still sees dominant.

Dominant is logic 0 and is actively driven; recessive is logic 1 and is just "released", with the termination pulling both wires together. A driven state always overpowers a released one, so the bus is a wired-AND where 0 wins. Arbitration, the ACK bit and error flags all rely on that one property.

04Key terms, defined one by one

Every word below appears all over this page and in interviews. Read each once; come back here whenever a word feels fuzzy.

Bit rate (bit/s) and bit time
How many bits per second the bus carries. At 500 kbit/s (500 000 bits per second) one bit lasts 1 / 500 000 = 2 µs; that duration is the bit time. Every node on one bus must use the same bit rate.
Frame
One complete CAN message on the wire: a fixed sequence of fields (start bit, identifier, length, data, checksum, acknowledgement, end). Classic CAN frames carry 0–8 data bytes.
Identifier (ID)
An 11-bit number (0x000–0x7FF, "standard" format) or 29-bit number ("extended" format) at the start of the frame. It names the content ("engine speed"), not the sender or receiver, and it is also the priority: the smaller the number, the more important.
Arbitration
The bit-by-bit contest that decides who may transmit when several nodes start at once. Every node sends its ID; whoever sent a 1 but reads back a 0 has lost and stops. The lowest ID wins.
SOF — Start Of Frame
The first bit of every frame, always dominant (0). Its falling edge (recessive → dominant) tells every node "a frame starts now" and lines up all their clocks.
RTR — Remote Transmission Request
The bit after the 11-bit ID. 0 = this is a normal data frame; 1 = a remote frame asking someone else to send this ID's data (rarely used today).
IDE — IDentifier Extension
0 = standard 11-bit ID, 1 = extended 29-bit ID follows.
DLC — Data Length Code
A 4-bit field saying how many data bytes follow (0–8 in classic CAN).
CRC — Cyclic Redundancy Check
A 15-bit checksum the transmitter computes over the frame. Each receiver recomputes it; a mismatch means the frame was corrupted.
ACK — Acknowledge slot
One bit near the end. The transmitter sends it recessive (1). Every receiver that got the frame correctly overwrites it with dominant (0). If the transmitter reads back 1, nobody received it.
EOF — End Of Frame, and IFS — InterFrame Space
EOF is 7 recessive bits closing the frame. IFS (also "intermission") is 3 more recessive bits before the next frame may start.
Bit stuffing
After 5 identical bits in a row, the transmitter inserts one opposite bit (a stuff bit); receivers delete it. This guarantees regular edges for clock synchronisation and makes 6 identical bits an unmistakable error signal.
Time quantum (tq)
The tiny time unit the controller divides each bit into, derived from its clock: tq = BRP / fCAN, where BRP (Baud Rate Prescaler) is a divider you program and fCAN is the controller's input clock. Example: 42 MHz clock, BRP = 6 → tq = 6 / 42 MHz = 142.9 ns.
Sample point (SP)
The instant inside each bit when the controller reads the bus value, given as a percentage of the bit time (typically 75–87.5 %). Sampling late gives the signal time to settle.
Error frame
When any node detects a problem, it deliberately sends 6 dominant bits (an error flag), which breaks the stuffing rule so every other node also notices. The damaged frame is thrown away by everyone and retransmitted automatically.
TEC / REC — Transmit / Receive Error Counter
Two counters inside every controller. Errors raise them (+8 per transmit error, +1 per receive error); successes lower them (−1).
Error active / error passive / bus-off
The three health states derived from TEC and REC. Error active (both ≤ 127): normal. Error passive (either > 127): still talks, but may no longer destroy other frames. Bus-off (TEC > 255): the node disconnects itself from the bus.
Acceptance filter
Hardware inside the controller that decides which IDs are stored for the CPU and which are ignored, so the CPU is not interrupted by irrelevant traffic.
Mailbox / RX FIFO
Small hardware buffers. A TX mailbox holds one frame waiting to be sent (bxCAN has 3). An RX FIFO (First-In First-Out queue) holds received frames until the CPU reads them (bxCAN: 2 FIFOs × 3 frames).
Termination
The 120 Ω resistor at each end of the bus. It stops signal reflections and pulls the bus back to recessive.
CAN FD, BRS
CAN with Flexible Data rate: up to 64 data bytes, and a BRS (Bit Rate Switch) bit after which the data part is sent faster (e.g. 2 Mbit/s instead of 500 kbit/s).
DBC file
A text "database" describing which signals (engine speed, temperature…) sit at which bit positions of which frame, with scaling. Tools and code generators read it.
ISO-TP and UDS
ISO-TP (ISO 15765-2, Transport Protocol) splits messages longer than 8 bytes into several frames. UDS (Unified Diagnostic Services, ISO 14229) is the diagnostics language that runs on top: read error codes, read the VIN, reprogram the ECU.
The ID is both the name and the priority of a message; the lowest ID wins arbitration. A frame is SOF, ID, RTR/IDE/DLC control bits, up to 8 data bytes, a CRC, an ACK slot and an EOF. Bit stuffing keeps clocks aligned, TEC/REC count errors, and those counters decide whether a node is error active, error passive or bus-off.

05One complete worked example: two ECUs send at the same moment

Setup: a 500 kbit/s bus (bit time 2 µs) with three ECUs. Exactly when the previous frame's intermission ends, two of them want to send:

ECUMessageIDSignal and scalingValue now
Engine ECU (node A)EngineSpeed0x12316 bits, 0.125 rpm per bit, little-endian1500 rpm
Brake ECU (node B)BrakePressure0x0A016 bits, 0.1 bar per bit, little-endian50.0 bar
Cluster (node C)Only receives. Its filters accept both IDs.

Step 1 — Firmware turns physical values into bytes

A CAN frame carries integers, not floating-point values. The DBC scaling rule is raw = (physical − offset) / factor.

EngineSpeed:   raw = 1500 rpm / 0.125 = 12000 = 0x2EE0
               little-endian (Intel): byte0 = 0xE0 (low byte), byte1 = 0x2E   → data E0 2E
BrakePressure: raw = 50.0 bar / 0.1   =   500 = 0x01F4
               little-endian:          byte0 = 0xF4, byte1 = 0x01             → data F4 01

Each ECU writes its ID, DLC = 2 and these two bytes into a TX mailbox and sets the TXRQ ("transmit request") bit. From here on, hardware does everything.

Step 2 — Both controllers wait for bus idle, then start together

A controller may only start a frame when the bus has been recessive for the intermission (3 bits after the 7-bit EOF). Both A and B see the same idle bus, so both send SOF (a dominant 0) in the same bit. The recessive→dominant edge of SOF hard-synchronises every node: A, B and C all restart their bit clocks on that edge, so from now on they all agree when each bit begins.

Step 3 — Arbitration, bit by bit

Both nodes now send their 11-bit ID, most significant bit (MSB) first. Write the IDs in binary:

               ID10 ID9 ID8 ID7 ID6 ID5 ID4 ID3 ID2 ID1 ID0
0x0A0 (brake)    0    0   0   1   0   1   0   0   0   0   0
0x123 (engine)   0    0   1   0   0   1   0   0   0   1   1
  1. t = 0, SOF: both send 0. Bus = 0. Both read 0 = what they sent. Continue.
  2. t = 1, ID10: both send 0, bus 0. Continue.
  3. t = 2, ID9: both send 0, bus 0. Continue.
  4. t = 3, ID8: brake sends 0 (dominant, driven), engine sends 1 (recessive, released). Wired-AND: bus = 0. At the sample point the engine ECU reads 0 although it sent 1. Rule: sent recessive, read dominant during arbitration = I lost. The engine controller stops driving in this same bit and becomes a receiver.
  5. t = 4 onwards: only the brake ECU transmits. It never noticed the contest: every bit it read back matched what it sent. Its frame on the wire is bit-for-bit identical to what it would have been with no competitor. That is why CAN arbitration is called non-destructive: no time and no bits were wasted.

Why did the lower number win? Because 0 is dominant and IDs go out MSB first, the first bit position where two IDs differ decides it, and the ID with a 0 there is numerically smaller. So lower ID = higher priority, automatically, from the wiring.

Count without stuffing: 1 + 11 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 4 + 16 + 15 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 7 = 60 frame bits (the general formula is 44 + 8 × n data bytes = 44 + 16 = 60), plus 3 IFS = 63.

Stuffing: the transmitter watches for 5 identical bits in a row (from SOF to the end of the CRC) and inserts an opposite bit. In this frame:

  • ID4…ID0 are 00000 → five zeros → a stuff 1 is inserted after ID0.
  • The stuff bit itself starts a new run. Then RTR, IDE, r0, DLC3, DLC2 = 00000 → five zeros → another stuff 1.
  • Two more runs of five occur later, in the data and CRC. Total: 4 stuff bits.

So the brake frame occupies 63 + 4 = 67 bit times = 67 × 2 µs = 134 µs on the bus. (Enter node A = 123 data E0 2E and node B = 0A0 data F4 01 in the Arbitration Sim tab and you will see exactly these numbers, including CRC 0x3DF5.)

Step 5 — Receivers check and acknowledge

  1. The cluster (C) and the engine ECU (A, now a receiver) both remove the stuff bits, recompute the CRC over the bits they received, and compare it with the 15 CRC bits on the wire. They match (0x3DF5).
  2. Because the frame is correct so far, both drive the ACK slot dominant. The brake ECU, which sent that bit recessive, reads back 0 and knows that at least one node received the frame correctly.
  3. After 7 clean EOF bits the frame is valid for everybody. The brake ECU's TEC (transmit error counter) is decreased by 1 (if above 0).
  4. In the cluster, the acceptance filter matches 0x0A0, the controller stores the frame in RX FIFO 0 and raises an interrupt. The ISR (interrupt service routine) copies it out; a task decodes F4 01 → raw 0x01F4 = 500 → 500 × 0.1 = 50.0 bar.

Step 6 — The loser tries again, automatically

The engine ECU's mailbox still holds its frame. After the brake frame's IFS, the engine controller starts again without any software involvement, wins (nobody else is waiting), and sends 0x123 with E0 2E. That frame has 3 stuff bits → 63 + 3 = 66 bit times = 132 µs.

t =   0 µs  both start SOF
t =   6 µs  engine loses at ID8 (bit 3 × 2 µs = start of 4th bit)
t = 134 µs  brake frame + IFS done   → engine starts its own SOF
t = 266 µs  engine frame + IFS done  (134 + 132)
Worst-case extra delay the engine saw: one brake frame = 134 µs.
On an oscilloscope across CANH–CANL you would see one continuous burst of 134 µs (the brake frame), 6 µs of recessive intermission, then the engine frame. You would never see a "collision" glitch at t = 6 µs — the bus simply continued with the brake ECU's bits.
When two nodes start together they both send their ID MSB first. At the first differing bit, the node sending recessive reads dominant, stops immediately and becomes a receiver, so the lower ID wins without corrupting anything. The winner's frame continues with stuffing, CRC, an ACK from the receivers and EOF; the loser's controller retries automatically after the intermission.

06Bit timing in one picture: how a node knows where a bit is

CAN has no clock wire. Each node has its own oscillator and must work out by itself where each 2 µs bit begins and when to look at it. The controller does this by chopping every bit into small equal slices called time quanta (tq) and grouping them into four segments:

SYNC 1
PROP 5
PHASE1 6
PS2 2
0▲ SP 12/1414 tq = 2 µs
  1. SYNC_SEG (1 tq): where an edge is expected to arrive if everyone's clocks agree.
  2. PROP_SEG: waiting time that covers the signal travelling to the far end of the cable and back (needed so that during arbitration every node sees every other node's bit before sampling).
  3. PHASE_SEG1 and PHASE_SEG2: safety margins on each side of the sample point. When a node sees an edge arrive a little late or early, it stretches PHASE_SEG1 or shrinks PHASE_SEG2 by up to SJW (Synchronisation Jump Width) tq to re-align. This is resynchronisation.
  4. Sample point: at the end of PHASE_SEG1 the controller reads the bus. Here: (1 + 5 + 6) / 14 = 12 / 14 = 85.7 % of the bit.

If one number is wrong — say the APB1 clock is really 21 MHz because someone changed a clock divider — the same register values give 21 MHz / (6 × 14) = 250 kbit/s. That node now speaks at half speed and every frame it sends is garbage to the others (Scenario 1 is exactly this bug).

Each bit is divided into time quanta: tq = BRP / f_CAN, bit time = (1 + TSEG1 + TSEG2) tq. The node samples at the end of TSEG1, typically around 80–87.5 %, and re-aligns its clock on recessive-to-dominant edges by up to SJW quanta. At 42 MHz, BRP 6 with 1 + 11 + 2 = 14 tq gives exactly 500 kbit/s at 85.7 %.

07When things go wrong: errors and self-removal in one story

Suppose a connector on the engine ECU is corroded, so that ECU's transmitted bits sometimes flip on the way out. Follow what happens:

  1. Detection. The engine ECU sends a 1 but reads back a 0 in the middle of its data field. Outside arbitration this is a bit error. (Other checks catch other problems: a receiver whose CRC does not match raises a CRC error; six identical bits in a row is a stuff error; a 0 where the format requires a 1 is a form error; no ACK is an ACK error.)
  2. Signalling. In the very next bit the engine ECU sends 6 dominant bits, the active error flag. Six identical bits violate the stuffing rule, so every other node now also detects an error and adds its own flag. The frame is destroyed for everybody: either all nodes accept a frame, or none do.
  3. Counting. The engine ECU's TEC goes up by 8. Receivers' REC goes up by 1.
  4. Retry. After an 8-bit error delimiter and the intermission, the engine ECU automatically retransmits.
  5. Healing or removal. Every successful transmission subtracts 1 from TEC. A healthy node with an occasional EMC hit recovers. A node that fails more than about 1 in 8 frames climbs: at TEC > 127 it becomes error passive (its error flags become 6 recessive bits, which cannot hurt anybody else's frame); at TEC > 255 it goes bus-off and stops transmitting completely. It may rejoin only after seeing 128 × 11 recessive bits (≈ 2.8 ms on an idle 500 kbit/s bus).
Bad engine ECU, every transmission fails:
attempt   1   TEC =   8        error active
attempt  16   TEC = 128  →     ERROR PASSIVE
attempt  32   TEC = 256  →     BUS-OFF  (256 > 255)
at 500 kbit/s each failed attempt ≈ 60–80 bit times ≈ 0.15 ms → bus-off after ≈ 5 ms
Any node that detects one of the five errors (bit, stuff, CRC, form, ACK) sends a 6-bit error flag that destroys the frame for everyone, and the transmitter retries. Errors raise the counters by 8 (transmit) or 1 (receive) and successes lower them by 1, so a genuinely faulty node climbs to error passive above 127 and bus-off above 255, removing itself instead of jamming the network.

Controller Area Network, from the wire to UDS flashing

This is the topic automotive interviewers (Bosch, Continental, NXP, Infineon, ZF, Aptiv) go deepest on, and the one big-tech hardware teams use to test protocol reasoning. The cards run beginner → advanced. Register-level examples use the STM32F4 bxCAN (APB1 = 42 MHz at 168 MHz SYSCLK) and the STM32G4 FDCAN (Bosch M_CAN IP). Other controllers are named where they behave differently.

1. What is it?

CAN (Controller Area Network) is a way for many small computers to share one pair of wires and send each other short messages. Bosch created it in 1986 for cars; it is standardised as ISO 11898-1 (the data-link layer: how frames, arbitration and errors work) and ISO 11898-2 (the high-speed physical layer: voltages and transceivers).

Three words describe it precisely, and each needs a definition:

  • Multi-master: there is no boss node that grants permission. Any node may start transmitting when the bus is idle.
  • Broadcast: every node receives every frame. There is no "send to ECU 7".
  • Serial: bits are sent one after another on a single differential signal.

Most importantly, frames carry no source or destination address. The identifier names the content ("engine speed"), and each receiver decides with its hardware filters whether it cares about that content. This is called content-based addressing.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. A node's firmware puts an identifier and 0–8 data bytes into a transmit buffer in its CAN controller.
  2. The controller waits until the bus has been idle (recessive) for the intermission, then starts the frame with a dominant Start-Of-Frame bit.
  3. If other nodes start in the same bit, arbitration on the identifier decides who continues; the others become receivers and retry later, automatically.
  4. The winner sends control bits, data and a CRC checksum. All other nodes receive, check the CRC and acknowledge in the ACK slot.
  5. If any node detects any error, it destroys the frame for everyone with an error flag, and the transmitter retransmits.
  6. Each node counts its own errors (TEC/REC). A node that keeps failing removes itself (bus-off).
THE FIVE PROPERTIES INTERVIEWERS WANT TO HEAR
  • Priority-based, non-destructive arbitration. If several nodes start at once, the lowest identifier wins without a single bit of the winner being lost. Because the delay any message can suffer is bounded and calculable (Tindell/Davis response-time analysis), CAN suits hard real-time control.
  • Wired-AND bus. A dominant bit (logic 0) overrides a recessive bit (logic 1). Arbitration, ACK and error signalling all depend on this.
  • Strong error detection. Five independent mechanisms (bit, stuff, CRC, form, ACK). Bosch quotes a residual (undetected) error probability below 4.7 × 10⁻¹¹ × message error rate: if 1 frame in 1 000 gets corrupted, only about 1 in 2 × 10¹³ frames is corrupted and slips through.
  • Fault confinement. Each node counts its own errors with TEC (Transmit Error Counter) and REC (Receive Error Counter) and takes itself off the bus (bus-off) if it is the faulty one, so one broken ECU cannot jam the bus forever.
  • Cheap, robust physical layer. Two wires, differential, twisted pair, good rejection of common-mode noise. Up to 1 Mbit/s for classic CAN; CAN FD runs the payload at 2–8 Mbit/s.

5. What goes wrong if… you treat CAN like an addressed network

"CAN nodes have addresses." No. Classic CAN identifies messages. Addressing appears only in higher layers (J1939 source address, CANopen node ID, UDS request/response ID pairs), and it is built out of the identifier.
  • Two ECUs given the same ID because someone thought "ID = ECU address": both win arbitration at the same time, then collide in the data field → bit errors → error frames → both retry and collide again. Symptom: bursts of error frames whenever both happen to send together, TEC climbing on both ECUs.
  • IDs assigned by "importance of the ECU" instead of urgency of the message: a slow diagnostic status from the engine ECU (ID 0x010) blocks a time-critical brake request from a "less important" ECU (0x300). Symptom: the brake frame's latency jitters by a frame time under load.

6. Say it like this

CAN is an ISO 11898 multi-master broadcast bus: any node may transmit when the bus is idle, every node receives every frame, and the identifier names the content and sets its priority rather than addressing a node. Its key properties are non-destructive bitwise arbitration on a wired-AND bus, five error-detection mechanisms, fault confinement with TEC/REC and bus-off, and a cheap, noise-robust differential two-wire physical layer.

1. What is it?

The physical layer is "what the electrons do": which voltages on which wires mean 0 and 1. High-speed CAN (ISO 11898-2) uses two wires, CANH (CAN High) and CANL (CAN Low), twisted together. Together they form a differential pair: information is carried by the difference between the two voltages, not by either voltage alone.

The two logic states are deliberately unequal. For dominant (logic 0) the transceiver actively drives the pair apart. For recessive (logic 1) it lets go, and the termination resistors pull both lines together. A driven state always overpowers an undriven one, so the bus behaves as a wired-AND of all transmitters.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. The CAN controller sets its TXD pin low to send a 0, high to send a 1.
  2. TXD low: the transceiver turns on two output transistors. One pulls CANH up towards about 3.5 V, the other pulls CANL down towards about 1.5 V. Into the 60 Ω load formed by the two terminators, the difference is about 2 V.
  3. TXD high: both transistors switch off. Nothing drives the wires. Each transceiver's internal bias network holds both lines near 2.5 V (half of the 5 V supply), and the termination resistors discharge any difference. Vdiff falls to about 0 V.
  4. Every transceiver's receiver comparator measures Vdiff = CANH − CANL. Above about 0.9 V it reports dominant (RXD = 0); below about 0.5 V it reports recessive (RXD = 1). The gap between 0.5 and 0.9 V is a guard band against noise.
  5. Each controller samples RXD at its sample point. Because every node sees the same wire, every node reads the same value, including its own transmitted bits.

5. What goes wrong if…

"Dominant is high voltage, so it is logic 1." CANH does go up, but CANL goes down, and the logic value of a dominant bit is 0. Say it as: dominant = 0 = driven, recessive = 1 = released.
  • CANH and CANL swapped at one connector: that node's dominant drives a negative Vdiff (≈ −2 V), which all receivers read as recessive.   • Transceiver left in standby (STB pin high): TXD has no effect. Observe: on a scope the bus stays at 2.5 V/2.5 V when the node transmits; the controller logs bit errors (LEC = "bit dominant", sent 0 read 1).

6. Say it like this

High-speed CAN is a differential pair. A dominant bit is logic 0: the transceiver drives CANH to about 3.5 V and CANL to about 1.5 V, giving 2 V difference. A recessive bit is logic 1: the transceiver releases both lines and the termination pulls them together at about 2.5 V. Because driven beats released, the bus is a wired-AND, and because receivers look only at the difference, common-mode noise and ground shifts cancel.

1. What is it?

Topology is the shape of the wiring. The textbook CAN topology is a linear bus: one main cable (the trunk) runs from one end of the network to the other, and each ECU joins it through a short branch called a stub. A termination resistor of 120 Ω sits across CANH and CANL at each of the two physical ends of the trunk.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. A cable is a transmission line. At CAN speeds the wire is long compared with how far a signal edge travels during its rise time, so the edge behaves like a wave travelling at about 5 ns per metre (about two-thirds of the speed of light). The cable has a characteristic impedance Z₀, the ratio of voltage to current of that travelling wave: about 120 Ω for typical CAN twisted pair.
  2. At an open end the wave reflects. When the wave reaches an end with nothing (or the wrong impedance) attached, some or all of its energy bounces back, adds to the next part of the signal, and produces ringing: the voltage overshoots and oscillates around the final level.
  3. A resistor equal to Z₀ absorbs the wave. Putting 120 Ω at the end makes the line look infinitely long to the wave: no reflection. Because each end of a line is a reflection point, both ends need one.
  4. The resistors also create recessive. When all transceivers release the bus, the only thing pulling CANH and CANL back together is the termination. Without it the bus capacitance discharges slowly through the transceivers' high-ohm inputs.
  5. Stubs are short unterminated lines. A wave entering a stub reflects from the ECU at its end. If the stub is short, that reflection returns before it matters; if it is long, it rings around the sample point.
  6. The round trip limits length. During arbitration a node must see the far node's bit before it samples, so cable delay × 2 must fit inside the early part of the bit.

5. What goes wrong if…

"Put the 120 Ω on every node so any node can be at the end." Four nodes with 120 Ω gives 120 / 4 = 30 Ω, overloads the drivers (≈ 67 mA for 2 V), shrinks the dominant level towards the 0.9 V threshold and breaks margins. Terminate exactly the two physical ends.
  • One terminator missing: observe 120 Ω on the meter, a dominant Vdiff near 3 V (the driver now sees only 120 Ω), ringing after every dominant→recessive edge. Works on a short bench harness, fails on the vehicle harness or at higher bit rates (see Scenario 3).
  • No terminator: recessive edges decay slowly as an RC curve; receivers see dominant for too long, and you get bit, stuff and form errors everywhere.
  • 2 m stub at 1 Mbit/s: the reflection from the stub's end returns after 2 × 2 m × 5 ns/m = 20 ns plus ringing time; several reflections stack up and the eye closes near the sample point. Errors appear only on certain nodes or certain bit patterns.

6. Say it like this

CAN is a linear bus terminated with 120 Ω at both physical ends, matching the cable's characteristic impedance so edges do not reflect, and providing the pull-together that makes recessive. Powered off you measure 60 Ω; 120 means one is missing, 40 means one too many. Stubs must be short, and bus length is limited by the round-trip delay fitting before the sample point, roughly 40 m at 1 Mbit/s and 100 m at 500 kbit/s.

1. What is it?

A CAN interface is split into two chips (or blocks) with very different jobs. The controller is digital: it understands the protocol (frames, CRC, arbitration, errors). The transceiver (transmitter + receiver) is analog: it converts the controller's logic levels into bus voltages and back, and survives the electrical abuse of a vehicle. Interviewers ask this to check that you know which problems are firmware/protocol problems and which are hardware problems.

2. How does it work, step by step? (one bit's journey)

  1. The controller decides the next bit is 0 and drives TXD low at the start of the bit.
  2. The transceiver's driver switches on after its propagation delay (tens of ns) and pushes CANH/CANL apart.
  3. The voltage wave travels along the cable to all other nodes (≈ 5 ns/m).
  4. Every transceiver, including the sender's own, compares CANH − CANL with its threshold and drives its RXD pin.
  5. The sender's controller sees its own bit come back on RXD after the loop delay (TXD → bus → RXD, typically 100–250 ns), and at the sample point compares the read value with the value it sent.
  6. Match → continue. Mismatch → either lost arbitration (during the ID) or a bit error (anywhere else).
  • RXD loopback is essential. That self-monitoring is how the controller detects lost arbitration, receivers' ACK and bit errors.
  • TXD dominant timeout (typ. 0.3–12 ms): if a crashed MCU holds TXD low, the transceiver releases the bus so the rest of the network survives.

1. What is it?

A frame is one complete message on the bus: a fixed sequence of fields, each with a fixed job. Classic CAN has four frame types: data (carries 0–8 bytes), remote (asks for data; RTR = 1, no data field), error (signals that something went wrong) and overload (a rare "wait" signal). The data frame in the standard (CAN 2.0A, 11-bit ID) format looks like this:

"The DLC can be 9–15 to send more bytes on classic CAN." No: on classic CAN, DLC > 8 still means 8 data bytes. Only CAN FD maps 9–15 to 12…64 bytes.

1. What is it?

Arbitration is how CAN decides who may transmit when several nodes start a frame in the same bit. The official name of the access method is CSMA/CD+AMP: Carrier Sense (listen until the bus is idle), Multiple Access (anyone may start), Collision Detection (notice that someone else is also sending), Arbitration on Message Priority (resolve it by comparing identifiers). It is also called CSMA/CR (Collision Resolution) because, unlike Ethernet, nothing is destroyed.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Wait for idle. A node with a pending frame waits until it has seen the end of the previous frame plus the intermission (after EOF: 7 + 3 = 10 recessive bits; after power-up it needs 11 recessive bits).
  2. Start together. All nodes that are ready send SOF in the same bit. The SOF edge hard-synchronises everybody.
  3. Send the ID MSB first and listen. In each bit, each transmitter drives its bit and reads RXD at the sample point.
  4. Compare:
    • Sent 0, read 0 → fine, continue.
    • Sent 1, read 1 → fine, continue.
    • Sent 1, read 0 → lost arbitration: stop transmitting immediately, become a receiver of the winning frame, retry after it ends.
    • Sent 0, read 1 → impossible during arbitration unless there is a hardware fault (that would be a bit error).
  5. Continue to the end of the arbitration field (ID + RTR for standard frames). Exactly one node remains, unless two nodes sent identical ID and RTR.
  6. Losers retry automatically after the winner's frame and IFS. The controller does this in hardware; firmware is not involved.

Why it is non-destructive: because the bus value is always exactly the winner's bit (dominant overrides recessive), the winner's frame is never corrupted, and no bandwidth is wasted on a collision. Compare Ethernet's CSMA/CD, where a collision destroys both frames and both back off for a random time.

Stuff bits do not break arbitration: until the first differing bit, all transmitters send identical bit sequences, so they insert identical stuff bits at identical positions.

4. Rules that fall out of it (with the reasoning)

  • Arbitration field (standard) = 11 ID bits + RTR. A data frame beats a remote frame with the same ID (RTR 0 vs 1).
  • Two nodes must never transmit the same ID with different data. They both "win" arbitration, then collide in the DLC/data field: the node sending recessive sees dominant → bit error → error frame. (Remote frames with identical ID and DLC are the only legal exception.)
  • Arbitration only works because all nodes sample the same bit at the same time: it needs the round trip delay to fit inside the bit before the sample point. This is the physical reason bit rate × bus length is limited.
  • Priority is per message, not per node. Assign IDs by urgency/deadline (deadline-monotonic: shorter deadline → lower ID), not by "which ECU is important".
  • The worst-case wait for the highest-priority frame is one lower-priority frame already in progress (≈ 270 µs at 500 kbit/s for 8 bytes), because a frame in progress is never interrupted.

5. What goes wrong if…

"Higher ID = higher priority" or "more 1-bits = more priority". The numerically lowest ID wins, decided at the first differing bit from the MSB. The number of 1s is irrelevant.
  • Duplicate ID from two ECUs: observe error frames that always start inside the DLC or data field of that ID, LEC = bit error at both ECUs (Arbitration Sim preset "Duplicate ID").
  • Bus too long for the bit rate: the far node's dominant bit arrives after the near node sampled; both think they won. Observe: bit errors during the ID field, only between distant nodes.
  • A "babbling idiot" sending ID 0x000 continuously: it wins every arbitration and starves everyone. CAN's fault confinement does not stop a node that sends valid frames; a bus guardian or ID-rate monitoring does.

6. Say it like this

Nodes start together after bus idle and send their ID MSB first while reading back every bit. Because 0 is dominant, a node that sends 1 and reads 0 knows a lower ID is on the bus, stops in that same bit and becomes a receiver; the bus always carries the winner's bits, so nothing is lost. It requires the round trip to fit before the sample point, IDs must be unique per transmitter, and priority belongs to the message, not the ECU.

1. What is it?

Bit stuffing is a rule that forbids long runs of identical bits inside a frame. After 5 consecutive bits of the same value, the transmitter inserts one bit of the opposite value, called a stuff bit. Receivers know the rule and silently delete it. The rule applies from SOF through the end of the CRC sequence; it does not apply to the CRC delimiter, ACK field, EOF, error or overload frames. The stuff bit itself counts as the first bit of the next run.

2. Why does CAN need it? (two reasons)

  1. Resynchronisation edges. CAN has no clock wire. Each receiver keeps its bit timing aligned by watching for recessive→dominant edges (Concept 9). A frame of eight 0x00 data bytes would contain 64 identical bits and no edges; the receivers' oscillators would drift apart. Stuffing guarantees an edge at least every 10 bits in the worst case (patterns like 1111101111), which bounds how much drift can accumulate.
  2. Frame delimiting. Since 6 identical bits can never occur inside a valid stuffed frame, the 6-dominant error flag and the 7-recessive EOF are unambiguous: any node seeing them knows they are not data.

A receiver that sees 6 identical bits inside a stuffed field raises a stuff error.

    size_t k = 0; int last = -1, run = 0;

    }

5. What goes wrong if…

"The worst case is one stuff bit per 5 bits, so ⌊(34+8n)/5⌋." The stuff bit starts a new run, so after the first stuff bit every 4 data bits can force another one. ⌊(34+64)/5⌋ = 19 underestimates by 5 bits (10 µs per frame at 500 kbit/s).
  • Bus-load or latency analysis without worst-case stuffing: a message set that looked like 48 % load is really 58 %. Observe: deadline misses on low-priority frames only when data patterns produce many stuff bits (e.g. 0x00 / 0xFF payloads).
  • A node with a bad clock: on a frame of many 0x00 bytes it drifts until it samples the stuff bit in the wrong place → stuff error. Observe: errors correlated with payload content.
  • Applying stuffing to EOF in a software CAN implementation: EOF must be 7 plain recessive bits; stuffing it would make every frame look malformed (form error).

6. Say it like this

After five equal bits the transmitter inserts one opposite bit, from SOF to the end of the CRC, and receivers remove it. It guarantees recessive-to-dominant edges for resynchronisation and makes six equal bits impossible inside a frame, so error flags and EOF are unambiguous. Because the stuff bit starts the next run, the worst case is one extra bit per four after the first, giving 135 bits for an 8-byte standard frame including the intermission.

1. What is it?

Bit timing is how a CAN controller decides, from its own clock alone, where each bit starts and the exact instant to read it. The controller divides one nominal bit time into small equal slices called time quanta (tq): tq = BRP / fCAN, where BRP (Baud Rate Prescaler) is an integer divider you program and fCAN is the controller's input clock (on STM32F4 bxCAN that is the APB1 bus clock, PCLK1). The bit is then built from four segments:

SYNC 1
PROP_SEG
PHASE_SEG1
PS2
0▲ sample point14 tq

2. How does it work, segment by segment?

  1. SYNC_SEG = 1 tq. If every node's clock were perfect, every edge would fall inside this quantum.
  2. PROP_SEG (propagation segment) waits out the physical round trip: 2 × (cable + transceiver + controller delay). It is what makes arbitration and ACK work over a long cable.
  3. PHASE_SEG1 (PS1): buffer before the sample point. Resynchronisation can lengthen it when an edge arrives late.
  4. Sample point = end of PS1. The controller reads the bus here. Position = (1 + PROP + PS1) / N, where N is the total number of tq per bit.
  5. PHASE_SEG2 (PS2): buffer after the sample point. Resynchronisation can shorten it when an edge arrives early.
  6. SJW (Synchronisation Jump Width): the maximum number of tq a single resync may stretch PS1 or shrink PS2. SJW ≤ min(PS1, PS2).

Many controllers merge PROP+PS1 into one TSEG1 register field, and call PS2 TSEG2: N = 1 + TSEG1 + TSEG2, sample point = (1 + TSEG1)/N. bxCAN allows TS1 = 1–16 and TS2 = 1–8, so N ≤ 25.

WHY A CRYSTAL IS NEEDED

Clock tolerance is limited by how far nodes drift between resync edges. Two conditions (Bosch / ISO 11898-1), with df = allowed relative frequency error of each node:

(1) df ≤ SJW / (20 × N)                          // 10 bits between edges (stuffing), two nodes drifting
(2) df ≤ min(PS1, PS2) / (2 × (13 × N − PS2))     // must still sample correctly across an error flag
e.g. N=16, PS1=PS2=SJW=2:  (1) 2/320 = 0.63 %   (2) 2/(2 × 206) = 0.49 %  → df ≤ 0.49 % (±4900 ppm)

A crystal (±50–100 ppm including ageing and temperature) has a huge margin. An internal RC oscillator such as the STM32F4 HSI is ±1 % at 25 °C and drifts further over temperature, which can exceed the budget, especially with short PS2/SJW and at high bit rates. CAN FD data phases are even tighter. So: crystal (or a resonator after checking the maths); RC oscillator: no.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • APB1 frequency changes but BTR does not (a clock-tree change for low power): 21 MHz / (6 × 14) = 250 kbit/s. Observe: the node sees bit errors on every transmission, goes bus-off, and (with auto-recovery) disturbs the bus periodically. That is Scenario 1.
  • Mixed sample points (one node 75 %, others 87.5 %) on a long bus: the early sampler reads bits before ringing settles. Observe: CRC/stuff errors that come and go with temperature and harness length.
  • fCAN / bitrate not an integer: 42 MHz at 1 Mbit/s gives 42 = 3 × 14 ✔, but 42 MHz at 800 kbit/s gives 52.5 — no exact setting exists. The nearest (BRP 4, N 13 → 807.7 kbit/s) is 0.96 % fast, which on its own already exceeds the ≈ 0.5 % oscillator budget. Observe: works with one partner on the bench, fails intermittently on the vehicle. Pick a CAN clock that divides all your bit rates.

The bit is divided into time quanta, tq = BRP over the CAN clock, and built from SYNC (1 tq), PROP, PS1 and PS2; N = 1 + TSEG1 + TSEG2 and the sample point is (1 + TSEG1)/N. For a 42 MHz STM32F4 at 500 kbit/s, N × BRP = 84, so BRP 6, TS1 11, TS2 2 gives 85.7 % and BTR = 0x001A0005. Clock tolerance is a few tenths of a percent, so CAN needs a crystal, and every node should use the same sample point.

1. What is it?

Every node has its own oscillator, and no two oscillators run at exactly the same frequency. Synchronisation is how receivers keep their idea of "where does this bit start?" aligned with the transmitter. There are two kinds: a hard synchronisation once per frame, and small resynchronisations during the frame.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Hard sync on SOF. On the recessive→dominant edge of SOF (after bus idle), every receiver restarts its bit timing so that the edge lies in SYNC_SEG. Phase error is forced to zero.
  2. Measure the phase error on later edges. On every other recessive→dominant edge during the frame, the controller notes in which quantum the edge fell. The phase error e is that position relative to SYNC_SEG, in tq.
  3. e = 0: edge in SYNC_SEG, no correction.
  4. e > 0 (edge late, it fell in PROP or PS1 — the transmitter's clock is slower than ours): lengthen PS1 by min(e, SJW) so our sample point moves later.
  5. e < 0 (edge early, it fell in PS2 of the previous bit — the transmitter is faster): shorten PS2 by min(|e|, SJW) so the next bit starts sooner.
  6. At most one resync between two sample points. A transmitter that is itself sending a dominant bit does not resync on a recessive→dominant edge with a positive phase error: that late edge is just its own bit coming back through the loop delay.

  • SJW = 1 with a resonator and a long frame of alternating stuff patterns: correction capacity is too small; errors appear only with certain payloads. Increase SJW (up to PS2).

Each frame starts with a hard sync on the SOF edge that zeros everyone's phase error. Only recessive-to-dominant edges are used because they are actively driven and sharp, and stuffing guarantees one at least every ten bits.

2. How does it work, step by step? (one corrupted frame)

  1. A spike flips bit 3 of data byte 2 on the way to the cluster ECU only.
  2. The cluster computes the CRC over what it received; it does not match the 15 CRC bits on the wire. The cluster does not drive the ACK slot. (Other receivers that got it right still ACK.)
  3. After the ACK delimiter the cluster starts an active error flag: 6 dominant bits.
  4. Those 6 dominant bits land in the EOF field, where everybody expects recessive → all other nodes detect a form error and send their own flags. The superposed flag lasts 6–12 bits.
  5. Everyone discards the frame. An 8-bit recessive error delimiter follows, then the intermission.
  6. The transmitter retransmits automatically (unless one-shot mode: bxCAN MCR.NART, FDCAN CCCR.DAR). Counters update: transmitter TEC += 8, receivers REC += 1.

5. What goes wrong if… (reading LEC like a detective)

  • LEC = 3 (ACK) on every transmission: nobody else on the bus is active, or they are all at a different bit rate, or in silent mode.
  • LEC = 4 (sent recessive, read dominant) outside arbitration: someone else is driving dominant during your frame: wrong bit rate on your node or another, duplicate ID, or reflections.
  • LEC = 5 (sent dominant, read recessive): your dominant never reaches the bus: transceiver in standby, CANH/CANL short, swapped wires, TXD not connected.
  • LEC = 6 (CRC) at receivers only: signal integrity (termination, stubs), clock tolerance, or a mismatched sample point.

6. Say it like this

There are five error types: bit and ACK errors detected by the transmitter, CRC errors by receivers, and stuff and form errors by anyone. Whoever detects one sends a six-bit error flag, which breaks stuffing or form so every node also rejects the frame, and the transmitter retransmits. On bxCAN the last error code in ESR tells you which one happened, and that usually points straight at the root cause.

1. What is it?

Fault confinement is CAN's way of making sure a broken node cannot destroy the network. Every controller keeps two counters: TEC (Transmit Error Counter) and REC (Receive Error Counter). Errors push them up; successes pull them down. Their values put the node in one of three states:

StateConditionBehaviour
Error activeTEC ≤ 127 and REC ≤ 127Normal. Signals errors with an active (6 dominant) error flag, destroying the frame for everyone.
Error passiveTEC > 127 or REC > 127Still transmits and receives, but signals errors with a passive (6 recessive) flag that cannot destroy other nodes' frames. Waits 8 extra bits (suspend transmission) after its own transmissions. CAN FD: sets ESI = 1.
Bus-offTEC > 255Takes no part in bus activity at all (no TX, no ACK, no error flags). Leaves only after 128 occurrences of 11 consecutive recessive bits, then counters reset to 0.

2. How does it work, step by step? (the counting rules, simplified ISO 11898-1)

  1. Transmitter sends an error flag: TEC += 8. Exceptions: (a) error-passive transmitter with an ACK error that sees no dominant bit during its passive flag; (b) stuff error during arbitration on a recessive stuff bit that was read dominant.
  2. Receiver detects an error: REC += 1. Receiver sees a dominant bit as the first bit after its own error flag: REC += 8.
  3. Bit error while sending an active error flag or overload flag: transmitter TEC += 8, receiver REC += 8.
  4. 14 consecutive dominant bits after an active flag (8 after a passive flag), and every further 8: += 8.
  5. Successful transmission: TEC −= 1 (if > 0). Successful reception: REC −= 1 if 1…127; if REC > 127 it is set to a value between 119 and 127.

The asymmetry (+8 / −1) means a node that fails more than about 1 frame in 8 drifts towards bus-off; occasional errors on a healthy node are forgiven. A transmitter is penalised more because a fault that only one node sees is most likely that node's fault.

1. What is it?

Bus load is the fraction of time the bus is busy carrying frames. 50 % load at 500 kbit/s means 250 000 of every 500 000 bit times are occupied. It matters because CAN never interrupts a frame in progress: the busier the bus, the longer low-priority frames wait, and the less headroom remains for retransmissions after errors.

2. How do you calculate it, step by step?

  1. List every message: its DLC, standard or extended ID, and its period (or the minimum gap between events for event-triggered messages).
  2. For each, compute the worst-case bits on the bus including stuffing and IFS: standard 8n + 47 + ⌊(34 + 8n − 1)/4⌋, extended 8n + 67 + ⌊(54 + 8n − 1)/4⌋.
  3. Bits per second for that message = bits / period.
  4. Sum over all messages and divide by the bit rate.
bus load = Σ (bits_i / period_i) / bitrate
bits_i   = worst-case stuffed length incl. IFS  (135 for 8-byte standard)

5. What goes wrong if…

  • Load computed with 108 bits per frame (no IFS, no stuffing): real load is 25 % higher than the spreadsheet. Observe: low-priority status frames arrive late or their periods jitter by several ms.
  • Event-triggered frames counted at their average rate: a burst (all door switches at once) temporarily exceeds 100 %. Count them at their minimum inter-arrival time.
  • No error budget: on a bus at 85 % load, one error frame plus retransmission per 100 frames pushes it over the edge.

6. Say it like this

Bus load is the sum over messages of worst-case frame bits divided by period, divided by the bit rate, using 135 bits for an 8-byte standard frame including stuffing and intermission. Twenty 8-byte frames every 10 ms at 500 kbit/s is already 54 %. OEMs target about 50 % or less, and for latency guarantees you use response-time analysis, because a low-priority frame can wait behind every higher-priority one plus one blocking frame.

1. What is it?

Because CAN is broadcast, every node receives every frame on the bus — on a busy powertrain bus that can be 3 000 frames per second. An acceptance filter is hardware inside the controller that compares each received ID with rules you program and only stores matching frames in the RX FIFOs (receive queues). Non-matching frames are still ACKed and checked for errors, but the CPU never sees them and gets no interrupt.

STM32F4 (two CAN controllers) has 28 filter banks shared by CAN1 and CAN2 (the split point is CAN_FMR.CAN2SB). Single-CAN parts (F103) have 14. Each bank is two 32-bit registers, FiR1 and FiR2, and can work in two modes and two scales:

2. How does a mask filter decide, step by step?

  1. The received frame's ID, IDE and RTR bits are laid out in a 32-bit word exactly like CAN_RIxR: STID[10:0] at bits 31:21, EXID[17:0] at 20:3, IDE bit 2, RTR bit 1.
  2. In mask mode, every mask bit of 1 means "must match", 0 means "don't care".
  3. The frame is accepted if (rx_word & mask) == (filter_word & mask).
  4. If several banks match, the winner is chosen by priority: 32-bit beats 16-bit, then list beats mask, then the lower bank number. Its index is reported in RDTxR.FMI (Filter Match Index), which you can use to dispatch without comparing IDs.
  5. The frame goes into the FIFO selected by FFA1R. Each FIFO holds 3 messages.

3. Worked example: accept exactly 0x100–0x1FF

common prefix: bits 10..8 = 001 ; bits 7..0 vary freely
→ ID  = 0x100, mask = 0x700 (bits 10..8 must match)

Test 0x1A5: (0x1A5<<21) & 0xE0000006 = 0x20000000 == FR1 & mask ✔ accept
Test 0x2A5: (0x2A5<<21) & 0xE0000006 = 0x40000000 ≠ 0x20000000 ✖ reject

5. What goes wrong if…

  • No active filter bank: bxCAN drops everything. Observe: the classic "CAN works in loopback mode but receives nothing on the real bus" — actually it receives nothing in loopback either unless a filter is active; the TX side and ACK work, so the bus looks healthy on an analyser.
  • Accept-all filter (mask 0) on a busy bus with slow ISR: 3-deep FIFO overruns in ≈ 0.7 ms; FOVR0 set, frames lost at random.
  • Mask bit polarity inverted (writing 0 for "must match"): the filter accepts almost everything and ignores the bits you cared about.

6. Say it like this

Filters compare each received ID with programmed rules so the CPU only sees relevant frames. On bxCAN a bank is two 32-bit registers used as ID plus mask or as a list of exact IDs, in 32- or 16-bit scale, routed to FIFO 0 or 1; a mask bit of 1 means must match. To accept 0x100 to 0x1FF you use ID 0x100 and mask 0x700 shifted to bit 21, and if no bank is active nothing is received at all.

1. What is it?

A TX mailbox (or TX buffer, message object) is a hardware slot that holds one frame waiting to be sent. Bus arbitration only orders frames that are already in hardware buffers of different nodes. Which of my own pending buffers my controller offers to the next arbitration is decided by the controller and the driver, and this is where real systems lose priority. Priority inversion means a high-priority frame waits behind a lower-priority one.

2. How does inversion happen, step by step?

  1. The ECU queues three status frames 0x400, 0x410, 0x420 into bxCAN mailboxes 0–2.
  2. Other ECUs keep sending 0x100–0x3FF traffic, so our 0x4xx frames keep losing arbitration and stay in the mailboxes.
  3. Now the application wants to send the urgent 0x080. All three mailboxes are busy, so it waits in a software queue.
  4. 0x080 cannot reach arbitration until one 0x4xx frame finally wins. Its latency now depends on the lowest priority traffic on the bus: classic inversion.
FORMS OF PRIORITY INVERSION
  • Buffer-index priority: a periodic low-priority frame in message object 1 is always sent before an urgent frame in object 8 of the same node.
  • FIFO order: a single TX FIFO (or a software queue) puts the urgent frame behind a low-priority one that keeps losing arbitration to other nodes' medium-priority traffic.
  • All mailboxes busy: three low-priority frames occupy all bxCAN mailboxes and keep losing arbitration; the urgent frame waits in software.
  • Refill gap: with one buffer, the time to reload it after TX completes lets lower-priority frames from other nodes win the next arbitration.

3. Worked example: the cost in microseconds

500 kbit/s, 8-byte frames ≈ 270 µs each (worst case)
Correct behaviour (0x080 offered at once): waits at most for 1 frame already on the bus → ≤ 270 µs
DCAN, 0x080 in message object 20, objects 1..10 pending status frames:
  waits for 10 frames of its own node + interleaved frames of others
  ≈ 10 × 270 µs = 2.7 ms (plus other nodes' traffic) → requirement of 1 ms violated

Fixes: ID-ordered selection (TXFP = 0, LBUF = 0), a software priority queue sorted by ID feeding the free mailboxes, reserving one mailbox for high-priority traffic, and aborting a lower-priority pending mailbox (TSR.ABRQx on bxCAN, TXBCR on FDCAN) to make room. Schedulability analysis assumes the node always offers its highest-priority ready frame to arbitration.

/* bxCAN: queue a frame in the next free mailbox */
int can_tx(uint16_t id, const uint8_t *d, uint8_t dlc) {
    uint32_t tsr = CAN1->TSR;
    if (!(tsr & (CAN_TSR_TME0 | CAN_TSR_TME1 | CAN_TSR_TME2)))
        return -1;                                  // all 3 busy
    uint32_t mb = (tsr & CAN_TSR_CODE) >> CAN_TSR_CODE_Pos;   // next free
    CAN1->sTxMailBox[mb].TIR  = (uint32_t)id << 21;            // STID, IDE=0, RTR=0
    CAN1->sTxMailBox[mb].TDTR = dlc & 0xF;
    CAN1->sTxMailBox[mb].TDLR = d[0] | d[1]<<8 | d[2]<<16 | (uint32_t)d[3]<<24;
    CAN1->sTxMailBox[mb].TDHR = d[4] | d[5]<<8 | d[6]<<16 | (uint32_t)d[7]<<24;
    CAN1->sTxMailBox[mb].TIR |= CAN_TI0R_TXRQ;                 // request
    return (int)mb;
}

/* make room for an urgent frame: abort the pending mailbox holding the highest ID */
void can_make_room(void) {
    int worst = -1; uint32_t worst_id = 0;
    for (int m = 0; m < 3; m++) {
        if (CAN1->TSR & (CAN_TSR_TME0 << m)) continue;      // empty: nothing to abort
        uint32_t id = CAN1->sTxMailBox[m].TIR >> 21;
        if (id > worst_id) { worst_id = id; worst = m; }
    }
    if (worst >= 0) CAN1->TSR = CAN_TSR_ABRQ0 << (8 * worst);  // ABRQ0/1/2 at bits 7/15/23
}   // the aborted frame goes back into the software queue

5. What goes wrong if…

  • bxCAN with TXFP = 1 (FIFO order) chosen "to keep message order": urgent frames wait behind status frames. Observe: latency of the top-priority ID depends on bus load.
  • The highest-priority frame mapped to a high message-object number on C_CAN/DCAN (Scenario 4): measured latency 8 ms instead of < 1 ms.
  • Calling can_tx() from two tasks without a lock: both read the same TSR.CODE, both write the same mailbox; one frame is lost or mixed (ID of one, data of the other).

6. Say it like this

Arbitration only orders frames that are already in hardware buffers, so priority can be lost inside the node: controllers that pick by buffer index, a FIFO-ordered queue, or all mailboxes filled with low-priority frames that keep losing. The fixes are ID-ordered selection such as bxCAN TXFP = 0, a priority-sorted software queue, a reserved mailbox, and aborting the lowest-priority pending frame to free a slot.

1. What is it?

CAN FD (CAN with Flexible Data rate; Bosch 2012, standardised in ISO 11898-1:2015) fixes classic CAN's two limits: 8-byte payloads and a 1 Mbit/s ceiling. It keeps arbitration exactly as in classic CAN (so that part is still limited by bus length) and then switches to a faster bit rate for the data phase, with payloads up to 64 bytes.

SOFID 11RRSIDEFDF=1resBRS⇢ fast: ESIDLC 4DATA 0–64 BSBC 4CRC 17/21⇠ CRC del · ACK · EOF

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. Arbitration phase at the nominal rate (e.g. 500 kbit/s): SOF, ID, RRS, IDE exactly like classic CAN, because all nodes must still be in lock-step.
  2. FDF bit recessive announces "this is an FD frame". Then a reserved bit (res, dominant).
  3. BRS bit (Bit Rate Switch). If recessive, at this bit's sample point every node switches to the data bit rate (e.g. 2 Mbit/s). Only one node now drives the bus, so the round-trip constraint no longer applies.
  4. ESI, DLC, data, stuff count and CRC are sent at the fast rate.
  5. At the sample point of the CRC delimiter all nodes switch back to the nominal rate, because the ACK slot again involves every node at once.

6. Say it like this

CAN FD keeps classic arbitration at the nominal rate, then after a recessive BRS switches to a faster data rate for ESI, DLC, up to 64 bytes and a CRC-17 or CRC-21 with a stuff-bit count, and switches back at the CRC delimiter for the ACK. FDF replaced r0, RRS replaced RTR so there are no remote frames, and DLC 9 to 15 map to 12 up to 64 bytes. 1. What is it?

A CAN transmitter always checks every bit it sends by reading it back on RXD (bit-error detection). Its RXD lags its TXD by the transceiver loop delay — typically 100–250 ns. In the nominal phase that is small compared with a 2 µs bit and fits inside PROP_SEG. In a fast data phase it does not: at 2 Mbit/s a bit is 500 ns, at 5 Mbit/s only 200 ns. The transmitter would compare bit n it is sending with bit n−1 still coming back, and flag false bit errors. TDC (Transmitter Delay Compensation) moves the point where the transmitter checks its own bits.

2. How does it work, step by step?

  1. During each FD frame, at the FDF→res edge (recessive→dominant, the last edge before the bit-rate switch and always present), the controller measures the time from its own TXD edge to the RXD edge. That is the actual loop delay, TDCV (TDC value).
  2. It places a secondary sample point (SSP) at TDCV + TDCO (TDC offset, programmed) after the start of each transmitted data bit.
  3. In the data phase the transmitter compares the bit it sent with RXD sampled at the SSP, which now lies in the correct returning bit.
  4. Receivers are not affected: they simply sample the bus at their normal sample point.

3. Worked example: 5 Mbit/s data phase

             the returned copy of that bit occupies 160 … 360 ns, so the SSP lands
             137.5 ns into it (≈ 69 % of the bit) — safely inside the correct bit ✔

A transmitter monitors its own bits, but its RXD lags TXD by the transceiver loop delay of 100 to 250 ns, which at 2 to 5 Mbit/s is comparable to or longer than a bit. TDC measures that delay on the FDF-to-res edge of every frame and checks the transmitter's own bits at a secondary sample point equal to the measured delay plus an offset, typically the data sample point. Receivers are unaffected; without it only the node's own BRS frames fail with data-phase bit errors.

1. What is it?

CAN XL is the third generation of CAN (CiA 610, ISO 11898-1:2024). It carries 1–2048 bytes per frame with a data phase up to about 20 Mbit/s, positioning CAN between CAN FD and 10 Mbit/s single-pair Ethernet (10BASE-T1S). CAN SIC (Signal Improvement Capability, CiA 601-4) is a transceiver feature that cleans up ringing so CAN FD can run 5–8 Mbit/s on real, imperfect wiring.

2. How does CAN XL work, step by step?

  1. Arbitration is classic CAN, but the ID is an 11-bit priority ID used only for arbitration, not for content.
  2. Control bits: XLF (XL format, where FD had "res") and ADH (arbitration-to-data high) mark the switch.
  3. Header: an 8-bit SDT (Service Data unit Type: what is inside — e.g. a tunnelled Ethernet frame or a CAN FD frame), SEC (CANsec security present), 11-bit DLC, 8-bit VCID (Virtual CAN network ID, like a VLAN), 32-bit acceptance field (addressing/content), protected by a 13-bit header CRC.
  4. Data phase: with SIC XL transceivers the bus switches to a FAST mode in which the transceiver drives both levels push-pull (no longer dominant/recessive), allowing ≈ 10–20 Mbit/s.
  5. Frame CRC: 32 bits. Then back to arbitration mode for the ACK.

1. What is it?

A CAN frame's data field is just 0–8 (or 64) bytes. A DBC file (originally a Vector format, now the de-facto standard) describes how named signals are packed into those bytes: which bits, in which byte order, how to scale the raw integer into a physical value, and who sends and receives it. Getting the byte order wrong is one of the most common integration bugs in the industry.

BO_ 291 EngineData: 8 ECM                // 291 = 0x123, 8 bytes, sender ECM
 SG_ EngineSpeed : 24|16@1+ (0.125,0) [0|8031.875] "rpm" TCM,BCM
 SG_ CoolantTemp : 8|8@1+ (1,-40) [-40|215] "degC" BCM
 SG_ TorqueReq   : 39|12@0- (0.5,0) [-1024|1023.5] "Nm" TCM
//               start|len@order sign (factor,offset) [min|max] unit receivers
//  @1 = Intel (little-endian), @0 = Motorola (big-endian); + unsigned, - signed
//  Extended-ID messages have bit 31 set in the DBC ID: 0x80000000 | id

2. How does packing work, step by step?

  1. Scale: raw = (physical − offset) / factor, rounded to an integer; on decode physical = raw × factor + offset.
  2. Number the payload bits: bit k = byte ⌊k/8⌋, bit (k mod 8); bit 0 is the LSB of byte 0, bit 7 its MSB, bit 8 the LSB of byte 1.
  3. Intel (@1): start bit = signal LSB. Further bits go upward: k+1, k+2… crossing into the next byte's low bits. Lower byte holds less significant bits.
  4. Motorola (@0): start bit (in the DBC file) = signal MSB. Bits go downward within a byte (7→0), then continue at bit 7 of the next byte. Lower byte holds more significant bits. (Tools display a "sawtooth" layout; some GUIs show the LSB as the start bit — always check which convention a tool uses.)
  5. Signed signals are two's complement over len bits; sign-extend after extraction.

/* Generic DBC extraction, payload up to 64 bytes */
uint64_t sig_get(const uint8_t *p, unsigned start, unsigned len, int motorola) {
    uint64_t v = 0;
    if (!motorola) {                            /* Intel: start = LSB */
        for (unsigned i = 0; i < len; i++) {
            unsigned b = start + i;
            v |= (uint64_t)((p[b >> 3] >> (b & 7)) & 1u) << i;
        }
    } else {                                    /* Motorola: start = MSB */
        unsigned b = start;
        for (int i = (int)len - 1; i >= 0; i--) {
            v |= (uint64_t)((p[b >> 3] >> (b & 7)) & 1u) << i;
            b = ((b & 7) == 0) ? b + 15 : b - 1;   /* next byte, bit 7 */
        }
    }
    return v;
}
int64_t sign_extend(uint64_t v, unsigned len) {
    uint64_t m = 1ull << (len - 1);
    return (int64_t)((v ^ m) - m);
}
/* physical = (signed ? sign_extend(raw,len) : raw) * factor + offset */

5. What goes wrong if…

"Just memcpy the bytes into a uint16_t." That only works for byte-aligned Intel signals on a little-endian CPU. Motorola signals, non-aligned start bits and a big-endian core (PowerPC MPC57xx in many ECUs) all break it, and the code is not portable.
  • DBC says @1, sender encodes Motorola (Scenario 5): observe a plausible-looking but wildly wrong value (50 km/h shown as 348 km/h) that changes non-linearly with the real value.
  • Copying the start bit when changing @1 to @0: Intel start 16 is the LSB of byte 2; Motorola for the same bytes needs start 23 (MSB of byte 2). Leaving 16 makes the signal run into the wrong bytes.
  • Forgetting sign extension: −10 Nm on a 12-bit signal (0xFEC = 4076) is shown as +2038 Nm.

6. Say it like this

A DBC defines each signal by start bit, length, byte order, sign, factor and offset; physical equals raw times factor plus offset. In Intel order the start bit is the LSB and the signal grows into higher bytes; in Motorola order the DBC start bit is the MSB, bits go down within a byte and continue at bit 7 of the next byte. I extract bit by bit so the code is independent of CPU endianness, and I sign-extend signed signals.

1. What is it?

A classic CAN frame carries at most 8 bytes, but diagnostics regularly needs more: a 17-character VIN (Vehicle Identification Number), a list of fault codes, or a 2 MB firmware image. ISO-TP (ISO 15765-2, "Transport Protocol") is the layer that cuts a long message into numbered CAN frames, sends them with flow control, and reassembles them at the receiver. It supports messages up to 4095 bytes (or 4 GB with an escape sequence).

FramePCI (first byte(s))Content
Single Frame (SF)0L — L = length 1…7Whole message (CAN FD: 00 LL for up to 62 bytes)
First Frame (FF)1L LL — 12-bit length 8…4095First 6 bytes. Length > 4095: 10 00 + 32-bit length
Consecutive Frame (CF)2N — N = sequence number7 bytes each. SN starts at 1, wraps F → 0

PCI = Protocol Control Information: the first nibble (4 bits) of byte 0 says the frame type.

2. How does a transfer work, step by step?

  1. Message ≤ 7 bytes: send one Single Frame, PCI 0L. Done.
  2. Longer: the sender sends a First Frame with the total length (12 bits) and the first 6 data bytes, then stops and waits.
  3. The receiver allocates a buffer and answers with a Flow Control frame: FS (flow status) = 0 ContinueToSend, BS (block size) = how many CFs before the next FC (0 = all), STmin = minimum gap between CFs.
  4. The sender sends Consecutive Frames of 7 bytes each with sequence numbers 1, 2, … F, 0, 1 … respecting STmin.
  5. After BS frames (if BS > 0) the sender waits for another FC.
  6. When the received byte count reaches the FF length, the receiver delivers the whole message to the upper layer (UDS).
  • FS (flow status): 0 = ContinueToSend, 1 = Wait, 2 = Overflow/abort.
  • STmin: 0x00–0x7F = 0–127 ms; 0xF1–0xF9 = 100–900 µs; others reserved (treat as 127 ms).
  • Timers: N_Bs — sender waits for FC (1000 ms); N_Cr — receiver waits for next CF (1000 ms); N_As/N_Ar — CAN frame transmission confirmation (1000 ms). Expiry aborts the message.
  • Addressing: normal (CAN ID only), extended (first data byte = target address), mixed. OBD: functional request 0x7DF, physical requests 0x7E0–0x7E7, responses 0x7E8–0x7EF.

void isotp_ff(uint8_t f[8], const uint8_t *msg, uint16_t len) {
    f[0] = 0x10 | (uint8_t)(len >> 8);   // PCI type 1 + length bits 11..8
    f[1] = (uint8_t)len;                 // length bits 7..0
    memcpy(&f[2], msg, 6);               // first 6 payload bytes
}
int isotp_parse_fc(const uint8_t f[8], uint8_t *bs, uint32_t *stmin_us) {
    if ((f[0] >> 4) != 3) return -1;     // not a flow control
    *bs = f[1];
    uint8_t st = f[2];
    *stmin_us = st <= 0x7F ? st * 1000u
              : (st >= 0xF1 && st <= 0xF9) ? (st - 0xF0) * 100u : 127000u;
    return f[0] & 0x0F;                  // FS: 0 CTS, 1 WAIT, 2 OVFLW
}

5. What goes wrong if…

"The sender sends the FF and all CFs back to back." It must wait for the FC after the FF (and after every block). Forgetting to send the FC from the receiver side, or filtering out the FC ID, is the most common reason an ISO-TP transfer stalls and times out after N_Bs.
  • FC sent on the functional ID 0x7DF (Scenario 6): the ECU waits on 0x7E0. Observe: FF, then 1 s silence, ECU logs N_Bs timeout.
  • Sender ignores the STmin the receiver asked for: the receiver's 3-deep FIFO overruns, a CF is lost, the next CF has the wrong SN, the receiver aborts.
  • SN wrap not handled (expects 0x10 after 0x0F): every message longer than 6 + 15 × 7 = 111 bytes fails at CF 16.

6. Say it like this

ISO-TP segments messages longer than one frame: a Single Frame for up to 7 bytes, otherwise a First Frame with a 12-bit length and 6 bytes, then Consecutive Frames of 7 bytes with a 4-bit sequence number. The receiver paces the sender with Flow Control frames carrying flow status, block size and STmin, and timers N_Bs and N_Cr abort a stalled transfer. Segmented transfers use physical addressing, and the FC must go to the ID the sender listens on.

1. What is it?

UDS (Unified Diagnostic Services, ISO 14229) is the request/response language a diagnostic tester (workshop tool, end-of-line station, flashing PC) uses to talk to an ECU: read and clear fault codes, read identifiers, run routines, and reprogram the flash. 5. What goes wrong if…

  • Tester does not send TesterPresent: after S3 = 5 s without a request, the ECU drops back to the default session; the next TransferData gets 7F 36 7F (not supported in active session).
  • Tester times out at P2 = 50 ms during erase instead of honouring 0x78 and waiting up to P2*: flashing aborts every time on large ECUs.
  • BSC repeated after a lost response: the server may accept a repeated BSC as a retry (ISO allows it) but a skipped one gives 0x73 wrongBlockSequenceCounter.

6. Say it like this

UDS is a request/response protocol over ISO-TP: a positive response is the SID plus 0x40 and a negative one is 7F, SID, NRC. Flashing goes extended session, DTC and comms off, programming session into the bootloader, seed and key, erase with responsePending, RequestDownload which returns the max block length, TransferData blocks with a wrapping counter, TransferExit, a check routine, and ECU reset. 1. What is it?

SAE J1939 is the higher-layer protocol used on CAN in heavy vehicles: trucks, buses, tractors, construction machines, generators, marine engines. It defines what the 29-bit identifier means, standard messages for engine speed, fuel rate and so on, and how ECUs claim addresses. It runs at 250 kbit/s (J1939-11/-15) or 500 kbit/s (J1939-14), with 29-bit identifiers and a sample point of 87.5 %.

Priority 3EDP 1DP 1PF 8PS 8SA 8

2. How is the 29-bit ID built, field by field?

  1. Priority (3 bits, 28–26): 0 highest … 7 lowest. Only affects arbitration.
  2. EDP / DP (Extended Data Page / Data Page, bits 25–24): select the page of PGN definitions.
  3. PF (PDU Format, bits 23–16): decides the frame type. PDU1 (PF < 240): PS = destination address (peer-to-peer, 0xFF = global). PDU2 (PF ≥ 240): PS = group extension, always broadcast.
  4. PS (PDU Specific, bits 15–8).
  5. SA (Source Address, bits 7–0): who sent it, e.g. 0x00 = engine #1, 0x03 = transmission.
  6. PGN (Parameter Group Number) = EDP, DP, PF and (only for PDU2) PS. It identifies the message content. An SPN (Suspect Parameter Number) is one signal inside a PGN.

2. How does a device come up, step by step?

  1. Power on: node 5 sends a boot-up message on 0x705 with data 00 and enters pre-operational state (SDO allowed, PDOs not).
  2. The master configures it via SDO (Service Data Object: confirmed read/write of any OD entry) — e.g. PDO mapping, heartbeat period.
  3. The master sends NMT 000: 01 05 (start node 5). Node 5 goes operational.
  4. Node 5 now sends PDOs (Process Data Objects: unconfirmed, compact, real-time) e.g. on 0x185, and its heartbeat on 0x705 with 05 every N ms.

5. What goes wrong if…

  • Expecting PDOs from a node still in pre-operational: nothing arrives until NMT start; the heartbeat shows 7F.
  • Two devices with the same node ID: identical COB-IDs with different data → bit errors and boot-up collisions.
  • Index byte order swapped (10 00 instead of 00 10): the device answers abort 0x06020000 (object does not exist).

6. Say it like this

CANopen builds 11-bit COB-IDs from a 4-bit function code and a 7-bit node ID, around an object dictionary addressed by index and sub-index. NMT on ID 0 controls states, SDOs on 0x600 and 0x580 plus node are confirmed reads and writes of any object, PDOs carry mapped real-time data, and heartbeat on 0x700 plus node reports the state. For example 0x18A is TPDO1 of node 10.

1. What is it?

When CAN misbehaves, the cause is in one of three places: the physical layer (wiring, termination, transceiver), the configuration (bit rate, sample point, filters), or the software (IDs, timing, buffers). Three tools separate them: an oscilloscope shows the analog truth on the wire; a CAN analyser (Vector CANoe/CANalyzer, PEAK PCAN-View, Kvaser) decodes frames, error frames and bus load; Linux SocketCAN gives you a scriptable analyser for the price of a USB adapter.

2. How to debug, step by step

  1. Power off, multimeter CANH–CANL: expect 60 Ω (see Concept 3).
  2. Power on, scope CH1 on CANH, CH2 on CANL, both referenced to the ECU's ground. Add a math channel CH1 − CH2 (or use a differential probe). Trigger on the falling edge of CANL / rising of Vdiff, or use the scope's CAN decoder and trigger on an ID or an error frame.
  3. Check levels: recessive 2.5 / 2.5 V, dominant 3.5 / 1.5 V, Vdiff ≈ 2 V into 60 Ω. A dominant Vdiff of ≈ 3 V suggests one terminator missing (120 Ω load); ≈ 1.2 V suggests extra terminators.
  4. Measure the narrowest pulse (1 bit) to confirm the bit rate: 2.00 µs = 500 kbit/s.
  5. Zoom on edges: ringing or a staircase at the dominant→recessive edge means reflections (missing termination, long stub). If ringing reaches the sample point, you get sporadic bit/stuff/CRC errors.
  6. Find error frames: a run of ≥ 6 dominant bits not on a frame boundary. Note which field they interrupt: ACK delimiter → nobody ACKed or CRC error at a receiver; mid-frame → bit/stuff/form error.
  7. Common-mode level (CANH + CANL)/2: a DC shift indicates ground offset between ECUs.
  8. Correlate with the controller: read LEC, TEC and REC in each ECU (Concept 10).

5. What goes wrong if…

  • Single-ended probe ground clipped to the wrong ECU: ground offset adds a DC shift and you may "see" a bad recessive level that is really a probing artefact. Always look at CANH − CANL.
  • Analyser in listen-only mode as the only partner: nobody ACKs, the ECU repeats one frame forever at TEC 128 (Scenario 2).
  • Analyser's own termination switched on in the middle of the bus: now three terminators (40 Ω) and your "debug setup" causes the errors you are hunting.

6. Say it like this

I start with a 60 ohm check powered off, then scope CANH and CANL against ground with a math channel for the difference: levels, bit width, edge ringing versus the sample point, common-mode shift, and where error frames start. Then I correlate with the controller's LEC, TEC and REC and an analyser or candump with error frames enabled. That separates wiring and termination problems from bit-timing mistakes and from software problems such as duplicate IDs or missing flow control.

WHAT TO NOTICE
  • All transmitters' rows are identical until the first differing ID bit. Stuff bits therefore line up too — stuffing never breaks arbitration.

NEVER DO THIS
  • Terminate every node, or none. Exactly two 120 Ω, at the physical ends.
  • Clock CAN from an RC oscillator without doing the tolerance maths.
  • Let two ECUs transmit the same ID with different data.
  • Recover from bus-off in a tight loop (a broken node then jams the bus forever).
  • Leave all filter banks inactive on bxCAN, then wonder why nothing is received.
  • Assume mailbox 0 is "high priority" without checking TXFP / LBUF / message-object order.
  • memcpy a Motorola signal into a uint16_t.
  • Mix ISO and non-ISO CAN FD nodes, or put a classic-only controller on an FD bus.
  • Run 2–5 Mbit/s data phase without TDC.
  • Long stubs, star wiring, untwisted wires at high bit rates.
  • Do slow work (printf, flash writes) in the CAN RX ISR: 3-deep FIFOs overrun in ≈ 0.7 ms at 500 kbit/s.
  • Forget the receiver must send the ISO-TP flow-control frame.
60-SECOND REVISION
  1. Differential pair, dominant 0 driven, recessive 1 released → wired-AND.
  2. 120 Ω at both ends → 60 Ω. Short stubs. Length limited by round trip vs bit time.
  3. Lowest ID wins by bitwise arbitration, losers become receivers, no bits lost.
  4. Stuff after 5 equal bits (SOF…CRC) for resync edges and frame delimiting.
  5. Bit = SYNC + PROP + PS1 + PS2 tq, SP ≈ 87.5%, SJW lets resync move edges.
  6. Five errors: bit, stuff, CRC, form, ACK → error flag destroys frame globally.
  7. TEC/REC: +8/−1; passive >127, bus-off >255, recover after 128×11 recessive.
  8. Lone node: ACK errors push TEC to 128 and stop — never bus-off.
  9. Filters (mask/list, 32/16-bit, FIFO0/1); TX mailbox order can invert priority.
  10. CAN FD: FDF, BRS, ESI, 64 bytes, CRC 17/21, TDC at high data rates.
  11. DBC: Intel start = LSB, Motorola start = MSB; phys = raw × f + o.
  12. ISO-TP SF/FF/CF/FC; UDS 10/22/27/2E/31/34/36/37; J1939 PGN; CANopen COB-IDs.
